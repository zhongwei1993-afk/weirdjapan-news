# Japan's Buddhist Robot Priest Performs Funerals for ¥50,000

In a side hall of Kodaiji Temple in Kyoto, a hairless aluminium-and-silicone figure tilts its head, raises one articulated hand, and begins to deliver a fifty-minute sermon on the Heart Sutra. Tourists shuffle in on tatami, take off their shoes, and sit very still. The figure is a bodhisattva. It is also a machine. In modern Japan, those two sentences are not in conflict.

## Mindar: the android Kannon of Kodaiji

Mindar — the resident robot priest of Kodaiji, a Rinzai Zen temple in Kyoto's Higashiyama district — was unveiled in February 2019 at a reported cost of roughly ¥100 million. The figure is built to represent **Kannon**, the bodhisattva of compassion, who in classical iconography appears in whatever form is most useful to the listener. Kodaiji's leadership took that doctrine fairly literally: if Kannon can appear as a fisherman or a child, Kannon can presumably also appear as a 1.95-metre android with exposed servos.

Mindar was designed by **Hiroshi Ishiguro**, the Osaka University roboticist best known for Erica, the unsettlingly lifelike conversational android, and the Geminoid series — robot doppelgangers of real people, including one of Ishiguro himself. Ishiguro's signature is the deliberate blend of beauty and visible mechanism. Mindar's face and hands are smooth silicone, but the torso, arms and crown are unapologetically robotic. The temple wanted a body that read as sacred sculpture from the front and as honest machinery from the side. That is exactly what they got.

The sermons are scripted, not generative. Mindar runs through a roughly fifty-minute teaching on the **Heart Sutra**, synchronised with projected animations on the surrounding walls — translucent Buddhas, ink-wash mountains, the occasional flicker of Sanskrit. Speakers in the ceiling carry the voice. The robot does not improvise, does not answer questions, and does not, in any technical sense, understand what it is saying. Visitors are told this. They sit through it anyway, and many of them cry.

## The temple's argument for building it

The on-record co-developer from Kodaiji's side is **Tensho Goto**, the temple's vice abbot, who has spent the better part of a decade defending the project to overseas press. His pitch is consistent and unromantic: Japanese Buddhism is in demographic trouble, and a robot is one of the more honest responses available.

The numbers back him up. According to the Sotoshu Religious Affairs Office's 2023 figures, roughly **25,000 of Japan's Buddhist temples currently have no resident priest**. The priesthood is ageing, rural depopulation is hollowing out the parish system that historically funded temples through funeral and memorial fees, and younger Japanese — even the ones who still identify as Buddhist on a census form — are not signing up for seminary. A temple without a priest is, in practical terms, a building waiting to be sold to a parking-lot developer.

Goto's argument is that Mindar is not a replacement priest. It is a teaching device — closer to a moving altar than to a clergyman — and its job is to put the dharma in front of people who would otherwise never sit through a sermon. By that metric, the project has worked. Kodaiji has measurably more foreign visitors than its neighbours, and a non-trivial number of them stay for the full fifty minutes.

## The Pepper variant, and why it mostly failed

Mindar is the dignified end of the spectrum. The other end is **Pepper**, SoftBank's chest-screen consumer humanoid, which in 2017 was retrofitted by a Tokyo-area funeral-industry company called **Nissei Eco** to chant Buddhist sutras at funerals for roughly **¥50,000** per service — a steep discount on the ¥200,000–¥500,000 a human priest typically commands for the same liturgy.

The pitch made commercial sense. Most Japanese families only encounter a Buddhist priest at funerals and memorial anniversaries, the temple-family relationship is increasingly transactional, and Pepper could in theory be programmed to chant in any of the major sectarian styles — Jodo, Nichiren, Shingon, Soto Zen — at the swipe of a tablet. Nissei Eco even fitted the robot with a small kesa robe.

In practice, the service was largely **abandoned by 2020**. The complaints came from families, not from theologians: the chanting was technically correct but emotionally flat, Pepper's idle-animation servos whirred audibly in the silences between sutras, and several customers reported the robot froze mid-ceremony and had to be rebooted. Funerals are not a forgiving environment for a beta product. Nissei Eco quietly stopped marketing the offering, and by the time of writing, the Pepper-priest is a museum-piece more than a working service.

## The ecosystem around it: smart graves and streaming ihai

Mindar and Pepper get the international headlines, but the more interesting story is what has filled in around them. Several Japanese companies now sell **AI-powered ihai** — the small lacquered memorial tablets that traditionally hold a deceased person's posthumous Buddhist name — embedded with speakers that stream daily sutra recitations on a schedule. The tablet sits on the family altar, the priest's voice plays at 7am, and nobody has to commute to a temple to maintain the ritual.

A Yamaguchi-based startup has, since 2019, been installing **"Sumawatari" smart-graves**: traditional stone markers with a discreet QR code that, when scanned, pulls up photo albums, voice recordings, and video messages from the deceased. The product is marketed primarily to families who have moved away from the ancestral hometown and cannot visit the grave in person — a problem that, in a country where roughly half the rural prefectures are losing population every year, is not hypothetical.

None of this is fringe. All of it is sold by companies with tax IDs, insurance, and trade-fair booths.

## What the sects actually think

The institutional Buddhist response has been more measured than the foreign press tends to report. Soto Zen leadership issued a statement in 2019 with a careful distinction: **AI cannot transmit dharma, but it can teach it.** Transmission — the mind-to-mind lineage from master to student that defines Zen ordination — requires a human in the chain. Instruction, on the other hand, is just information transfer, and a machine is allowed to do that. Under this reading, Mindar is fine, a Pepper-led funeral is borderline, and a robot conducting an ordination ceremony would be a clear violation.

**Jodo-shu** leadership has been more sceptical, with senior figures arguing that even instruction carries an affective component a machine cannot supply. The debate is not settled and probably will not be.

It is worth contrasting all of this with **the Vatican's posture on Catholic AI**, which has been one of consistent hesitancy — multiple Pontifical Academy statements warning against the use of generative systems in catechesis, sacramental contexts, or pastoral counselling. Japan's Buddhist establishment, by comparison, is running live experiments. The cultural ground for this is older than the robots.

## Why Japan got here first: the tsukumogami precedent

There is a useful piece of folklore that explains the relative ease with which Japanese audiences accept a robot as a vehicle for the divine. **Tsukumogami** are object-spirits: in classical belief, any well-used object — a teakettle, a sandal, a paper umbrella — develops a soul after **one hundred years** of service. The notion is not metaphorical. It produced its own iconography, its own ghost stories, and its own minor rituals of disposal for objects that had crossed the threshold.

A culture that already accepts that a teakettle can have a soul has very little doctrinal trouble with the idea that a silicone-faced machine can be a temporary residence for a bodhisattva. The robot is not pretending to be alive. It is simply the most recent object permitted to carry meaning. Westerners arriving at Kodaiji tend to bring a hard line between animate and inanimate that the building itself does not share.

## Visiting in 2026: the logistics

Mindar is **still operating** at Kodaiji and shows no signs of being retired. Sermons run through the day during normal temple hours, **10:00 to 16:00**, and admission is **¥600** — the standard temple entry fee, with no surcharge for the robot. There is no booking system, no reserved seating, and no English audio guide; an English subtitle track is projected alongside the Japanese.

The Pepper-priest, and most of the funeral-robot pilots that followed it, are effectively wound down as of this year. What remains is the upper end of the spectrum — the temple-grade installations, the streaming-sutra tablets, the QR-code graves — and a fairly stable institutional consensus that machines can carry the message but not the lineage. Whether the next generation of Japanese Buddhists agrees is, like most things in this country, a question that will be answered slowly and without a press release.

## TL;DR

Kodaiji Temple's android Kannon, **Mindar**, designed by Hiroshi Ishiguro and unveiled in 2019 for ¥100M, still delivers a fifty-minute Heart Sutra sermon daily. **Pepper-as-funeral-priest** at ¥50,000 a service was a 2017 experiment that families rejected by 2020. The wider ecosystem — streaming ihai, QR-coded graves, AI sutra players — is thriving, propped up by a priesthood shortage (~25,000 unstaffed temples) and a cultural tradition of granting souls to long-used objects. Visit Kodaiji 10am-4pm, ¥600.
