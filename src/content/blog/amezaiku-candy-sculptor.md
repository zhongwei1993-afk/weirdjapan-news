# Japanese Candy Sculptors Make Animals From Hot Sugar in 3 Minutes

A customer points at a photo of a koi carp. The artisan nods, reaches into a pot of glowing syrup the color of weak tea, and pulls out a fist-sized blob that is, technically, hot enough to remove a fingerprint. Three minutes later there is a fish in his palm — scales, tail, the faint scorched eye done with a blowtorch — and the customer is handed a stick with a translucent animal balanced on top.

This is amezaiku (飴細工), Japanese candy sculpting, and it has been a street craft in this country for roughly three hundred years. It is also, depending on which artisan you ask, somewhere between a dying art and a quietly successful Instagram business.

## The 80-Degree Dare

The medium is **mizuame** — a thick, slightly amber rice-malt syrup that Japanese cooks have been making since at least the Nara period. Heated, it becomes a pliable, taffy-like mass. Cooled, it sets into a glassy, fragile sugar sculpture that can hold detail down to the scales on a fish or the wings on a beetle.

Working temperature sits around 80°C. That is hot enough to scald skin instantly, and amezaiku artisans famously work with bare hands. They are not immune; they are calloused. Apprentices spend their first weeks with bandaged fingers, learning to read the syrup by feel — too hot to shape, too cool to bend, exactly right for about ninety seconds.

The toolkit is short. A pair of small Japanese scissors (nigirishabasami) for cutting fins, ears, antennae. A blowtorch for scorching detail — eyes, the dark line down a koi's back, the brown tips on a butterfly's wings. Edible food coloring for the rest. The genuinely dangerous part is the 250°C zone the syrup passes through on its way down from the pot; brushing the wrong patch of syrup at that temperature is a hospital visit. Most working artisans have at least one scar they will show you if asked politely.

## From Heian Temples to Edo Street Performance

The craft did not start as street art. It arrived from China sometime in the 8th century, during the Heian period, as part of the broader package of Buddhist material culture flowing into Japan: incense, sugar, sutras, the idea of offering decorative sweets to temples. For its first several hundred years amezaiku was a religious object, made by specialists for ceremonies and shrine festivals.

The Edo period (roughly the 1700s) is where it became what we now recognize. Sugar became cheaper, cities got bigger, and a class of street performers began working amezaiku at festivals and on busy temple approaches. They worked fast because they had to: an Edo crowd would not stand around for forty minutes. The three-to-five-minute animal — pull, twist, cut, paint, hand it over — was a commercial constraint that hardened into an aesthetic.

By the late 19th century amezaiku stalls were a normal part of any matsuri (festival). By the 1980s they had mostly vanished, killed off by health regulations about food handling, the decline of street vending generally, and the simple fact that nobody was apprenticing anymore. For a while you could go to a major Tokyo festival and not see a single ame stall.

## Takahiro Yoshihara and the Ameshin Revival

The single name responsible for amezaiku not quietly disappearing in the 2000s is **Takahiro Yoshihara**, who opened a shop called **Ameshin** near Sensoji temple in Asakusa in 2008. Yoshihara had trained in the dying tail end of the apprentice system and decided, against the advice of essentially everyone, to open a permanent storefront for a craft that traditionally lived on a folding table at festivals.

The shop worked. It worked partly because Asakusa is a tourist neighborhood that rewards exactly this kind of visible, theatrical craft; partly because Yoshihara is genuinely one of the best technical amezaiku artists alive; and partly because his Instagram account — currently over 200,000 followers — turned the three-minute koi into a globally watched performance.

He then did the thing that matters most: he opened a school. Ameshin's apprentice program has graduated roughly 40 artisans, who are now working at shops, hotels, weddings, and festivals across Japan. Forty is not a large number. It is, however, considerably more than zero, which is where the trend line was pointing in 2007.

Ameshin itself now operates three Tokyo locations: the original **Asakusa Honten**, a branch inside **Tokyo Skytree Town**, and **Ameshin Sennichiya** near Ueno. Yoshihara has taught workshops abroad — Brooklyn in 2019, various European art fairs — which is a sentence that would have been incomprehensible in 1995.

## Subjects, Prices, and How a Foreigner Actually Books One

The standard repertoire is animals: koi carp, dragons, frogs, bunnies, butterflies, the occasional cat. A basic piece — bunny, frog, butterfly — runs around 15 minutes and ¥1,500 to ¥3,000. A koi or dragon with full detailing takes 30 minutes and lands between ¥3,000 and ¥5,000. Custom commissions go higher; an elaborate dragon on a stand can be ¥10,000 or more, but those are usually wedding centerpieces and corporate gifts, not walk-in trade.

The walk-in process at Asakusa Honten is mercifully simple. Show up, point at the laminated menu of available animals, pay at the counter, and wait roughly twenty minutes. The artisan works at a low table in the front window, which means you can — and are expected to — watch the entire process. This is part of the product. Amezaiku without an audience is just a confectioner making a fish; the customer-as-witness is the performance art component the Edo street vendors built into the craft three hundred years ago.

Tourists sometimes assume the sculptures are display-only. They are not. They are candy, in the strict sense — you can eat the koi. Most people don't, because they paid ¥4,000 for it, and amezaiku is genuinely tasty in the way unflavored toffee is tasty, which is to say only briefly interesting. The display dome included with most pieces exists because the sugar absorbs moisture from the air, slumps, clouds, and eventually collapses within a few weeks. The art is, by design, temporary.

## The Wider Street-Craft Category

Amezaiku sits inside a small, loose category of Japanese crafts whose entire appeal is watching a skilled person make something fast in front of you. The most internationally famous is probably **[plastic food sampuru](/blog/plastic-food-craft)**, the hyperreal restaurant window models. **[Kintsugi](/blog/kintsugi-gold-pottery)**, the gold-seam pottery repair, gets the philosophical magazine articles. **Kanzashi** hairpin flower-folding still happens at a handful of Kyoto shops. Glass crafts like **edo-kiriko** cut-glass and calligraphy specialties like **nuke-no-fude** (a single-stroke style) round out the list.

What they share is a small physical product, a long technical apprenticeship, and a performance-of-skill element that distinguishes them from craftwork done quietly in a back room. They are, for lack of a better word, watchable. Amezaiku is among the most watchable of all of them, because the medium is visibly trying to harden faster than the artisan can work.

## Heritage Status, Mt Fuji Season, and Candy as Ephemeral Art

In 2018 Taito Ward — the Tokyo ward containing Asakusa and Ueno — designated amezaiku an **intangible cultural heritage** at the municipal level. The designation is not national, and it does not come with funding, but it does mean that the craft is now formally recognized as something worth keeping alive. As of 2026 the protection remains in place, and Ameshin's apprentice pipeline is the practical mechanism through which it gets honored.

Spring is the busiest commercial season. Cherry-blossom weeks bring a surge of commissions for **Mt Fuji shapes** — a stylized white-and-pink mountain on a stick, sometimes with sakura petals embedded in the sugar — which sell as souvenirs to both foreign tourists and domestic visitors taking the train back to Osaka or Fukuoka with something delicate wrapped in a box.

The cultural reading that gets attached to amezaiku in essays and gallery wall texts is usually the one about ephemerality: candy as a metaphor for the Japanese taste for things that decay beautifully and on schedule. Cherry blossoms. Tea ceremony. The whole mono-no-aware tradition of finding the point of an object in its disappearance. This is mostly true. It is also slightly retrofitted; the Edo street vendors were not making koi-shaped sugar to express the impermanence of being. They were making it because children would pay for it and it was cheaper than wood. But both things can be true, and the modern revival has leaned into the ephemerality reading with some success — there is a reason Yoshihara's Instagram works.

## TL;DR

Amezaiku is the 300-year-old Japanese craft of pulling 80°C rice-syrup candy into animal shapes — koi, dragons, butterflies — in three to fifteen minutes, bare hands, scissors, blowtorch. Nearly extinct by the 2000s, it was revived by Takahiro Yoshihara's Ameshin shop in Asakusa (2008), whose apprentice program has produced about 40 working artisans. Walk-in commissions in Tokyo run ¥1,500 to ¥5,000, take twenty minutes, and you watch. Taito Ward designated it intangible cultural heritage in 2018. The candy is edible, but melts within weeks. That is part of the point.
