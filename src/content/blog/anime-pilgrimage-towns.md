# Anime Fans Are Saving Rural Japan by Visiting Their Favorite Town

A 22-year-old in a hoodie steps off the train at Washinomiya Station in rural Saitama. He walks twelve minutes to a Shinto shrine that has been standing, in some form, since the 8th century. He buys a wooden prayer plaque, draws an anime girl on it with a felt pen, hangs it next to a thousand others doing the same thing, and bows respectfully on his way out. The priests, who once found this disturbing, now order the plaques in bulk.

This is **seichi junrei** — 聖地巡礼, literally "holy site pilgrimage." It used to refer to circuits like the 88 temples of Shikoku. Now it mostly means going to the convenience store where a cartoon high schooler bought a melon bread.

## The Moment It Became a Thing: Washinomiya, 2007

The accepted origin story is *Lucky Star*. The 2007 slice-of-life series featured four schoolgirls whose hometown was modeled on Washinomiya, a quiet commuter town an hour north of Tokyo. The shrine in the opening credits is real. Within months of the anime airing, the shrine's New Year visitor count jumped from around 90,000 to over 300,000. The Anime Tourism Association now estimates Washinomiya pulls roughly **80,000 anime-specific visitors a year**, more than a decade after the show ended.

The chief priest's first reaction was, by his own admission, deep discomfort. The shrine is a working religious site, not a photo op. But the visitors were polite, they bought omamori, they cleaned up after themselves, and they kept coming back. The shrine eventually printed limited-edition ema plaques featuring the *Lucky Star* characters and licensed an annual festival mikoshi carried by cosplayers. It is now, financially, one of the more secure mid-sized shrines in Saitama. This is the template every site below has followed.

## The Map: Where Otaku Go to Pray

The Anime Tourism Association formed in 2016 and publishes an annual **"88 Holy Sites of Anime" map**, deliberately echoing the Shikoku temple circuit. Fans collect them like a checklist. A partial geography of the obsession:

- **Hida Furukawa, Gifu** — Mitsuha's town in *Your Name* (2016). The local tourism office credits the film with **200,000+ additional annual visitors** and an estimated ¥1 billion-plus yearly economic boost, sustained nearly a decade after release.
- **Hikone & Toyosato, Shiga** — Hikone Shrine and the old wooden Toyosato Elementary School, both standing in for the *K-On!* universe. Toyosato keeps a club room reconstructed exactly as drawn, with the tea set out.
- **Shimonita, Gunma** — A mountain town that owes its weekend tourism to *Yowamushi Pedal*, a cycling anime in which characters grind up its actual passes.
- **Ohama Park and Kameido, Tokyo** — The neighborhoods *Cardcaptor Sakura* fans walk through with frame-accurate screenshots on their phones.
- **Onomichi, Hiroshima** — The hilltown of *A Silent Voice* (2016), already a literary pilgrimage site, now double-booked with anime tourists.
- **Suzu, Ishikawa** — Setting of *Suzu and the Whale*. A peninsula town that, prior to the film, mostly appeared in news coverage of depopulation.
- **Daisen, Akita** — The quiet northern setting of *Wandering Son*.
- **Chichibu and Onishi, Saitama** — *Anohana* territory. The town hangs character ema at the local shrine and runs an annual lantern festival timed to the series finale.

Each entry follows the same arc: anime airs, fans arrive with screenshots, the town's chamber of commerce panics for six months, then prints a stamp rally.

## The Money: Why Mayors Stopped Resisting

For a long time, local governments treated otaku tourism as embarrassing — adult men photographing vending machines is not the brochure image rural Japan grew up wanting. Then they looked at the receipts.

Hida Furukawa's tourism board now lists *Your Name* as a permanent line item. The Cool Japan strategy at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs explicitly cites anime tourism as soft-power infrastructure. **Anime Japan**, the industry trade show held at Tokyo Big Sight every March, draws around **150,000 attendees** and functions as a kind of pilgrimage trade fair, with regional tourism boards renting booths next to the studios.

At individual sites the merchandise economics are surprisingly granular. A limited **goshuin** stamp — the calligraphy seal collectors hunt — can cost ¥500 to ¥1,000 and exists in editions that turn over every few months. A character-printed omamori amulet runs around ¥500. An ema plaque is ¥800 to ¥1,500, and gets hung in a wooden rack that has, in some shrines, completely displaced the racks for conventional wishes about exam results.

The Studio Ghibli Park, which opened in Aichi in 2022, is the corporate-scale version of the same instinct: build the place fans were already trying to visit. Universal Studios Japan's **Super Nintendo World** in Osaka runs on adjacent logic — not a pilgrimage site exactly, but the same audience, the same willingness to fly halfway around the world for a specific staircase.

## The Tension: When the Town Doesn't Want You Anymore

The flattering version of this story ends with rural revival. The actual version includes residents who would like their street back.

The clearest example is the **Kamakurakokomae railway crossing** featured in *Slam Dunk* — for years a manageable trickle of tourists, then, after the 2022 film, an unmanageable flood. Buses can't turn. Residents can't cross their own street. Local authorities have introduced barriers and crowd marshals. The pattern has repeated at the Itsukushima-style scenic bridge in Yamanashi that appeared in a *Slam Dunk* sequence, where the local government now requires reservations and timed entry to keep the surrounding village functional.

Even Washinomiya, the founding success story, has had years where the shrine quietly asked fan groups to space out their visits. Hida Furukawa posts multilingual signs reminding visitors that the houses in the film are private homes and the people inside them are not Mitsuha. The Anime Tourism Association's literature now spends as much space on manners as on maps.

It is, in the WeirdJapan sense, a deeply Japanese problem: a system that works beautifully until exactly one too many people find out about it.

## The Cultural Reading

Strip the cosplay off and what's left is recognizably old. Japan has a thousand-year tradition of literary and religious pilgrimage — to Basho's stops, to Saigyo's cherry trees, to the temples a monk happened to die at. Walking to a place because a story happened there is not a new instinct. The story being a 2007 anime about four girls eating choco cornets is, in the grand sweep of things, a minor variation.

What's actually new is the speed. A novel takes a generation to produce pilgrims. A twelve-episode anime takes about six months. By the time the local tourism office has updated the signage, the next show is already airing and there is a new village to find on Google Maps.

## How to Plan a Five-Day Anime Pilgrimage

A workable circuit, west from Tokyo:

- **Day 1 — Tokyo.** Akihabara, Nakano Broadway, an evening at Anime Japan if your dates line up. Use the **Anime Pilgrimage Map** app published by the Anime Tourism Association to flag locations near your hotel; many are unmarked otherwise.
- **Day 2 — Hida Furukawa, Gifu.** Shinkansen to Nagoya, then the JR Hida limited express. The *Your Name* sites are walkable from the station. Stay in a ryokan; the town is small.
- **Day 3 — Suzu, Ishikawa.** Long travel day across to the Noto Peninsula. The pace drops sharply. This is the leg that reminds you most pilgrimages are mostly transit.
- **Day 4 — Onomichi, Hiroshima.** Down the Sanyo line. *A Silent Voice* bridges and stairs, plus the older Ozu film locations layered on top.
- **Day 5 — Osaka or Aichi.** Either Super Nintendo World at USJ or the Ghibli Park outside Nagoya, depending on which fandom you came for.

A **JR Pass** mostly pays for itself on this itinerary; we've broken down the math separately in [is the JR Pass worth it in 2026](/blog/jr-pass-2026-worth-it). For a lighter version, several of these can be done as [Tokyo day trips on a budget](/blog/tokyo-day-trips-budget).

## TL;DR

Seichi junrei is the Japanese practice of treating anime filming locations as pilgrimage sites — a 2007 *Lucky Star* visit to a Saitama shrine kicked off a national movement that now sustains an 88-spot official map, an annual ¥1 billion+ boost to towns like Hida Furukawa, character-printed prayer plaques at working shrines, and, increasingly, reservation systems for railway crossings that became too famous to function. It is genuinely saving some dying villages. It is also, very quietly, annoying the neighbors.
