# Japan's Most Beloved Bread Has Sweet Bean Paste Inside

Portuguese missionaries brought bread to Japan in 1543. For the next three hundred years, almost nobody ate it. Bread was foreign, vaguely Christian, and associated with the wrong sort of people. Then in 1874, an unemployed samurai in Ginza filled a soft yeasted bun with sweet azuki paste, served it to the Emperor at a cherry-blossom party, and reset the entire trajectory of Japanese baking. The bun is called **あんパン (anpan)**. It now sells roughly fifty million units a year through one parent company alone, and a children's anime character based on it generates around one billion yen in annual licensing revenue. Most foreigners walk past it at the konbini for ¥120 without realizing they are looking at the founding object of modern Japanese bread.

## The Samurai Who Invented Japanese Bread

The man responsible was **Yasubei Kimura (木村安兵衛)**. He had been a low-ranking retainer in the Tokugawa system, which collapsed under him during the Meiji Restoration. Like thousands of other suddenly-unemployed samurai, he needed a second act. In 1869 he and his son opened a small bakery in Tokyo called **Kimuraya Sōhonten (木村屋總本店)**. It still exists. The flagship is the same address on Ginza 4-chome it has occupied since 1874, directly across from the Wako clock tower.

The problem Kimura faced in his first five years was straightforward: Japanese people did not want Western bread. The leavening agent was hops or brewer's yeast, the loaves were dense, and the entire object smelled faintly of a religion the government had spent two centuries trying to suppress. Bread was eaten by foreigners, by sailors, and by the small Christian remnant in Nagasaki. It was not Tuesday-morning food.

Kimura's fix was to stop trying to make Western bread. In 1874 he substituted **sakadane (酒種)** — a sourdough starter cultured from rice koji, the same fermentation used for sake — for the European yeast. The result was sweeter, softer, and smelled like a Japanese kitchen rather than a Portuguese galleon. Then he filled it with **tsubuan (粒あん)**, the chunky red bean paste already familiar from wagashi confectionery, and topped it with a salt-cured cherry blossom pressed into the center. Anpan was, structurally, a Western bun wrapped around a Japanese sweet. It read as bread to the curious and as dessert to the cautious.

## The Cherry Blossom That Legitimized Bread

The product needed a marketing event. It got the best one available.

In April 1875, Kimura's son Eisaburo presented anpan to **Emperor Meiji and Empress Shōken** during a **hanami (花見)** outing at the Mito Tokugawa villa in Sumida. The sakura-blossom decoration in the center was not decoration; it was a deliberate gesture toward a seasonal flower the imperial household was contractually obligated to admire. The Emperor approved. The Empress reportedly asked for the bun to be made a regular delivery to the palace.

That single endorsement did what a decade of import substitution could not. Bread stopped being a Christian curiosity and became, by imperial implication, **Japanese**. The story is now taught in elementary school food-history units. Kimuraya still presses a sakura blossom into the center of its classic anpan, and the date — **April 4th** — is still observed within the industry as **Anpan Day**.

The wider pattern is worth noting. Most of what is now called **yōshoku (洋食)** — the genre of Western-derived Japanese comfort food that includes omurice, tonkatsu, and curry rice — was assembled in roughly the same forty-year window, by the same kind of people, under the same pressure to make the unfamiliar palatable. Anpan was the first successful case. The bread that followed it, including melon-pan and kare-pan and the various ham-and-mayo rolls now stacked in every konbini, descends from the same lineage: a Western form, a Japanese filling, and a quiet refusal to make either side dominant.

## The Recipe, Examined

The Kimuraya method has been only mildly updated in 150 years. The dough is still leavened with **sakadane** koji starter, which gives it a faintly sweet, slightly tangy profile and a much finer crumb than commercial yeast produces. It proves slowly, sometimes overnight. The skin browns to a deep mahogany because of the high sugar content in the dough itself, not because of an egg wash, and the contrast between the dark exterior and the pale interior is part of how good anpan is judged at a glance.

The filling is the more visible variable. There are two standard options:

- **Tsubuan (粒あん)** — chunky paste in which the azuki beans retain their shape and skin. Earthier, less sweet, sold under a **sesame-seeded crown** at most reputable bakeries.
- **Koshian (こしあん)** — smooth paste, beans pushed through a sieve, skins discarded. Cleaner, sweeter, usually marked with a **poppy seed** or no topping at all.

A good anpan is judged on three things. First, the bean-to-dough ratio: anything under fifty-five percent filling by weight is considered stingy, and the best examples push past sixty. Second, the sweetness of the skin: it should be noticeably less sweet than the paste, so the bun reads as bread first and dessert second. Third, the structural integrity of the seam on the underside, which separates the work of a skilled baker from the work of a machine.

## The Konbini Economy of a National Snack

Anpan has now been industrialized to a degree that would have astonished its inventor.

**Yamazaki Baking (山崎製パン)**, the parent company of the Yamazaki Daily konbini-aisle brand, ships **more than fifty million anpan a year** through 7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart, and the supermarket chains. The standard konbini unit costs roughly **¥120**, contains a respectable but unromantic koshian, and is engineered for a four-day shelf life. It is the workhorse version: cheap, reliable, eaten on trains, packed into school lunches, and almost never photographed.

Above that floor sits an entire pyramid of specialist bakeries. **Tokyo Anpan** in Marunouchi, **Anpanya** in Kichijōji, and the bean-paste-focused **Anplus** in Asakusa each treat the bun as a vehicle for a single varietal of azuki, often from a named producer in Hokkaidō. **Tokyo Station's Centre Tomoda** sells a salted-butter anpan that sells out by mid-morning. Prices at this tier run **¥250–¥450** per piece, and the queue is part of the product.

And then, presiding over all of it, is **Anpanman (アンパンマン)** — the children's anime character invented in 1973 by the manga artist **Yanase Takashi (やなせたかし)**. Anpanman is, literally, a sentient anpan who fights crime by allowing hungry children to eat pieces of his own head. The franchise has run continuously on TV Asahi since 1988, holds the Guinness record for most characters in an animated series, and generates roughly **one billion yen per year in licensing alone** for the character. Anpan is the only Japanese pastry with its own superhero.

## Where to Eat It, and What to Look For

For visitors, the rough hierarchy is:

- **Kimuraya Ginza Honten (銀座木村家)** — the original 1869 location. The classic sakura anpan is around **¥220**. Buy two and a koshian one for comparison. The basement counter is faster than the ground floor.
- **Centre Tomoda, Tokyo Station** — inside the Marunouchi side, near the Daimaru basement. Salted-butter anpan, gone by lunch.
- **Anplus (Asakusa)** — single-origin Hokkaidō tokachi-azuki, queued out the door on weekends.
- **Any konbini, any prefecture, any hour** — the ¥120 control sample. Worth eating at least once for calibration.

Regional variations are real but underpublicized. Hokkaidō bakeries lean heavily on **tokachi-azuki (十勝小豆)**, the prestige bean varietal grown around Obihiro, which produces a darker and more mineral paste. Okinawa, working with what it has, fills its anpan with **beni-imo (紅芋)** purple sweet potato instead of azuki — same form, completely different color, sold mostly to tourists at Naha airport. Kyoto's old-line wagashi houses, when they bother with bread at all, push the bun toward the dessert end and serve it with matcha.

The identification rules carry across all of them. Skin not too sweet. Bean ratio over fifty-five percent. A neat **sesame-seed crown** if it is tsubuan, a clean unmarked dome if it is koshian, and a faint sourdough tang on the breath if the baker is still using sakadane. Everything else is decoration.

## TL;DR

Anpan is a soft sweet bun filled with azuki bean paste, invented in 1874 by the ex-samurai Yasubei Kimura at Kimuraya Sōhonten in Ginza. After Emperor Meiji ate one at a cherry-blossom viewing in 1875, bread — until then treated as foreign and faintly Christian — was relicensed as Japanese food. A century and a half later, anpan is sold in every konbini for ¥120, shipped fifty million units a year by Yamazaki, fronted by an anime superhero who feeds children his own face, and still made by the original Ginza bakery to roughly the original recipe.
