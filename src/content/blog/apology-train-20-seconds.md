# Why a Japanese Train Apologized for Leaving 20 Seconds Early

On a Tuesday morning in November 2018, a commuter train on the Tsukuba Express line north of Tokyo pulled out of Minami-Nagareyama station at 9:44:20 instead of 9:44:40. Nobody complained. Nobody missed it — there were no passengers waiting on the platform. The operator issued an unprompted public apology anyway, posted it to their website, and the story bounced around international news desks for the better part of a week. The BBC translated it. American morning shows played it for laughs. In Japan, it was filed away as a slightly embarrassing Tuesday.

This is the only country on Earth where being twenty seconds early is a press release.

## The 20-Second Incident, and Its Older Cousin

The 2018 Tsukuba Express case is the one everybody knows, mostly because someone in the foreign press finally translated the statement and the phrase "we sincerely apologize for the inconvenience" applied to a third of a minute hit the internet at the right angle. But it wasn't the first, and it wasn't the most dramatic.

A year earlier, in November 2017, JR West issued an almost identical apology when a Sanyo Shinkansen left Hakata 25 seconds ahead of schedule. The company's statement noted that the conductor had "failed to confirm the departure time on his watch." Same template, same bow, same week-of-international-attention. The 2018 Tsukuba case went more viral only because the Tsukuba Express is a private commuter operator, which made it sound, to outside ears, like even the small lines were doing it.

Departure time in Japan is not a target. It is a number. Trains leave on the second printed on the timetable, and if they don't, somebody has to explain in writing why.

## The Certificate That Excuses You From Work

The infrastructure that makes 20-second apologies possible runs in the other direction too. If a Japanese train is more than five minutes late, the operator automatically issues a **chien shoumeisho** (遅延証明書) — a delay certificate. Station staff hand them out in paper form at the gates, and every major operator now publishes a downloadable PDF version on their website, time-stamped to the minute, valid for whichever morning you needed it.

You give it to your boss. Your boss accepts it without comment. It is not a favor; it is a document. The Japanese workplace runs on the assumption that lateness is the train company's fault, never yours, and the train company has agreed to issue the paperwork that proves it. JR East alone hands out millions of these a year. Foreign tourists discover them by accident — caught by a Yamanote line stoppage, handed a slip they didn't ask for, and only later realizing they could have used it to skip a meeting back home.

Nobody else does this. In London, your delayed Thameslink is your problem. In Japan, it is a clerical matter, and there is a form.

## How On-Time "On-Time" Actually Is

The numbers are the thing that breaks Western readers. According to JR East's 2024 operating data, roughly 90% of Shinkansen arrivals land within one minute of the scheduled time. Not five minutes. Not "on time-ish." One minute.

Central Japan Railway's published figure for the Tokaido Shinkansen, for the entirety of 2022, was an average delay per train of 0.5 minutes — and that figure includes every weather suspension, every earthquake stoppage, every wild boar on the tracks near Mishima. The average. For the year. Thirty seconds.

For comparison:

- **UK National Rail**: about 97% of services arrive within five minutes of schedule, per the Office of Rail and Road's most recent published period.
- **Amtrak**: roughly 75% of intercity trains arrive within ten minutes.
- **Tokaido Shinkansen**: 0.5 minutes, averaged, including the year a typhoon shut down the line.

A British "on time" and a Japanese "on time" are not the same word. They aren't even the same unit of measurement.

## The Engineering Underneath the Politeness

None of this is achieved by good intentions. It is achieved by an enormous stack of automation, training, and quietly enforced incentives.

Shinkansen trains run under **ATC** (Automatic Train Control), which continuously adjusts speed against the schedule rather than against a maximum. If a train is six seconds behind, ATC nudges it. The driver's job is partly to monitor the system and partly to perform the last hundred meters into the station by hand, because the platform-stopping accuracy expected of human drivers is roughly **three seconds** and within a stopping mark painted on the platform edge. Bonuses and internal performance scores are tied to it. Drivers carry pocket watches calibrated against the network clock and consult them visibly during the run.

Then there is the choreography. At nearly every staffed Shinkansen and major commuter platform, you will see a **shokuin** — a uniformed station attendant — step to the platform edge as the train departs, point with a white-gloved finger down the length of the cars, and salute. The point-and-call ritual (*shisa kanko*) isn't theater; it's a verified safety procedure that reduces human error rates by something like 85% in industrial studies the railways have been quoting since the 1990s. It just happens to look like the train is being formally seen off, which, ceremonially, it is.

The cultural ground under all of this is **meiwaku** — the strong, baseline aversion to causing trouble for other people — scaled up to infrastructure. A late train inconveniences thousands of strangers. The system is designed, top to bottom, to not do that.

## The Dark Side of the Stopwatch

It is worth saying clearly that the obsession has killed people.

On April 25, 2005, a rapid service on the JR West Fukuchiyama Line derailed entering a curve at Amagasaki, killing **107 people** including the driver and injuring more than 500. The 2007 accident investigation report, and the subsequent Aircraft and Railway Accidents Investigation Commission findings, attributed the crash to the driver entering the curve at roughly 116 km/h on a section rated for 70. He was attempting to make up approximately **1 minute 20 seconds** of delay accumulated earlier in the run. JR West's internal *nikkin kyoiku* ("day-shift re-education") culture, in which drivers who fell behind schedule were subjected to humiliating retraining, was identified as a structural contributor.

The Fukuchiyama derailment reshaped Japanese rail safety policy and is the reason ATC and overspeed protection were extended to lines that previously didn't have them. It is also the reason no honest article about Japanese punctuality should pretend the apology train is purely charming. The same system that produces 20-second statements produced, once, a driver who could not afford to be 80 seconds late.

The country knows this. The anniversary is observed every April. Safety culture inside JR West was rebuilt almost from scratch. But the underlying demand — that the train be on time, always — was not removed. It was made survivable.

## What It Looks Like in 2026

The current state of the system is, if anything, more precise than it was in 2018. JR East's English-language app — search "JR East Train Info" in either app store — now publishes real-time delay alerts down to the individual line, in English, with the delay-certificate PDF reachable in two taps. Most operators have followed. Tourists with a Suica or Welcome Suica card can pull a chien shoumeisho on their phone before they reach the ticket gate.

The 20-second-apology genre has, meanwhile, become a small cultural joke inside Japan itself. The cycling manga *Yowamushi Pedal*, which is mostly about high-school road racing but periodically wanders into deadpan commuter humor, has a recurring gag about a character bowing to a departure board. The joke only lands because the readership recognizes the bow.

And the punctuality is load-bearing in a way Western transit isn't. The entire Japanese transit economy — connecting buses scheduled four minutes after the train, *ekiben* lunchbox vendors timing trays to platform stops, courier networks that hand parcels between Shinkansen cars at 90-second station calls — runs on the assumption that the timetable is the ground truth. If trains were "on time within five minutes," none of it would work. The 20-second apology is not an excess. It is the maintenance of a tolerance that everything else has been built around.

## TL;DR

Japanese train operators publicly apologize for delays measured in seconds because their entire commercial and social timetable assumes the printed time is the actual time. JR East Shinkansen lands within 1 minute about 90% of the time. The same precision killed 107 people at Amagasaki in 2005. The country has decided, with eyes open, to keep it.
