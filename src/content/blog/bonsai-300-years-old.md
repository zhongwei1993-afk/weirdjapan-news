# Some Japanese Bonsai Trees Are 300 Years Old. They're Inherited.

A bonsai (盆栽) is not a houseplant. It is, in some cases, a piece of family infrastructure older than the United States — a living asset that has been pruned, wired, repotted, watered, and inherited through eight or ten generations of the same family, and is now insured for more than a Tokyo apartment.

The oldest documented surviving tree in Japan is roughly a thousand years old. It is owned by the Emperor. It is a pine. And nobody, in any meaningful sense, actually owns it — they just happen to be the current person in charge of keeping it alive.

## The Tang Dynasty Import Nobody Returned

The format we now call bonsai started in Tang Dynasty China as penjing (盆景) — miniature landscapes in shallow trays, designed to compress mountains and rivers into something you could put on a desk. Buddhist monks brought the practice to Japan sometime around the 1100s, along with a great deal of other Chinese cultural baggage that the Japanese kept, refined, and never quite gave back.

What Japan added was Zen. Once the practice slipped into the orbit of Zen Buddhism in the Kamakura and Muromachi periods, bonsai stopped being a landscape in a tray and started being a single tree — one trunk, asymmetric, deliberately incomplete, meant to suggest the wind and weather of a place rather than recreate the place itself. Penjing depicted scenery. Bonsai implied it. The difference sounds small until you spend twenty minutes looking at one.

By the Edo period, samurai households kept bonsai the way English country houses kept libraries: as quiet evidence that the owner had time, taste, and the patience to wait for something to grow.

## The Thousand-Year-Old Pine in the Palace

The headline tree of Japanese bonsai is called **Sandai-Shogun-no-Matsu** (三代将軍の松) — "The Third Shogun's Pine." It is a five-needle pine, it is roughly a thousand years old, and it lives at the Tokyo Imperial Palace.

The name comes from Tokugawa Iemitsu, the third Tokugawa shogun, who reportedly took a liking to the tree in the 1600s and had it brought into the shogunal collection. From the shoguns it eventually moved to the Imperial Household, where it has been maintained by court gardeners ever since. The Imperial Palace East Gardens occasionally display bonsai from the collection; the Sandai-Shogun-no-Matsu itself is not regularly on public view, which is consistent with the general principle that the more important a Japanese cultural object is, the harder it is to actually see.

It is officially designated a National Treasure. There is a non-zero argument that it is the oldest continuously cared-for houseplant on Earth.

## The Tree That Survived Hiroshima

The other tree people know by name is the **Yamaki Pine** — a Japanese white pine, roughly 390 years old, currently living at the United States National Arboretum in Washington, D.C.

The Yamaki family had been training the tree in Hiroshima for six generations. On August 6, 1945, it was sitting behind a wall of their nursery, about three kilometers from the hypocenter of the atomic bomb. The wall absorbed the blast. The tree survived. The family kept caring for it. In 1976, as part of the U.S. Bicentennial, Masaru Yamaki donated it — along with no mention of where it had been on August 6, 1945 — to the United States.

The Arboretum only learned about the Hiroshima detail decades later, when two of Masaru's grandsons visited the collection in 2001 to check on the family tree. It is now one of the most visited objects in the museum, and it is free to see, which is a sentence that would have surprised every previous owner.

Other named trees worth knowing about: **Mansei-en's Hiroshige Pine**, a black pine in the roughly six-hundred-year range, kept at the Mansei-en nursery in Omiya; the Higurashi maple at Shunkaen; the various ancient junipers (shimpaku) collected from the cliffs of the Japan Alps and trained over generations into shapes that look, more than anything, like the cliffs they came from.

## Omiya Bonsai Village: The Saitama Suburb Built by an Earthquake

The center of gravity for serious Japanese bonsai is not Tokyo. It is a residential neighborhood in Saitama called **Omiya Bonsai Village** (大宮盆栽村).

It exists because of the 1923 Great Kantō Earthquake. Tokyo's bonsai gardeners, who had been clustered around the Hongo and Dangozaka neighborhoods, were burned out of their nurseries when central Tokyo went up in fire. In 1925 a group of them moved north as a collective, bought farmland in what was then a quiet pine forest, and founded a new village with explicit rules: every household had to grow at least ten bonsai, keep a hedge, and stay out of two-story construction so the morning sun could reach the trees.

Most of the original rules are gone. Several of the nurseries are not. **Mansei-en**, **Toju-en**, **Seiko-en**, **Fuyo-en**, and **Kyuka-en** are still operating in Omiya today, each of them with collections that include trees older than the nursery itself. You can walk between them in a single afternoon. Admission to the nurseries is free; the assumption is that you might, in principle, eventually buy a tree.

Next door is the **Omiya Bonsai Art Museum** (さいたま市大宮盆栽美術館), which opened in 2010 and is, somewhat improbably, the only public bonsai museum in Japan. Admission is ¥310. The rotating display of roughly sixty trees is taken from the museum's own collection plus loans from the village nurseries. Photography is allowed in the outdoor garden and politely forbidden indoors, which is also where the best trees are.

## The Inheritance Economics

The thing nobody tells you about a 300-year-old bonsai is that almost none of its value is in the tree itself. The value is in the eight or ten generations of decisions — every pruning cut, every wire, every repot — that the current caretaker did not have to make, because someone in 1780 already made them.

This produces an asset class that does not really exist elsewhere. A serious old bonsai is, functionally, a piece of ceramic-funded inheritance: passed down inside a family or a nursery, kept alive by an apprentice system, occasionally insured for ¥100,000,000 or more, and almost never sold on the open market. When one does change hands, it is usually quietly, between collectors, at a price nobody publishes.

For everyone else, the modern retail market exists at roughly these tiers:

- **Starter kit** (juvenile juniper, plastic pot, instructions): ¥3,000.
- **Established juniper or maple**, five to fifteen years of training, ready to display: ¥30,000.
- **Museum-quality black pine**, fifty years of training, named lineage: ¥500,000 and up. Often much up.
- **Heritage tree**: not for sale. If it is for sale, it is not heritage.

The pricing implies its own ecosystem, which implies its own crime. In January 2019, thieves climbed into the Iimura Shoju-en nursery in Saitama and walked off with seven trees, including a 400-year-old shimpaku juniper that the owner valued at around ¥10,000,000 on its own. The owner's wife posted instructions online begging the thieves, in detail, to water the tree every day and mist the leaves twice. The trees were not recovered. The general assumption is that they were trafficked overseas.

This is also why **CITES** — the international convention on endangered species trade — covers a lot of bonsai material. Yamadori (collected wild trees) often involve protected species; export requires phytosanitary inspection, a two-year quarantine in many destination countries, and, for some species, paperwork that is genuinely harder to obtain than a work visa. Buying a famous bonsai and flying it home is, in practice, not a thing tourists do.

## The Kokufu-ten and How to Actually See This

Once a year, in February, the **Kokufu Bonsai Exhibition** (国風盆栽展) takes over the Tokyo Metropolitan Art Museum in Ueno. It has run since 1934, it is widely considered the most important bonsai exhibition in the world, and being selected for display is roughly the bonsai equivalent of an Oscar. The trees are rotated mid-show so that no single specimen is left out for more than a few days. Tickets are inexpensive. The crowd is mostly Japanese retirees and a small contingent of extremely serious foreigners with notebooks.

The rest of the year, the practical answer is Omiya. From Tokyo Station, take the **Saikyo Line** to **Saitama-shintoshin**, about thirty minutes. The Bonsai Art Museum is roughly a ten-minute walk from Toro Station on the Utsunomiya Line, one stop further. Plan a half-day. Bring cash for the ¥310 entry. Do not, under any circumstances, touch a tree.

## TL;DR

Bonsai arrived from Tang China around the 1100s, got refined by Zen Buddhism into single-trunk minimalism, and turned into one of the few hobbies on Earth where the object is older than the owner's great-great-grandparents. The thousand-year-old Sandai-Shogun-no-Matsu lives at the Imperial Palace. The Hiroshima-survivor Yamaki Pine lives in Washington. The five great Omiya nurseries — Mansei-en, Toju-en, Seiko-en, Fuyo-en, Kyuka-en — and the ¥310 Omiya Bonsai Art Museum are thirty minutes from Tokyo on the Saikyo Line. Most Japanese homes have a houseplant. A small number of them have, instead, a piece of inheritance.
