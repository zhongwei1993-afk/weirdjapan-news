# Japan Has 11 Islands Where Cats Outnumber Humans. We Visited One.

There are roughly **eleven documented islands in Japan** where the cat population dwarfs the human one. Tourists call them *neko-jima* (猫島) — "cat islands." Locals, the few who remain, just call them home. On most of them, the math is grim and adorable in equal measure: dozens of elderly fishermen, hundreds of free-roaming cats, one ferry a day, and a slow demographic curve heading in only one direction.

This is the story of how those islands came to exist, what they actually look like in 2026, and the quiet ethical mess that bubbles underneath every viral cat video shot on a Japanese seawall.

## What Counts as a "Cat Island"

The unofficial definition is simple: a small, inhabited Japanese island where free-roaming cats outnumber the registered human residents. By that measure, roughly eleven qualify, though the list shifts as villages empty and TNR programs reduce colonies. The headline cases:

- **Tashirojima** (Miyagi Prefecture) — about **50 human residents** and roughly **100 cats** per a 2024 local survey. Most census-style counts cite a ratio closer to 6-to-1 if you include kittens born in spring. The flagship "cat island."
- **Aoshima** (Ehime Prefecture) — about **13 humans** and, until recently, **~150 cats**. The most photographed.
- **Manabeshima** (Okayama Prefecture) — roughly **250 humans, 200 cats**. The most livable, with a guesthouse and a tiny cafe.
- **Iwashijima** (Kagawa Prefecture) — a small Seto Inland Sea fishing community with a stable colony in the low dozens.
- **Genkaijima** (Fukuoka Prefecture) — closer to a working fishing port than a tourist site, with cats clustered around the harbor.

The remaining names — **Ainoshima**, **Sanagishima**, **Muzukijima**, **Okishima**, and a couple of contenders in the Inland Sea — round out the eleven. None have hotels. None have convenience stores. All require a same-day return ferry.

## The Edo-Era Origin Story

The cats did not arrive as pets. They arrived as **labor**.

In the Edo and early Meiji periods, several of these islands ran small-scale silkworm operations alongside fishing. Silkworm cocoons attract mice. Mice, untreated, can wipe out a year's silk yield in weeks. So farmers brought cats — working animals, not companions — to keep the mice off the trays.

When Japan's domestic silk industry collapsed in the **1920s**, undercut by synthetic fibers and global price shocks, the silkworm sheds shut down. The cats, however, did not leave. There was no one to remove them, no one to neuter them, and a steady supply of fish scraps along the docks. They bred. The humans aged. The math began its slow inversion.

By the postwar decades, several islands had quietly become majority-cat. Nobody marketed this. It was simply a fact of village life until the internet found it.

## Tashirojima: The Flagship

If you only visit one cat island, the consensus pick is **Tashirojima**, off the coast of Miyagi Prefecture in northeast Japan. The ferry runs from **Ishinomaki Port**, takes about **40 minutes**, and costs roughly **¥1,500 each way**. Annual visitor numbers hover around **5,000**, a figure capped less by demand than by the modest capacity of the boat.

Tashirojima carries a heavier history than its visitors usually realize. The **2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami** struck this coastline directly. Parts of the island were damaged and the small human community lost infrastructure that has never fully come back. Most of the cats, by various local accounts, survived — they tend to flee uphill at the first tremor, and the island's elevated interior gave them somewhere to go. The resilience of the colony became, over time, part of the island's identity. It is worth treating that history with the seriousness it deserves rather than as a cute footnote.

There is a small **cat shrine** (*Neko-jinja*) in the island's interior, built generations ago after a falling rock accidentally killed a working cat and the fishermen, feeling guilty, gave it a kami. The shrine is now ringed by cat-shaped stone offerings left by visitors. It is the rare tourist site in Japan that feels neither overproduced nor abandoned.

Since **2022**, a community-led initiative called **Tashirojima Reborn** has been quietly rebuilding houses, funding TNR, and trying to attract a handful of younger long-term residents. Progress is real but slow.

## Aoshima: Two Hours, No Vending Machines

**Aoshima** sits off Nagahama on the Shikoku coast. The ferry is **30 minutes**, costs roughly ¥1,400 round-trip, and holds about 30 people. There are **no cars on the island**. There are **no shops**. There is no vending machine, which in Japan is approximately as rare as snow in Naha. Bring your own water. Bring your own food. Bring out your own trash.

You step off the boat and the cats arrive before the humans do — twenty or thirty of them on the seawall, watching with the calm intensity of long-term residents whose daily rhythm now revolves around the ferry timetable. Many have notched ear-tips, the international marker for spayed or neutered animals. They are, by feral-cat standards, in good condition.

The village is two streets long. Most houses are abandoned. The remaining residents — roughly **13 people**, almost all over 75 — are polite but reserved. They are the last fishermen and their wives, and they did not ask to host a tourist economy.

In **2014, a Buzzfeed-attached viral video pulled in around 1.2 million views** and put Aoshima on every "weird Japan" listicle on the planet. Day-tripper numbers spiked. The cat population, fed indiscriminately by visitors, ballooned past 200 by 2017. The cats got fat. Some got sick. In **2018**, the island formally **banned tourist feeding** after a documented weight-gain epidemic and a string of feline diabetes cases.

The intervention worked. Sterilization continued. By **2026**, the colony has settled at around **80 cats**, with most of the reduction simply due to **age** — the post-viral generation reaching the end of natural feral lifespans, with fewer kittens replacing them. Aoshima is, gently, winding down.

## The Welfare Question Nobody Puts on the Postcard

A free-roaming feral colony is not a petting zoo. It is a population of un-owned animals living outdoors with no veterinary baseline, exposed to weather, parasites, territorial fights, and — in cold winters — real risk of starvation. Kitten mortality on un-managed islands is high. Untreated upper-respiratory infections move quickly through dense colonies.

The intervention that actually works is **TNR** — *trap, neuter, return* — and **Japan SPCA** along with affiliated volunteer veterinary groups now run sustained programs on most of the eleven islands. **Aoshima Cat Project** has been the most visible, funding spay/neuter cycles, basic vaccinations, and feeding-station protocols. **Tashirojima Reborn**, mentioned above, folds cat welfare into a broader village-revival effort.

If you visit, the locally-supported ethics are straightforward and worth following:

- Do not bring or distribute large quantities of food. If feeding is permitted, it is at **designated times and stations** managed by residents, not whenever a tourist feels like it.
- Bring **fresh water** in warm months — dehydration is a bigger risk than hunger.
- Do not pet skittish cats. The friendly ones will find you.
- **No flash photography.** The cats live here; you are passing through.
- **Avoid the weekend rush.** Weekday ferries are quieter for both cats and humans.
- Take all trash, including food packaging, off the island.

There is also a real, ongoing **environmental concern**: feral cat colonies are documented predators of seabirds and small native species, and several Japanese conservation groups have raised concerns about the ecological footprint of romanticizing un-managed island colonies. TNR addresses welfare but does not fully resolve this tension. It is worth knowing before you book the ferry.

## Cats, Commerce, and the Maneki-Neko

Cat-tourism in Japan does not stop at the ferry terminal. Tokyo has its own land-based version in [Yanaka's cat-friendly old-town backstreets](/blog/yanaka-cat-village/), and the broader animal-cafe economy — from [owl cafes in Tokyo](/blog/owl-cafe-tokyo/) to [hedgehog cafes in Roppongi](/blog/hedgehog-cafe-roppongi/) — orbits the same cultural gravity: a dense urban population, limited domestic space, and an unusually strong public affection for small animals.

That affection runs deep. The **maneki-neko**, the beckoning good-luck cat seen in every shop window from Hokkaido to Okinawa, traces its most-cited origin to **Imado Shrine in Asakusa** during the Edo period, when a poor old woman is said to have been guided to prosperity by the gesture of a beckoning cat in a dream. Whether or not the legend is literal, the iconography is everywhere — and the cat islands sit, culturally, at the strange far end of the same line.

## Practical Notes for 2026

A short, current reference for the four most accessible islands:

- **Tashirojima** — Ferry from **Ishinomaki Port**, ~40 min, ~¥1,500 each way. Small guesthouse availability if booked weeks ahead.
- **Aoshima** — Ferry from **Nagahama Port** (Ehime), ~30 min, ~¥1,400 round-trip. Day-trip only. No facilities.
- **Manabeshima** — Ferry from **Kasaoka** (Okayama), ~70 min. Guesthouse and a cafe exist.
- **Ainoshima** (Fukuoka) — More frequent boats; closer to a working fishing port than a tourist site.

## TL;DR

- About **eleven Japanese islands** qualify as *neko-jima*, with cats outnumbering humans — most heavily on **Tashirojima** (~50 humans / ~100 cats) and **Aoshima** (~13 humans / ~80 cats in 2026).
- The colonies trace back to **Edo-era silkworm farmers** importing cats for pest control; the silk industry collapsed in the 1920s and the cats stayed.
- A **2014 viral video** triggered a tourism boom that led to feline weight-gain problems and a **2018 feeding ban** on Aoshima.
- **TNR programs** (Aoshima Cat Project, Tashirojima Reborn) now manage most colonies, but welfare and ecological concerns remain real.
- If you go: weekday ferries, fresh water, no flash, no surplus food, and treat the **2011 tsunami history** of Tashirojima with the seriousness it deserves.

> The cats of Aoshima are not pets. They are the last residents of a village that has run out of people.
