# Japan Has Cigarette-Shaped Incense for Smoking After You've Quit

On a butsudan — a Buddhist home altar — in a quiet apartment in Suginami, a woman places a single unlit Seven Stars in a small brass holder next to her late husband's photograph. She lights the filter end. It glows orange and burns slowly, releasing a smell that is unmistakably tobacco, though no tobacco is being smoked. The cigarette is incense. The husband has been dead for nine years. He used to smoke a pack a day, and on the anniversary of his death, he still does.

This is not a novelty product, exactly. It is a category. And to understand why it exists, you have to start with the dead.

## TL;DR

- Japanese Buddhist tradition encourages offering the deceased things they loved in life — sake, coffee, cigarettes — at gravesites and home altars, a practice called **kuyo (供養)**.
- Kobayashi Pharmaceutical and Daihatsu (the incense maker, not the carmaker) sell **cigarette-shaped incense** — *tabako senko* — that mimics the scent of real brands like Marlboro, Mild Seven, and Seven Stars using tobacco extract.
- Prices run **¥500–¥800** per box, peak demand around **obon (mid-August) and ohigan (spring/autumn equinox)**.
- Anti-smoking groups dislike it. Bereaved families keep buying it anyway.

## The Tradition of Giving the Dead What They Liked

Japanese Buddhist mourning is unusually literal. The dead are not abstract spirits drifting in a vague afterlife — they are specific people with specific preferences. The funeral altar will often include a bowl of the deceased's favorite rice dish. The 49th-day memorial may feature their preferred sake. On obon, when ancestors are believed to return briefly to the household, families set out the foods and drinks the dead enjoyed when they were alive.

The practice is called *kuyo* (供養), usually translated as "memorial offering." The underlying logic is gentle and unsentimental: you cared for this person while they lived; you continue to care for them after they die; caring means remembering what they liked. A widow who knew her husband took two sugars in his coffee will continue putting two sugars in the cup she leaves on the altar. To do otherwise would feel like forgetting.

Cigarettes fall squarely into this category. For an entire generation of Japanese men — the cohort who built the postwar economy in a haze of Hi-Lite smoke — tobacco was not a vice to be excised from the eulogy. It was part of the person. Leaving a pack at the grave is, in a sense, the only honest way to host them.

## What You'll Actually See at the Cemetery

Walk through Yanaka Reien in Tokyo on a weekend afternoon and you will pass headstones with offerings arranged on the small stone shelves at their base. Some are predictable: rice, fruit, fresh chrysanthemums. Others are not. A can of Asahi Super Dry. A bottle of Pocari Sweat. A bento from the convenience store, still in its plastic. And often, tucked beside a stick of conventional sandalwood incense, a single cigarette — sometimes lit, sometimes not — propped against the stone.

At Higashi-Honganji's Tokyo branch in Asakusa, monks have grown used to families leaving entire unopened packs of Mild Seven on the temple's memorial registers during anniversary services. At nearby Sensoji, where the great incense cauldron in front of the Hondo draws crowds who waft the smoke onto their shoulders for health, the smaller side altars sometimes hold cigarette offerings left by relatives of regulars who used to come here every morning before work, smoke one, pray, and walk to the station.

The most striking version of the practice appears at military cemeteries. At Chiran in Kagoshima, where the kamikaze pilots flew from in 1945, visitors leave Coca-Cola — a drink the teenage pilots had tasted only rarely, and which has become a kind of shorthand for the lives they never got to live. The bottles sit in rows. The caps are removed so the spirits can drink.

## Enter the Cigarette Incense

The problem with leaving a real cigarette on an altar is obvious. It is a fire hazard. It smells, for non-smokers, awful. In an aging Japan where children of smokers are increasingly non-smokers themselves, the offering becomes a tribute the giver cannot bear to sit next to.

The solution arrived in the early 2000s from **Kobayashi Pharmaceutical**, a company best known abroad for menthol cooling patches and toilet deodorizers. Their *tabako senko* — literally "tobacco incense" — looks almost exactly like a cigarette: paper-wrapped, filter on one end, roughly 70mm long. You light it from the filter side, which is treated to ignite cleanly. It burns for about five minutes. A box of around twenty sticks retails for ¥500 to ¥800, sold in Buddhist supply shops, larger drugstores, and online via Amazon Japan.

The product line splits into recognizable archetypes. There is a Marlboro-style red box for the man who smoked American imports. There is a green-and-white version for fans of Mild Seven (now rebranded Mevius, though the incense stuck with the old name because the old name is what the dead knew it as). There is a Seven Stars variant — sharper, slightly sweeter — and a Hi-Lite-adjacent option for the truly old-school. None of them carry the actual trademarked names. The packaging is suggestive without being legally actionable.

The scents are not synthetic approximations of "what cigarettes smell like in general." They use **tobacco extract** — a concentrated essence derived from cured tobacco leaves — blended into the standard *makko* (incense binder) base. The result is a smell that smokers and ex-smokers recognize instantly and that non-smokers find startlingly true to life. There is no nicotine. There is no tar. There is, however, real tobacco aroma, which is why some anti-smoking advocates dislike the product on principle.

**Daihatsu Group** — the long-established incense manufacturer in Osaka, unrelated to the Daihatsu motor company — produces a competing line aimed more at temples and cemetery suppliers, sold in bulk to Buddhist goods wholesalers. Between the two companies, the niche is small but stable, and demand spikes predictably twice a year: around **obon in mid-August**, when families return to ancestral homes to greet returning spirits, and during **ohigan**, the weeks around the spring and autumn equinoxes when Japanese Buddhism observes its biannual grave-visiting season.

## The Controversy Nobody Wins

Predictably, anti-smoking groups have raised objections. Japan's Society for Tobacco Control has flagged tabako senko as a form of cultural normalization, arguing that the visual familiarity of a glowing cigarette — even one made of incense — softens public attitudes toward smoking at exactly the moment Japan is finally pushing smoking rates down. There have been editorials. There have been calls for warning labels.

The pushback from bereaved families has been quiet but firm: this is a funerary product, not a recreational one. Nobody is buying tabako senko for the experience of smoking. They are buying it because their father is dead, and their father smoked, and the alternative is to pretend he didn't.

Kobayashi Pharmaceutical, for its part, has not made the product a marketing priority. It sits on the shelf next to the lavender-scented sandalwood and the coffee-flavored memorial incense — another niche entry in the same category — and the company has shown no interest in either expanding or apologizing for it.

## Memory as a Smell

There is a particular Japanese expression that comes up in this context: *omokage* (面影), usually translated as "a trace" or "a lingering image" of someone gone. The word implies that the dead persist not as ghosts but as half-glimpses — a phrase in someone's handwriting, a song on the radio, the shape of a relative's profile in a stranger on the train.

Smell is the most omokage-shaped sense. It cannot be summoned on demand and it cannot be photographed, but when it arrives it brings the person with it almost completely. The widow lighting a Seven Stars on her husband's altar is not performing a ritual she half-believes in. She is reproducing, deliberately, the smell of being married to him. For five minutes the apartment smells the way it used to smell. Then the stick burns out. Then she opens a window.

It is, like a great deal of Japanese mourning, deeply practical and quietly devastating, and nobody involved seems to find it strange.
