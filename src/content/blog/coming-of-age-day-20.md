# Japan Celebrates 20-Year-Olds With a Day of Drunk Speeches

On the second Monday of every January, roughly a million Japanese twenty-year-olds put on the most expensive clothes they have ever worn, file into civic auditoriums, and listen to their mayor talk about responsibility. Then, in the legally sanctioned aftermath, a meaningful percentage of them get drunk in public for the first time in their lives. Foreign tourists wandering past a shrine that afternoon usually assume a wedding has exploded.

It hasn't. It's Seijin no Hi — Coming-of-Age Day — and it has been running, on roughly this schedule, since 1948.

## The Holiday Itself

Seijin no Hi (成人の日) is a national holiday honoring everyone who turned twenty in the previous calendar year. The date floats: second Monday of January, courtesy of Japan's Happy Monday system, which long ago shoved most public holidays onto Mondays to manufacture three-day weekends. Banks close. Schools close. Public transit fills, slowly, with extremely well-dressed people who are clearly not commuting.

Twenty is the load-bearing number. It used to be the legal age of adulthood in Japan — the threshold for voting, signing contracts, smoking, drinking, and being fully on the hook for your own decisions. The holiday formalizes the crossing. Every municipality in the country hosts its own ceremony, called seijin-shiki (成人式), and the Internal Affairs Ministry estimates that about 1.06 million people qualified as new adults in 2026. That sounds like a lot until you compare it to the 1970 peak of 2.46 million. The number has been quietly halving for half a century, which is one of those Japanese statistics that says everything about everything.

Attendance still runs around eighty percent of eligible twenty-year-olds nationally. For an optional event in an aging country with a famously cynical youth cohort, that is an absurdly high turnout.

## What Actually Happens at Seijin-shiki

The ceremony itself is, by design, mildly dull. Attendees gather at a city hall, civic auditorium, or convention center. The mayor or a designated dignitary delivers a speech about adulthood, civic duty, and the future of Japan. There may be a video montage. There is almost always a commemorative gift — a small bag, a certificate, occasionally a municipal mascot keychain. The whole thing lasts about an hour.

The interesting part is the costume.

Women, overwhelmingly, wear furisode (振袖) — the long-sleeved formal kimono reserved for unmarried women, with sleeves that nearly graze the floor. A full furisode set, including the obi, accessories, hair, and makeup, runs anywhere from ¥150,000 to ¥500,000 to rent for the day. Many families own one, passed down or commissioned at significant cost. Hairstyling alone often starts at 4 a.m., which is why hair salons across Japan are essentially unbookable for the entire first half of January.

Men have it easier. Most wear a Western suit. A smaller, photogenic minority wear hakama — the pleated trousers traditionally paired with a haori jacket — which photograph well and cost considerably less than a furisode rental.

After the ceremony, the day shifts into its second, more important phase: photography. New adults travel in clusters to local shrines, parks, and family homes for portraits. The group shrine selfie has become the dominant aesthetic, with rows of furisode-clad women arranged on stone steps and male friends in suits arranged sheepishly behind them. Then, dinner with family. Then, often, drinking with friends — which, until 2022, was the first legally permitted drinking of their lives.

## The 2022 Legal Reform That Broke Nothing

In April 2022, Japan lowered the legal age of adulthood from twenty to eighteen. Eighteen-year-olds can now sign contracts, take out loans, get married without parental consent, and obtain a driver's license under fully adult terms. Voting had already been lowered to eighteen in 2016.

What did not change: the drinking age, the smoking age, and the legal gambling age all remain twenty. The seijin-shiki ceremony, in most municipalities, also remained at twenty.

This produced exactly the kind of bureaucratic contradiction Japan handles best by ignoring it. You are an adult at eighteen for purposes of borrowing money, but a child for purposes of buying a beer to celebrate borrowing money. A handful of municipalities — Iwate Prefecture's Morioka, for example — experimented with eighteen-year ceremonies. Most kept twenty, citing the practical objection that eighteen-year-olds are in the middle of university entrance exams in January and would not attend. The kimono industry, which has a meaningful political voice, was reportedly not displeased by this decision.

The official renaming of the ceremony in many cities — from seijin-shiki to hatachi no tsudoi (はたちの集い, "gathering of twenty-year-olds") — was the only visible concession. Everything else proceeds as before.

## The Economics of Looking Good for One Day

The kimono rental industry generates over ¥1.5 billion annually in direct Coming-of-Age Day business. The photo studio market built around the holiday clears another ¥3 billion, with major chains like Studio Alice and Studio Mario booking sessions twelve to eighteen months in advance. Furisode Hakubaikan and Kimono Lease Online dominate the rental market, and pre-ceremony "preview" sessions — where the family photographs the new adult in the rented furisode weeks before the actual day — have become standard upsells.

This is, in aggregate, a multi-billion-yen mobilization built around a single Monday.

It also occasionally collapses. The most notorious case was Hanairo, a discount kimono rental chain that took deposits from thousands of customers ahead of Coming-of-Age Day 2018, then shuttered its Shibuya location and several other branches the morning of the ceremony. Hundreds of twenty-year-olds arrived at locked doors to discover their reserved kimono — and the cash they had pre-paid — were gone. Volunteer kimono dressers and rival shops mobilized to clothe as many of the stranded as possible. Hanairo's parent company filed for bankruptcy days later. The incident is still cited every January as a cautionary tale about cheap rentals.

## Spectacle, Idols, and the Disney Variant

Municipalities have noticed that urban twenty-year-olds increasingly skip the ceremony. The decline is most pronounced in central Tokyo wards, where attendance has slipped well below the national average and where the speech-from-the-mayor format competes poorly with literally anything else.

The counter-strategy is entertainment. Yokohama Arena's ceremony — one of the largest in the country, with roughly 10,000 attendees in 2024 — pairs the civic speeches with live idol performances and DJ sets. Urayasu City, home to Tokyo Disney Resort, has since 2002 offered its new adults a free post-ceremony Disneyland session. The image of a thousand furisode-clad women lined up for the teacups is one of the more reliable wire-service photos of the January news cycle.

It works, narrowly. Urayasu's attendance rates remain among the highest in the Kanto region.

## The Yokosuka Tradition

Then there is the other tradition.

In a handful of cities — Yokosuka and Tachikawa being the most notorious, with appearances also in Kitakyushu and Naha — a small minority of new adults treat seijin-shiki as license to riot, decoratively. Modified motorcycles, brightly colored zoot-suit-adjacent hakama, fireworks fired indoors, occasionally a flipped or dented vehicle. Police nationwide make roughly fifty arrests per year tied to Coming-of-Age Day disturbances, almost all in this same handful of cities, almost all of them young men.

The Japanese press covers these incidents with a mixture of disapproval and, frankly, anticipation. Local TV crews now stake out the known cities the night before. The aesthetics have hardened into a recognizable subculture — part bosozoku biker-gang revival, part reality-show audition — and the participants are, transparently, performing for the cameras they know will be there. Yokosuka's mayor has periodically asked them to stop. They have not stopped.

## Cultural Context for Visitors

Seijin no Hi sits in the same category as the American bar or bat mitzvah and the Latin American quinceanera: a culturally specific, family-centered marker of legal-and-symbolic adulthood, observed by a society that otherwise has very few formal rituals for entering grown life. Like those events, it survives mostly on photographic and economic momentum — the photos must be taken, the dress must be rented, the relatives have already booked the train tickets.

If you are visiting Japan in early January:

- Do not book a hair salon between January 2 and the holiday Monday itself. You will be politely refused. The salons are running twenty-year-olds through their chairs in continuous shifts from before dawn.
- Expect kimono crowds at major shrines on the holiday Monday, especially Meiji Jingu, Yasaka, and Sumiyoshi Taisha. Photography is generally welcomed at a respectful distance. Asking before taking close-up portraits of strangers is good manners.
- Train stations are unusually beautiful and unusually slow. The escalators do not move faster for kimono.

It is the closest thing modern Japan has to a national graduation day. The mayor's speech is forgettable. The drinking is, by global standards, restrained. The clothes are extraordinary. And the underlying message — that adulthood is something a community formally hands you, in a room, on a specific Monday — is one of the more genuinely foreign things Japan still does in public.

## TL;DR

Seijin no Hi is Japan's second-Monday-of-January holiday for new twenty-year-olds. About 1.06 million qualified in 2026, down from a 1970 peak of 2.46 million. Eighty percent attend their local seijin-shiki ceremony, women in ¥150,000–¥500,000 furisode kimono, men in suits or hakama. The legal adulthood age was lowered to eighteen in 2022, but the ceremony, drinking, smoking, and gambling all stayed at twenty. The kimono industry clears ¥1.5 billion a year off the day, Tokyo Disneyland has hosted twenty-year-olds since 2002, and a small subset of new adults in Yokosuka and Tachikawa still mark the occasion by setting off fireworks indoors.
