# How Conveyor-Belt Sushi Math Feeds Tokyo's Hungriest Office Workers

A plate of salmon nigiri costs ¥110. The belt moves at roughly eight centimeters per second. The plate you just grabbed has an RFID chip in its base, a camera tracking how long it has been circulating, and a touchscreen above your knee that knows what you've ordered, what's running low in the kitchen, and how many gachapon spins you've earned. None of this is presented as impressive. It is just lunch.

Kaiten-zushi — conveyor-belt sushi — looks like a gimmick. It is, in fact, one of the most quietly engineered fast-food systems on earth, and the four chains running it now move more raw fish than most countries' entire restaurant industries combined.

## The Osaka Brewer Who Watched Beer Bottles Too Long

The standard origin story goes like this: in the mid-1950s, a small sushi shop owner named Yoshiaki Shiraishi was having staffing problems in Higashiosaka. He couldn't hire enough chefs, and customers couldn't see what was being made. Then he toured the Asahi beer factory in Suita and watched bottles glide past on a conveyor for an uninterrupted ten minutes. The mental jump from beer to sushi took him another five years of prototyping the belt angle, the speed, and the curvature of the corners so plates wouldn't slide off.

In April 1958 he opened Genroku Sushi (元禄寿司) in Fuse, Higashiosaka, with the first working kaiten belt. It ran at a steady 8cm per second — the same speed most modern chains still use, because faster makes people anxious and slower makes the rice dry out. Genroku still exists. The original shop is a minor pilgrimage site for restaurant nerds.

For about two decades kaiten-zushi was a regional Osaka curiosity. Then the 1970 World Expo in Osaka put Shiraishi's belt in front of a national audience, and the format started spreading. By the 1990s it had eaten the casual sushi market whole.

## The Four-Chain Cartel That Now Runs Lunch

Today four chains effectively define the segment:

- **Sushiro** (スシロー): the giant, over 600 domestic stores, listed via parent FOOD & LIFE COMPANIES.
- **Kura Sushi** (くら寿司): around 500 domestic stores, the tech evangelist of the group.
- **Hama Sushi** (はま寿司): roughly 540 stores, owned by Zensho — the same conglomerate behind Sukiya gyudon — and the most aggressive on price.
- **Choushi-maru** (銚子丸): a smaller premium chain concentrated in Kanto, positioned half a tier upmarket.

Between them they cover almost every suburban shopping center, every JR station with a department store attached, and most Aeon mall food floors. You are rarely more than a fifteen-minute walk from one in any city with a population over 200,000.

## The Math of a ¥110 Plate

The headline number is ¥110, or ¥120 after consumption tax shifts. Premium plates — fattier tuna, uni in season, certain shrimp — sit at ¥165 or ¥330. A handful of "specialty" items climb higher, but the base SKU is engineered around ¥110.

To make that work, food cost has to land under thirty percent. Industry filings from Sushiro's parent suggest a ¥150 plate carries roughly ¥40-45 of food cost — soy, rice, fish, wasabi, the plate itself. The rest is rent, labor (now heavily automated), utilities, and the belt.

This is only possible because the supply chain has been industrialized end to end. Salmon arrives frozen from Norway and Chile in standardized blocks. Rice is cooked centrally and portioned by robot — a Suzumo nigiri robot can shape 3,600 pieces of rice per hour. Soy sauce is bottled by the megaliter. The "chef" in most kaiten kitchens is a teenager placing pre-sliced fish on pre-formed rice, occasionally toasting something with a torch.

The result: a meal that would have cost ¥4,000 at a counter sushi shop in 1990 now costs ¥1,500, served faster, with no human interaction required.

## The 2010 Touchscreen Revolution

For its first half-century, kaiten-zushi worked exactly as the name implied — plates went around and around, you grabbed what you wanted. This was wasteful. Up to half of plates went uneaten and had to be discarded after about thirty minutes of circulating, when the rice began to dry.

Around 2010, Kura Sushi started a quiet rebuild. They installed individual tablets at every seat, let customers order directly, and routed those orders on a second "express" belt above the main one — usually with a small bullet train or cartoon mascot delivering the plate to your exact seat number. The shared rotating belt became almost vestigial.

They also embedded an RFID chip into the base of every plate. The system knows when a plate was made, where it has been, and when it should be removed. AI cameras above the belt now flag plates that have been circulating too long, or whose surface looks wrong. Hama Sushi and Sushiro followed within five years. The whole industry quietly converted from "self-service buffet on a belt" to "logistics network with a vestigial belt."

The economic effect was significant: food waste fell, labor dropped further, throughput per seat climbed. Sushiro IPO'd in 2017 at roughly ¥120 billion market cap on essentially this thesis.

## The Gachapon and the Soy-Sauce Saboteurs

Kura took the engineering one step further with Bikkura Pon — the slot above each seat that swallows your empty plates. Every five plates triggers an animated gachapon on the tablet; win, and a capsule toy drops out of the wall. This is partly child entertainment, partly a clever way to make customers physically dispose of their own dishes, eliminating bussing labor.

Then, in January 2023, a teenager in Gifu uploaded a phone video of himself licking a soy sauce bottle at a Sushiro and putting it back on the conveyor. Two more videos surrounding similar pranks went viral within a week. Sushi tero — sushi terrorism — became a national story. Sushiro's stock dropped roughly five percent in a day, wiping out about ¥17 billion in market value.

The chains responded with surprising speed. Sushiro removed shared soy sauce bottles in many branches, installed partition glass between belt and seats at the worst-affected stores, mounted additional security cameras over the belt, and accelerated the shift toward order-only express tracks with no rotating plates at all. Several locations now have no shared belt; everything you eat was ordered specifically for you and delivered directly to your seat. The pranksters were sued, criminally charged, and a wider moral panic about restaurant manners briefly took over morning television.

The format adapted within months. Which is itself the Japanese pattern: an aberration appears, the system absorbs it, the new equilibrium becomes invisible.

## Exporting the Belt

Sushiro now operates stores in Singapore, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Korea, mainland China, and the US, where its first Los Angeles outpost opened in 2024. Kura Sushi USA — a separately listed entity — opened a flagship Manhattan store in 2023 and has been expanding aggressively up the coast. Both chains pitch themselves abroad on the same logic that worked in Japan: industrialized supply chain plus theatrical tech plus low headline price.

The overseas pricing is, predictably, less generous. A plate in Times Square runs $3.50, a different proposition from ¥110 in Osaka.

## Etiquette, Quickly

- Do not return a plate to the belt. Once it is in your hand, it is yours.
- Do not order from the belt and then ignore the plate. The system is tracking you.
- Do not double-dip soy sauce with already-bitten nigiri.
- Stack empties or use the in-counter slot — both are correct.
- Green tea is free; the powder is in a jar at your seat and the hot water tap is built into the counter.

## TL;DR

Kaiten-zushi was invented in 1958 by an Osaka shop owner who watched beer bottles too long. Sixty-eight years later it is a ¥600-billion segment dominated by four chains, run on RFID plates, tablet ordering, express bullet-train belts, and AI spoilage cameras. The base plate still costs ¥110. The shared rotating belt is slowly disappearing. The system absorbed a viral sabotage panic in 2023 and emerged more automated than before. Lunch arrives in under three minutes.
