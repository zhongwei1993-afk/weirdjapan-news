# The Tokyo Vending Machine That Sells Live Crabs

A few steps from the Yodobashi exit of Akihabara JR station, between a taxi rank and a row of capsule-toy machines, there is a refrigerated cabinet with a glass front. Inside, slowly moving in shallow, oxygenated water, are crabs. You insert ¥3,000. A door slides open. A crab, sealed inside a clear plastic carrier, drops into the collection tray. It is, at this point, still alive. You walk away with it.

The machine is called the Snow Crab Vending Machine. It has been there, in one form or another, since 2019. It is operated by a Tokyo seafood wholesaler named Manchuya — written 万千夜, literally "ten-thousand-thousand-nights" — and it is one of the more honest expressions of how Japan thinks about food, retail, and the line between novelty and infrastructure.

## What's actually inside the cabinet

The standard product is a live Hanasakigani (花咲蟹), the spiny red crab pulled from the cold waters off eastern Hokkaido, or a Watarigani (渡蟹), the smaller blue swimming crab common to Tokyo Bay and points south. The cheaper slots start around ¥3,000. The premium slots — larger animals, heavier shells — run to about ¥7,000. Manchuya sources its stock from suppliers in Hokkaido and trucks it down to a holding facility in Tokyo, where the crabs are loaded into the machines and topped up every few days depending on turnover.

A second cabinet, usually adjacent, handles what the company loosely calls "lobster" — generally Ise-ebi (Japanese spiny lobster) or, on lower-priced days, large kuruma-ebi prawns. Both are sold in the same drop-and-carry format. The presentation is identical to a soft-drink machine: backlit menu, coin slot, selection buttons, satisfying mechanical thunk. The contents are not.

Manchuya has expanded slowly. There are now units at the Yodobashi-side exit of Akihabara station, inside Atre Ueno, and, since 2023, at a roadside lot in Honjo, Saitama. The Honjo opening was the company's first installation outside central Tokyo and is read inside the industry as a test of whether the format works for drivers rather than commuters.

## The broader live-seafood vending economy

Manchuya is the most photographed example, but it is not the only one. In Chiba prefecture, a small fishing cooperative installed a chilled vending unit in 2021 that dispenses whole live yellowtail (buri), gutted on request by a staff member who appears from a back room when the machine pings. In Hyogo, along the Seto Inland Sea coast, several oyster and mussel farmers run unmanned roadside boxes stocked daily with that morning's harvest, paid for with a coin slot and an honor system.

Around those live offerings sits a much larger ecosystem of frozen-seafood machines. Frozen tuna chunks for home sashimi run about ¥1,500 a pack. Salmon fillets, individually vacuum-sealed, sit alongside boil-in-bag octopus and pre-portioned ikura. None of this is hidden behind a specialty-store counter. It is in train stations, parking lots, and the side walls of pachinko parlors, sold the same way a can of coffee is sold.

Compared with the United States, where vending is still overwhelmingly limited to soft drinks, chips, and the occasional refrigerated sandwich, the Japanese machine has become a genuine retail channel. Live animals are simply the far end of that spectrum.

## The engineering, and the company behind it

The hardware is more interesting than it looks. Each cabinet runs a small refrigeration loop tuned to the species — colder for Hanasakigani, slightly warmer for Watarigani — paired with a low-noise air pump that keeps dissolved oxygen in the holding water above a set threshold. An RFID tag on each plastic carrier tracks inventory, so the operator's back office knows in near real time which units are running low and which animals have been sitting too long.

A Tokyo-based FoodTech startup called Yorozu Kakou is behind several of the newer installations, including the Honjo unit and at least one of the Atre Ueno cabinets. The company sells the machines as a turnkey package: hardware, supply contract, monitoring software, and a maintenance route. Their pitch to wholesalers is straightforward — vending lets a small seafood operator extend trading hours into the late-night and pre-dawn windows when traditional fish counters are closed, without paying for a clerk.

The viral moments tend to come from operator error, not engineering failure. In 2022 a clip circulated widely on Japanese social media of a tourist retrieving a Watarigani from an Akihabara machine, fumbling the carrier on the pavement, and watching the crab scuttle, briefly and surprisingly fast, in the direction of the station entrance. It was recaptured. The video was viewed several million times and is, in practical terms, the single most effective piece of marketing the format has ever had.

## Welfare, law, and what Japan does not regulate

The animal-welfare question is real and worth stating plainly. In 2021 the London School of Economics published a review, commissioned by the UK government, concluding that decapod crustaceans — crabs, lobsters, prawns — meet the scientific criteria for sentience. The UK formally recognised that finding by amending the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act the same year. The practical consequence in Britain is a slow tightening of how live crustaceans can be transported, stored, and killed.

Japan has no equivalent statute. Crustaceans are not covered by the Act on Welfare and Management of Animals, which applies to vertebrates. The vending machines themselves fall under a standard retail food-vendor license issued at the municipal level. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government's food-safety inspections cover hygiene, refrigeration, and product labelling. They do not inspect, or have a category for, the welfare conditions of a live animal held in a coin-operated cabinet. There is, in other words, no regulator whose job it is to ask how long a crab can reasonably be kept in a vending slot before sale.

This is not, by the operators' account, a deliberate evasion. It is the absence of a framework. Manchuya states publicly that unsold animals are rotated out within a defined window and either returned to a holding tank or sold through conventional channels. That claim is not externally audited because there is no body whose remit covers auditing it.

## Why this is tolerated, and exported

The cultural reading is the part most Western coverage misses. Japan has a long, matter-of-fact relationship with live seafood at the point of sale. Tanks of swimming fish in supermarket aisles, live eels behind glass at department-store food halls, and the ikezukuri tradition of preparing sashimi from a fish still moving on the plate are all unremarkable parts of the domestic food landscape. A vending machine, in that context, is a logistics change, not a moral one.

It also helps that the format reads, domestically, as practical rather than provocative. The customer base is not primarily tourists. It is restaurant owners doing a late top-up, izakaya cooks who missed the morning market, and a thin layer of curious locals. The viral foreign attention is a side effect, and one the operators appear to enjoy without depending on. Tourists pay full price for the novelty. Trade buyers come back the next week.

Whether that equilibrium holds is an open question. If Japan ever extends sentience-based protections to crustaceans, the machines will need a different regulatory category, and possibly a different design. Until then, the cabinets stay lit, the pumps keep running, and the crabs keep arriving in plastic carriers, one ¥3,000 coin at a time.

## TL;DR

- A Tokyo seafood wholesaler called Manchuya has been selling live Hanasakigani and Watarigani from vending machines outside Akihabara station since 2019, with units now at Atre Ueno and a 2023 location in Honjo, Saitama.
- Prices run ¥3,000 to ¥7,000 per crab. Supplementary cabinets sell live lobster and prawns.
- The format sits inside a broader Japanese ecosystem of seafood vending, from Chiba's yellowtail machines to ¥1,500 frozen tuna packs in train stations.
- The engineering — oxygenated tanks, species-tuned refrigeration, RFID inventory — is partly supplied by FoodTech startup Yorozu Kakou.
- Japan's animal-welfare law does not cover crustaceans, unlike the UK's post-2021 framework, and no Tokyo inspection certifies the live-animal side of the operation.
