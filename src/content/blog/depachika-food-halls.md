# Tokyo's Department Store Basements Are Foodie Paradises

Take any escalator in a Tokyo department store and ride it down past the perfume counters, past the handbags, past the polite woman bowing at the elevator. Keep going until the air gets colder and starts smelling like soy-glazed eel, butter croissants, and pickled plum at the same time. You have arrived at the **depachika** (デパ地下, literally "department-store basement") — a 6,000-square-meter underground food city run by 150 to 300 vendors who all believe their single product is worth a 40-minute queue. They are usually right.

## What a Depachika Actually Is

Depachika are the basement-level food halls beneath Japan's major department stores. They are not food courts. There is no central seating, no plastic trays, no shared cutlery. What you get instead is a tightly packed grid of glass counters, each one operated by a separate brand — sometimes a 400-year-old wagashi house, sometimes a Parisian patisserie that has decided Shinjuku is the better market.

The categories rotate predictably across every major depachika:

- **Wagashi and yokan** — traditional Japanese sweets, often in lacquered boxes meant as gifts
- **Prepared bento** — assembled by hand at 5am, sold out by 8pm
- **Tempura, tonkatsu, yakitori** — fried to order or stacked in heated cases
- **Sushi and sashimi** — including ¥18,000 platters built like architectural models
- **Tsukemono** — pickled vegetables sold by the gram from open wooden barrels
- **French patisserie** — Pierre Hermé, Henri Charpentier, Sadaharu Aoki, all competing in the same hallway
- **Regional sake and shochu** — often poured for free if you look interested and over 30

The price ceiling is theoretical. The floor is roughly ¥800 for a respectable bento. The ceiling is whatever Toraya has decided a single piece of yokan should cost today. (Currently around ¥1,500. For a slice. About the size of a domino.)

## The Six Tokyo Locations That Matter

There are dozens of depachika in greater Tokyo. These six are the ones serious eaters argue about:

- **Isetan Shinjuku** — 3-14-1 Shinjuku, Shinjuku-ku. Considered the gold standard. Two basement floors, roughly 300 vendors, the patisserie section alone has its own escalator logic. The flagship.
- **Mitsukoshi Nihonbashi** — 1-4-1 Nihombashi-Muromachi, Chuo-ku. The oldest. Opened as a kimono shop in 1673, so its depachika sells the kind of wagashi that historically went to shoguns.
- **Mitsukoshi Ginza** — 4-6-16 Ginza, Chuo-ku. Smaller than Nihonbashi but denser. Strong on prepared mains, weak on bread.
- **Takashimaya Shinjuku** — 5-24-2 Sendagaya, Shibuya-ku. The most foreigner-legible. Wider aisles, more English signage than the others, attached to the JR Shinjuku Takashimaya Times Square complex.
- **Daimaru Tokyo Station** — 1-9-1 Marunouchi, Chiyoda-ku. The transit hub depachika. Built directly above Tokyo Station's ekiben concourse, which means you can grab a regional bento for the bullet train downstairs after browsing the proper depachika upstairs. (More on this in a minute.)
- **Matsuya Ginza** — 3-6-1 Ginza, Chuo-ku. The locals' pick. Less polished than Mitsukoshi, better deals after 7pm.
- **Tokyu Food Show Shibuya** — B1F, Shibuya Mark City. Reopened in 2023 after the Shibuya station rebuild. Designed for commuters, so the bento packaging is unusually train-friendly.

## The Tasting Culture (And the 7:30pm Discount)

Two things make depachika unlike any food retail experience in the West.

First: **the samples**. Almost every counter offers something on a toothpick — a cube of wagyu, a sliver of yokan, a corner of cheese, a thimble of sake. This isn't a Costco-style loss-leader play. It's a centuries-old retail logic that assumes a customer who tastes will buy, and a customer who buys will return, and a customer who returns is the only kind worth having. The samples are also, technically, a free lunch if you have the stamina and the shamelessness. (We are not encouraging this. We are simply noting that it is mathematically possible.)

Second: **the closing-hour discount**. Most depachika start marking down ready-meals around 7:00pm, with the steepest cuts — typically 50% off — landing between 7:30 and 8:00pm, roughly 30 minutes before the basement closes. Bento that were ¥1,800 at lunch become ¥900 at 7:45. Sushi platters get yellow stickers slapped over the price tags. A small, polite crowd of locals — pensioners, single salarymen, students — appears like clockwork. There is no announcement. There is no scrum. People simply know.

If you want the full experience, do both: graze the samples at 4pm, return for half-price sushi at 7:45pm, eat the entire thing on a bench in a nearby park because depachika themselves have no seating. This is not a flaw. This is the design.

## Counters to Actually Visit

A short list of vendors that appear across multiple depachika and reliably justify the queue:

- **Toraya** — Imperial-grade yokan. Single piece around ¥1,500. The flagship counter is at Mitsukoshi Nihonbashi, but they have presence in most major basements.
- **Tsujiri** — Uji matcha specialists. Their matcha soft-serve at Takashimaya Shinjuku is what people are queueing for, and yes, it tastes like grass that went to finishing school.
- **Pierre Hermé Paris** — Macarons at roughly ¥350 each. The Isetan Shinjuku counter has flavors that do not exist in Paris, including a yuzu-shiso version that should be illegal.
- **Henri Charpentier** — French patisserie founded in Ashiya, in 1969. The financiers are the move. Around ¥250 a piece, boxed beautifully, indistinguishable from gold.
- **Aoyagi** — Shumai. Small, hand-folded, sold in cardboard boxes that steam open like little gifts. Best at Daimaru Tokyo Station because you can eat them on the train.
- **Echire** — Salted French butter croissants. The Marunouchi flagship is its own building, but Isetan stocks the basics. Limited quantities daily.

A passport gets you tax-free shopping at any of the major depachika on purchases of **¥5,001 or more in a single transaction**. The desk is usually on the ground floor or the seventh, never the basement, and the process takes about ten minutes. For a ¥6,000 box of macarons being shipped home in a suitcase, this is worth doing.

## How to Navigate Without Japanese

Depachika are surprisingly forgiving for non-Japanese speakers, because the entire space is engineered for the eye.

- **Point and pay works everywhere.** Vendors expect it. The transaction is brief and dignified.
- **Plastic food models** sit in many display windows showing exactly what's inside the bento. Price is on the sticker. There is nothing to decode.
- **English menus are uncommon** but English numerals on price tags are universal. If something costs ¥2,400, you will see "¥2,400."
- **Cash and IC cards (Suica, Pasmo) both work.** Most counters now accept Visa and Mastercard, though Toraya and the older wagashi houses sometimes still treat credit cards as a personal insult.
- **Refrigerated bags** are sold at the customer service counter for around ¥300 if you're carrying sashimi across town in August.

The depachika is Tokyo's **anti-konbini**. Where a 7-Eleven exists for the 11pm onigiri emergency, the depachika exists for the celebratory dinner, the apology gift, the in-law visit, the New Year box, the "we just signed the lease" moment. Price range runs roughly ¥800 for an entry bento to ¥10,000 and well beyond for the ready-made feasts. Nobody buys depachika food because they're hungry. They buy it because something is happening.

Before Tokyo Disneyland opened in 1983, the depachika at Mitsukoshi was — without exaggeration — the closest thing Japanese families had to a paid weekend destination. Parents took kids on the train into Nihonbashi specifically to ride the escalator, look at the cake counters, and eat lunch in the rooftop garden. Older Tokyoites still call it "Mitsuke food court," half-jokingly, because for an entire generation it served the function a theme park serves now.

And at Daimaru Tokyo Station, the depachika quietly bleeds into the **ekiben** ecosystem — the regional train bento halls one floor down. The handoff is seamless: you can browse Hokkaido uni at the depachika upstairs, then carry it onto a Shinkansen platform downstairs without ever stepping outside. For ekiben specifically, we wrote a [whole separate field guide](/blog/ekiben-train-bento).

## TL;DR

Depachika are the food halls under every major Japanese department store — 150 to 300 vendors per basement, organized by category, priced from ¥800 to ¥10,000+. The six Tokyo locations worth your time: Isetan Shinjuku, Mitsukoshi Nihonbashi, Mitsukoshi Ginza, Takashimaya Shinjuku, Daimaru Tokyo Station, Matsuya Ginza, plus Tokyu Food Show in Shibuya. Free samples at most counters, 50% discounts on ready-meals around 7:30pm, tax-free on ¥5,001+ with a passport. No seating, no English menus, no problem — point and pay. Toraya for yokan, Tsujiri for matcha, Pierre Hermé for macarons, Aoyagi for shumai. Lunch is theater. Dinner is theater on sale.
