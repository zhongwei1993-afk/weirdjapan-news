# Tokyo's Drone Light Shows Replaced Fireworks. Some Hate It.

A 1,824-drone elephant once floated over Tokyo Bay in total silence. No bang, no sulfur smell, no paper shells raining into the water — just an animal-shaped constellation rearranging itself politely for the cameras. Half of Tokyo loved it. The other half went home grumbling that fireworks have a soul and a swarm of lithium batteries does not.

That argument has been running, more or less without resolution, since 2018. It is also the argument that explains why summer in Japan is starting to look subtly, unmistakably different.

## A boom that started quietly around 2018

Drone light shows did not arrive in Japan with a press release. They crept in around 2018, mostly as a novelty layered on top of corporate product launches and a few experimental year-end events. Intel's Shooting Star drones had already been doing PyeongChang Olympics duty; Chinese operators were running 1,000-plus drone formations for tourism boards in Shenzhen. Japan watched, took notes, and then — characteristically — over-engineered its own version.

By 2020, domestic operators had begun building proprietary fleets and choreography pipelines instead of importing them. By the time crowds were allowed back outdoors after the pandemic, the drone show had quietly become a normal line item on event budgets for shopping malls, hotel openings, and city anniversaries. It is the kind of technology shift Japan tends to do well: nothing announces itself, and then one summer you realise every department store rooftop event has a drone act.

## SKY ELEPHANT and the moment it went mainstream

The cultural inflection point has a name, and the name is SKY ELEPHANT. During the delayed Tokyo 2020 Olympics in summer 2021, an unofficial display sent roughly 1,824 drones into the air over Tokyo Bay to form a slowly walking elephant, which then morphed into the Earth, which then morphed into the Games logo. The footage went viral, partly because it was genuinely beautiful and partly because, in a pandemic-emptied city, it was one of the only public spectacles anyone could safely watch.

Four years later, Osaka raised the stakes. The 2025 Expo at Yumeshima featured drone displays involving 3,000-plus aircraft, choreographed to music and integrated with the pavilions' own lighting. Visitors who had come to look at architecture stayed to film the sky. That show, more than any single Tokyo event, is what turned drones from "cool one-off" into "expected feature of any major Japanese spectacle."

## Who actually flies them

Three operators dominate the domestic market.

- **Red Cliff Inc.** — the most visible name, responsible for many of the largest Tokyo and Osaka displays. They run proprietary swarm software and have steadily pushed the upper limit of how many aircraft can be in a single Japanese show.
- **Shooting Star Drone** — Intel's spun-out platform, used widely in the early years and still flown by licensed partners for events that want a known global brand attached.
- **Kanagawa Tokai Aero Service** — a more traditional aviation operator that pivoted into drone choreography, particularly for shows around Yokohama Bayside and the Kanagawa coast.

A handful of smaller studios handle 200-to-500-drone shows for weddings, regional festivals, and the occasional pachinko parlour grand opening, which is a sentence that should not exist but does.

## What a show actually looks like

The format has converged to something fairly standard. A typical Japanese drone show uses **between 200 and 2,000 LED-equipped drones**, flying a **roughly ten-minute choreographed sequence** synchronised to music. Each drone is essentially a flying pixel; the choreography software treats the swarm as a 3D screen.

Imagery is custom and unmistakably local. Cherry blossoms unfurling and scattering. Mount Fuji rendered in outline, then filled in. Anime characters — licensed properly, because this is Japan and licensing is a sport — waving at the crowd. Mascots from the host city. Sponsor logos, inevitably, slotted in at the climax with the same dignity as a mid-roll ad.

Pricing scales with drone count, choreography complexity and venue. A corporate event with a custom narrative typically runs **between ¥10 million and ¥200 million**, the high end reserved for flagship product launches and city-funded summer festivals. That is not cheap, but it is also not wildly more expensive than a serious fireworks programme, which is part of why event planners keep switching.

## Why they're eating fireworks' lunch

Japan invented modern recreational fireworks culture. The Sumida River fireworks festival traces its origin to **1733**, draws roughly **one million attendees** to the riverbanks of eastern Tokyo every summer, and fires off something close to **20,000 shells** in a single evening. It is loud, hot, smoky, gloriously analogue, and absolutely central to the Japanese idea of summer.

It is also, increasingly, a logistical problem.

Climate change has pushed Japanese summers into genuinely dangerous heat. Crowd safety has become a more public concern after the 2022 Itaewon crush in Seoul reframed the conversation regionally. Coastal pyrotechnics in dry years carry real fire risk; municipal budgets have tightened; and the shells themselves are single-use objects whose debris ends up in the bay. Drones, by contrast, are silent, reusable, programmable, and do not catch dry grass on fire. For a venue making a cold-eyed risk assessment, the choice is no longer obvious.

So some venues have made the switch. Others have hedged, running a shortened hanabi programme followed by a drone sequence as a sort of digital encore. Purists hate this. Modernists love it. The Japanese internet has settled into a stable seasonal flame war between the two camps, conducted almost entirely in polite language.

## The regulation that made it possible

None of this would scale without a quiet piece of administrative law. In **December 2022**, METI and the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism updated Japan's drone regulations to permit **Level-4 BVLOS flights** — beyond visual line of sight, over populated areas, by certified operators.

Before that change, large drone swarms over urban crowds existed in a regulatory grey zone that required event-by-event exemptions. After it, operators could build a repeatable business. Almost every show described in this article post-dates that update, which is not a coincidence. Japan tends to legalise spectacle in batches.

## Where to actually see one

For visitors, the practical question is when and where. A short list:

- **Tokyo Bay summer events** — Odaiba and the Toyosu waterfront host drone displays through July and August, often tied to fireworks festivals or department-store anniversaries. Schedules drop in late spring; check city tourism boards.
- **Yumeshima, Osaka** — post-Expo, the artificial island has retained drone-show infrastructure for periodic displays. If you are in Kansai in summer, this is the highest-density option.
- **Yokohama Bayside** — Minato Mirai's harbour skyline is a regular drone-show venue, frequently for Kanagawa Tokai Aero Service shows tied to the city's port anniversary.
- **Universal Studios Japan** — since 2024, USJ has incorporated drone sequences into its night shows, which means you can see one without negotiating a single special-event ticket.

Hotels with rooftop bars in Shiodome, Roppongi and Shibuya have started quietly advertising "drone-view rooms" on summer event nights. The markup is real. The view, by most accounts, is worth it.

## The footprint, the future, and the awkward middle

The environmental story is more complicated than the marketing copy suggests. Drones are reusable; that is genuinely good. But each aircraft contains a lithium-ion battery with a finite cycle life, and a 2,000-drone fleet is a lot of batteries to eventually recycle. Charging the fleet for a single show consumes meaningful grid power. The net footprint is almost certainly lower than a comparable fireworks display, but "lower" is not "zero," and the industry is starting to be asked harder questions about end-of-life battery streams.

The next frontier is already visible. Operators are quietly piloting **AI-choreographed swarms** that generate formations from a text prompt or a piece of music, rather than from a human animator working frame by frame. Real-time wind compensation is becoming standard. A few R&D groups are working on drones that can also carry small pyrotechnic charges, which would close the loop by reintroducing the bang the purists miss — engineered, controlled, and presumably regulated to within an inch of its life.

That feels like the most Japanese possible resolution to the fireworks-versus-drones argument: not pick a side, just build the hybrid, then quietly normalise it until nobody remembers there was a fight.

## TL;DR

Drone light shows arrived in Japan around 2018, went mainstream with SKY ELEPHANT over Tokyo Bay in 2021, scaled to 3,000-plus aircraft at the Osaka 2025 Expo, and became a routine summer fixture after METI legalised Level-4 BVLOS flights in December 2022. Operators like Red Cliff, Shooting Star Drone and Kanagawa Tokai Aero Service run 200-to-2,000-drone shows for ¥10M-¥200M apiece, increasingly replacing fireworks at venues worried about heat, safety and debris. Purists are upset. The drones do not care.
