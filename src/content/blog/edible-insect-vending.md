# Japanese Vending Machines Sell Snack Bags of Grasshoppers

Somewhere between the hot-coffee machine and the umbrella dispenser in a Tokyo alley, there is a glass cabinet that sells crickets by the gram. It does not advertise itself. It does not flash. You feed it a ¥1,000 note, press a button, and a vacuum-sealed pouch of roasted insects drops into the tray. The transaction takes about nine seconds and nobody nearby looks up.

This is the current state of edible-insect retail in Japan: quiet, automated, and steadily expanding. Industry trackers counted roughly 150 insect vending machines across the country as of 2024, up from a handful in 2019. They are not a stunt. They are slowly becoming furniture.

## A Quiet Vending-Machine Boom

The format is simple. A standard refrigerated or ambient snack machine is loaded with shelf-stable packs of dried, seasoned, or sugared insects, then placed somewhere with foot traffic — a station underpass, the entrance to a discount store, the alley behind a ramen shop. There is no attendant. There is rarely signage in English. The price tags are small.

Most of the machines are operated or supplied by **TAKEO**, a Tokyo startup founded in 2014 by Ryota Mitsuhashi, which has become the dominant brand in domestic insect food. TAKEO ran its first vending machine outside its own shop in Kuramae and gradually licensed the model out to other operators. By 2024 the company's machines, plus a smaller wave of independent imitators, accounted for the bulk of the 150-or-so units now scattered across Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Kansai, and a handful of regional cities.

What's inside varies by location, but a typical machine carries:

- Roasted crickets, plain or salt-flavored
- **Inago** (rice-paddy grasshoppers), often boiled in soy and sugar
- Silkworm pupae, usually from Korean or Thai suppliers
- Whole scorpions and tarantulas, sourced from Southeast Asia
- Ant-encrusted lollipops and chocolate-coated mealworms
- Cricket-flour cookies and protein bars

Prices run from about **¥500 for a small pouch of crickets to ¥2,500 for a single tarantula or a gift box**. The pricier items are not really food. They are souvenirs, dares, or gifts for the kind of coworker who already owns everything.

## Where to Actually Find One in Tokyo

A few machines have settled into reliable spots. None are tourist attractions in any official sense, which is part of their appeal.

- **Shinokubo Mukimuki**, in the Korean-food district of Shin-Okubo, keeps a machine stocked with crickets, silkworms, and the occasional scorpion. The neighborhood's late-night crowd treats it as a curiosity vending machine, not a meal.
- **Asakusa Hozenji Yokocho**, a narrow lane near Sensoji, hosts one of the more photographed units, partly because it sits inside a row of small temple-adjacent shops and partly because the contrast photographs well.
- **Bug's Farm Kanda**, near the bookshop district, is closer to a vending-only shop than a single machine — multiple cabinets, a wider range of species, and laminated cards explaining sourcing.

There are also machines in Nakano, in front of certain Don Quijote branches, and in a few stations in Saitama and Kanagawa. TAKEO maintains a rough location list on its site, though it is not exhaustive and machines move.

## The Long History Vending Machines Are Standing On

Insect eating in Japan is not a 2020s import. **Inago no tsukudani** — small rice-paddy grasshoppers simmered in soy sauce, sugar, and mirin — has been documented as a regional staple in Nagano Prefecture since at least the Meiji period, and likely earlier. It was eaten because rice fields produced grasshoppers in large enough numbers to be worth gathering, and because mountain villages had limited access to sea protein.

**Hachinoko** — the larvae and pupae of wasps, also from Nagano and Gifu — has a similar story. The larvae were collected from underground nests, cooked in soy and sugar, and packed into rice bowls. Both dishes were poverty foods through the prewar and wartime period, eaten because they were available and cheap, not because they were fashionable.

That history is important context for the vending machines. Japan is not adopting an exotic Western trend. It is rediscovering, packaging, and selling back a food culture that mostly faded out of everyday life by the 1960s, when imported beef and refrigerated transport made grasshoppers redundant.

The current revival has a clearer trigger. In 2013 the UN Food and Agriculture Organization published *Edible Insects: Future Prospects for Food and Feed Security*, a 200-page report arguing that insects offered a sustainable protein source for a growing global population. The FAO noted, among other figures, that crickets convert feed to body mass roughly twelve times more efficiently than cattle, and that producing a kilogram of cricket protein uses about **90 percent less feed than the equivalent kilogram of beef**. Japanese startups — TAKEO, Bugmo, Gryllus — read the report and built companies on the back of it.

## Cookies, Cafés, and a High-School Incident

Most Japanese consumers will not buy a whole grasshopper. They will, sometimes, buy a cookie.

The cricket-powder product category has grown faster than the whole-insect one. **Bugmo Cricket Cookie**, made by an Osaka-based startup, sells for about **¥350 per pack** and uses cricket flour as roughly 10 percent of the dough. The flavor, by most accounts, is closer to a slightly nutty digestive biscuit than to anything obviously insect-derived. Bugmo also runs a D2C site that ships internationally, which has made it one of the easier entry points for curious overseas buyers.

The cookie category had its public stress test in November 2022, when a high school in Tokushima Prefecture served cricket-powder croquettes as part of a school-lunch program. The school had partnered with a local food-tech firm and framed the dish as an experiment in sustainable protein. The reaction online was sharp: parents complained that students had not been given an opt-out, food-allergy advocates pointed out the lack of clear labeling, and a small but loud contingent argued that pupils were being used as test subjects for an industry agenda. The school paused the program. The story circulated long enough to become a reference point in every subsequent debate about insect food in Japan.

It is worth being plain about the underlying safety issue. **Insect proteins overlap significantly with crustacean proteins** — tropomyosin in particular — which means anyone with a shrimp, crab, or shellfish allergy should treat cricket, grasshopper, and mealworm products as potential triggers. Japanese packaging usually notes this in small print. Vending machines, sometimes, do not.

## Sit-Down Insects: Cafés and ANTCICADA

For anyone who wants to skip the vending experience and eat insects with a plate and a chair, Tokyo has a small but real restaurant scene.

- **TAKEO Café**, attached to the company's flagship shop in Kuramae, serves cricket curry, silkworm rice bowls, and a rotating set of seasonal insect dishes. The room is bright, the menu is in Japanese and English, and the staff will answer sourcing questions in detail.
- **Saiboku Yokocho**, a food hall in Tokorozawa (Saitama), includes an insect-food stall as part of its rotation of small vendors, alongside grilled pork and craft beer.
- **ANTCICADA**, opened in Asakusa in 2020 by chef Yuta Shinohara, is the closest the category has to a fine-dining destination. The signature dish is an omakase ramen built on a broth of slow-simmered crickets, served as part of a tasting menu that runs around **¥6,000 per person**. Reservations are required and the room seats roughly a dozen.

ANTCICADA in particular has done more for the cultural legibility of insect food than any vending machine. A ¥6,000 ramen course is harder to dismiss as a gimmick than a ¥500 cricket pouch.

## Will Any of This Stick

The honest answer is that nobody knows yet. Insect food in Japan currently occupies a strange middle ground: too established to be a fad, too small to be mainstream. TAKEO is profitable but not large. Bugmo ships internationally but at modest volume. ANTCICADA is booked out but seats twelve. The 150 vending machines move product, but the per-machine revenue is reportedly modest.

The arguments in favor are familiar: protein efficiency, lower greenhouse emissions, smaller land use, an aging domestic population that may welcome alternative protein sources as agricultural labor shrinks. The arguments against are also familiar: insect food is more expensive than tofu or eggs, the supply chain is still small, the allergen overlap is real, and there is no obvious cultural pressure pushing ordinary households toward weekly cricket consumption.

What Japan has produced, instead, is a quiet retail infrastructure for people who are curious without being committed. You can buy a pouch of grasshoppers without talking to anyone. You can try a cricket cookie for less than the price of a coffee. You can eat a ¥6,000 cricket ramen and treat it as a tasting-menu experience rather than a sustainability statement. Whether any of this scales is a question for the next decade. For now, it is simply available, in vending machines, between the cigarettes and the canned coffee.

## TL;DR

Japan has roughly 150 edible-insect vending machines as of 2024, mostly run or supplied by Tokyo startup TAKEO. They stock crickets, grasshoppers, silkworm pupae, scorpions, and tarantulas for ¥500–¥2,500. The category sits on a long history of regional dishes like inago no tsukudani and hachinoko, was revived by the FAO's 2013 sustainability report, and now includes cricket cookies, specialty cafés, and the ¥6,000 cricket-broth ramen at ANTCICADA in Asakusa. Anyone with a shellfish allergy should skip the lot.
