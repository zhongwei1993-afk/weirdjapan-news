# Japan Built Bus Stops Shaped Like Giant Strawberries

On a quiet stretch of coastal highway in western Kyushu, a commuter waiting for the 7:42 bus stands inside a hollowed-out watermelon. Five kilometers up the road, a high-school student texts beneath a glossy red strawberry the size of a small van. Neither of them looks up. This is just where the bus stops.

> **TL;DR:** Along Route 207 in the Konagai district of Isahaya, Nagasaki, sixteen fiberglass bus shelters shaped like local fruit have stood since 1990, built for a regional travel expo and quietly never removed. Five of the most photogenic — strawberry, watermelon, orange, melon, and tomato — still serve actual commuters. To visit, take the JR Nagasaki Main Line to Konagai Station and walk or rent a bicycle.

The bus stops of Konagai are not a tourist attraction in any deliberate sense. There is no ticket booth, no gift shop, no signage pointing the way. They are functioning municipal infrastructure that happens to look like produce. Schoolchildren use them. Pensioners use them. The 9:14 to Isahaya pulls up beside a giant orange and nobody comments.

## A Travel Expo Nobody Remembers

In 1990, Nagasaki Prefecture hosted the **Japan Expo "Travel"** — Tabi Hakurankai, a regional event built around tourism, transport, and the idea that Kyushu might be persuaded into becoming a destination rather than a place people pass through on the way to somewhere else. The expo ran for several months at venues across the prefecture. Most of what was built for it has long since been demolished, recycled, or forgotten.

The fruit bus stops were not central to the program. They were a side commission — a small public-art gesture commissioned by the town of Konagai, then an independent municipality before its 2005 merger into Isahaya. The brief was modest: build something that would make visitors driving the coastal road remember the town. The designer, **Kashii Denwa**, settled on giant fiberglass fruit. Sixteen shelters were produced and installed along a roughly 8-kilometer stretch of National Route 207, between the towns of Konagai and Ariake.

The expo ended. The crowds went home. The bus stops stayed.

Three and a half decades later, most of the original sixteen are still standing — repainted occasionally, patched after typhoons, but otherwise unchanged. Local government has taken to calling them a tourism asset. Tour buses now slow down to let passengers photograph them. None of this was the plan.

## What Konagai Grows

The fruit selection is not arbitrary. The Ariake Sea coast of Isahaya is agricultural country, and each shelter corresponds to something the region actually produces:

- **Strawberries** — Nagasaki Prefecture is a major winter strawberry producer, and Isahaya farms ship to Kyushu and beyond.
- **Watermelons** — the warm, sun-baked plains behind the coastal road grow them in volume each summer.
- **Mandarin oranges (mikan)** — terraced groves climb the hills inland from Route 207.
- **Melons** — primarily the netted, expensive kind grown under glass.
- **Tomatoes** — a more recent regional specialty, marketed under the "Tomato Land" branding the local agricultural cooperative is still trying to make happen.

Of the sixteen original shelters, five varieties remain the most photographed. Each fruit appears multiple times along the route in slightly different scales and color treatments. The strawberries are the most popular — there is something about the contrast of a glossy red dome against grey asphalt and grey sky that translates well to a phone screen.

## The Stops Themselves

The shelters are roughly 2.5 meters tall, made of pigmented fiberglass over a steel frame. Inside, each contains a wooden bench, a timetable posted in a plastic sleeve, and — on the older stops — a small handwritten note from the previous user reminding the next user not to leave trash. The benches seat two adults uncomfortably or one adult and a school backpack comfortably.

The fruit are not stylized. They are not cartoon fruit. Kashii's design philosophy seems to have been straightforward representational mimicry: a watermelon should look like a watermelon, including the stripes and the small dimple where the stem attached. The strawberries have individual fiberglass seeds. The melon has the cross-hatched rind texture of a real **Yubari**-style melon. The effect from a passing car window is briefly disorienting — the brain registers "fruit" before it registers "structure," and there is a half-second of mild vertigo while the categories reshuffle.

Up close, the shelters show their age. Salt air from the Ariake Sea has dulled the paint. The orange has a small crack near the base where a truck reversed into it in 2014. The tomato — arguably the least convincing of the five, owing to the geometric difficulty of building a hollow seating area inside a sphere — has been repainted at least three times.

## How to Actually Visit

Konagai is reachable but not convenient, which is part of why the stops have remained relatively unbothered by mass tourism.

**By train from Nagasaki:** Take the JR Nagasaki Main Line from Nagasaki Station toward Isahaya. Transfer at Isahaya, then continue eastbound on the same line toward Tarami and Konagai. Total journey is roughly 50–70 minutes depending on connections. **Konagai Station** is unstaffed for much of the day; bring small change or an IC card.

**By train from Isahaya:** Direct, around 15–20 minutes. Isahaya is itself reachable from Nagasaki Airport by bus in about 40 minutes, which makes a same-day fly-in/visit/fly-out theoretically possible if your flight times cooperate.

**From Konagai Station:** The first fruit bus stop is roughly a 10-minute walk. To see the full string of five, plan on either a 2–3 hour walk along Route 207 or — the locally recommended option — a rental bicycle. A small shop near the station rents cheap city bikes; ask the station attendant if anyone is on duty, or check with the tourist information desk in Isahaya before you set out.

**By car:** Route 207 hugs the coast. Park at any of the small fishing harbors along the way; there are no dedicated lots at the bus stops themselves, and stopping in the road is genuinely dangerous because local drivers do not expect anyone to be photographing infrastructure.

The local bus does serve these stops. Schedules are sparse — sometimes one bus every two hours — so it functions more as a way to ride between two fruit than as serious transit.

## Photo Notes

The shelters face the road, which means the morning light favors the inland-facing fruit and the afternoon light favors the seaward ones. Overcast days produce the best color saturation; bright midday sun bleaches the older paintwork. The strawberry at Ariake is the most photogenic at golden hour, when the western light catches the seed texture. The watermelon shelters look best from below, shot upward against open sky.

Avoid photographing commuters without asking. The shelters are working bus stops, and the people inside them are not props. A nod and a quiet *sumimasen* covers most situations; most locals will simply step out of frame and wait for you to finish.

If you want the empty-shelter shot, early Sunday morning is the safest bet. The first bus of the day on weekends does not run until after 7 a.m., and the schoolchildren are absent.

## Why It Survives

Most countries would have removed the fruit by now. They were built for a temporary event, they have no real maintenance budget, and they occupy land that could host cleaner, cheaper aluminum-and-glass shelters of the kind that line every other rural Japanese road. The reason they remain is the same reason a great deal of Japanese eccentricity remains: nobody has bothered to take them down, and a few people have started quietly insisting they should stay.

There is no preservation society. No campaign. The shelters are not registered as anything. They simply continue to exist, repaired when broken, repainted when faded, photographed by a slow trickle of visitors who heard about them from someone who heard about them from someone else.

The 7:42 bus arrives. The commuter inside the watermelon stands up, brushes off, and gets on. The strawberry is empty again until 8:11.
