# Geisha Still Exist in Kyoto. Here's How the Profession Survives in 2026.

A geiko walks down a Gion side street at 5:47 p.m. in flat wooden okobo, white-painted neck, hair pinned with seasonal ornaments. She is going to work. Her work is a banquet at a 200-year-old wooden building where six men in suits will eat kaiseki and listen to her play shamisen for two hours. The bill — drinks, food, her time, the second maiko, the geiko who plays koto — will land somewhere between ¥150,000 and ¥250,000. The men will not touch her. That part keeps surprising people, so let's begin there.

## What a geisha actually is, in 2026

The Japanese word **geisha (芸者)** breaks down literally as "art person." In Kyoto, where the tradition is most concentrated, the senior practitioners are called **geiko (芸妓)** and their apprentices are **maiko (舞妓)**. Across the country, the count has collapsed: roughly **1,000 working geisha nationwide**, down from an estimated **80,000 in the 1920s**. That is not a typo. The profession has shrunk by something like 98% in a century.

Their job is the performance of traditional Japanese arts at private banquets. Training covers Kyoto-style dance (kyomai), shamisen, sometimes koto or tsuzumi drum, classical singing, tea ceremony, ikebana, calligraphy, and — the part Western coverage tends to skip — conversation. A maiko spends years learning how to keep a table of regional politicians, tofu suppliers, and visiting French diplomats talking comfortably for the length of a meal. It is a soft skill priced like a hard one.

They are not, and have never been, prostitutes. We will come back to this because the world refuses to let it go.

## Kyoto's five hanamachi, Tokyo's six, and a long thin line

Kyoto holds the largest preserved geiko/maiko population — about **250 women** working across five **hanamachi** ("flower towns"), the licensed entertainment districts that have operated continuously since the Edo period:

- **Gion Kobu** — the most famous, most expensive, most photographed
- **Gion Higashi** — smaller, attached to Yasaka Shrine
- **Pontocho** — the narrow lantern-lit alley along the Kamogawa
- **Kamishichiken** — the oldest, near Kitano Tenmangu, with its own beer garden in summer
- **Miyagawacho** — the youngest of the five, known for energetic dance performances

Tokyo, against the usual assumption that the tradition is dying everywhere outside Kyoto, still runs **six active hanamachi** with roughly **200 geisha** between them: **Asakusa, Yoshicho, Akasaka, Shinbashi, Mukojima, and Kagurazaka**. Tokyo geisha tend to be older on average, the recruitment harder, the okiya fewer, but the lineage is unbroken. Beyond these two cities — Kanazawa, Niigata, Atami, Fukuoka — a thin scatter of districts keeps perhaps one to fifteen geisha each, often a single retired matriarch and one apprentice she is trying to convince not to quit.

The 1,000 figure is generous. Some industry surveys put the actively-working number closer to 600.

## The five-year apprenticeship and the okiya house

A girl typically enters an **okiya** — the residential geisha house that contracts her training, lodging, kimono, and bookings — between **15 and 20 years old**. The okasan ("mother") who runs the house essentially adopts her for the duration. She becomes a **shikomi** (preparation stage), then **minarai** (watching), then debuts as a **maiko**. The full apprenticeship lasts about **five years**, after which she may "turn the collar" (erikae) and become a full geiko, or leave the profession entirely.

During the maiko years she does not own her kimono (one ensemble can run ¥3-5 million), does not keep her bookings fees directly, and is not generally allowed to date. The okiya recoups the cost of her training, kimono, and accommodation from her earnings; the senior geiko mentor her in technique and etiquette. It is a closed system that has survived because nobody has yet built a better way to teach a 16-year-old to dance Inoue-school kyomai well enough to perform it for paying customers four years later.

The trade-off, obviously, is that it is a very small life chosen very early. The number of girls willing to choose it keeps falling. **Kyoto recruits roughly 10 new maiko per year**, sometimes fewer.

## The ozashiki, the bill, and "ichigensan okotowari"

The performance unit is the **ozashiki**, a banquet held in a private room at a **ryotei** (traditional high-end restaurant) or **ochaya** (teahouse). One or two geiko and a maiko arrive, pour drinks, make conversation, perform a dance or two with shamisen accompaniment, lead drinking games, and leave when the booked time ends. A standard two-hour booking lands between **¥80,000 and ¥200,000 per person**, and that figure must include:

- The teahouse's three traditional **kaiseki courses** (food and venue)
- The geisha/maiko time (hanadai, "flower fee," billed in measured increments)
- Tip and miscellaneous service charges built into the ochaya's settlement

The ochaya bills the customer monthly; trust is the entire mechanism. Which is why, historically, you could not just walk in. The phrase **ichigensan okotowari (一見さんお断り)** — "first-time customers refused" — is not snobbery, it is accounting. Without an introduction from an existing patron or okiya, the teahouse had no way to verify you would actually pay the ¥800,000 invoice that arrived four weeks later. The rule has loosened, especially for foreign visitors booking through licensed cultural agencies, but it has not disappeared.

## How a tourist can actually see one, ethically

You almost certainly cannot book a private ozashiki cold, and you almost certainly should not try to chase one down a Gion alley with a phone camera (Kyoto fined people ¥10,000 for doing this in 2019, then closed entire private alleys to tourists in 2024). The respectful options are well-defined:

- **Gion Corner** (Kyoto) — a one-hour public sampler of seven traditional arts including a maiko dance. About **¥7,000**. Tourist-pitched but legitimate; the performers are real students of the schools.
- **Asakusa Geisha Show** (Tokyo) — monthly public performance by working Asakusa geisha. Around **¥6,000**.
- **Curated okiya/teahouse experiences** via **Inside Kyoto**, **Wabunka**, or **IsshoKey** — small-group ozashiki-style dinners with English interpretation, priced ¥30,000-¥80,000 per person. These are the closest a first-time foreign visitor will get to the real thing without the introduction problem.
- **Public dance festivals** — **Miyako Odori** (Gion Kobu, April), **Kamogawa Odori** (Pontocho, May), and the autumn equivalents are open-ticket performances by full hanamachi rosters.

If you only have a Kyoto afternoon, the festivals are the answer. The money goes to the hanamachi directly.

## The misconception that will not die

Geisha are artists. **Oiran** and **yuujo** were licensed Edo-period courtesans, an entirely separate class who worked in the walled Yoshiwara pleasure quarter and whose profession was made illegal by the **1958 Anti-Prostitution Law**. The two categories overlapped in costume aesthetics — the high okobo, the elaborate hair — which is precisely why Westerners conflate them, and why Japanese viewers do not. Oiran wore their obi tied in the front. Geisha tied it in the back. The distinction was visible from a block away.

The English-language confusion was set in concrete by Arthur Golden's 1997 novel **Memoirs of a Geisha** and the 2005 film, both of which built dramatic tension on the sale of a virginity ritual ("mizuage") that scholars and former geiko have repeatedly disputed as a misrepresentation of an unrelated hair-changing ceremony. Mineko Iwasaki, the geiko Golden interviewed, sued him. The damage to the international image of the profession has been, by every account from inside the industry, severe and lasting.

The other reportable shadow is the **2014 maiko abuse case**, in which a former Kyoto apprentice publicly described coerced drinking and sexual harassment by customers at ozashiki, and inadequate protection by her okiya. The hanamachi associations responded with revised guidelines, age verification on alcohol, and clearer customer conduct rules. It is fair to note: the structural risk in any system where a 17-year-old is paid to entertain wealthy older men is real, and the reforms have been partial rather than total.

## A 2026 honest assessment

The profession is surviving, not thriving. International fascination has produced very few new recruits — being photographed by strangers on the way to work is not, it turns out, a draw. The Kyoto okiya have adapted: **English-language lessons**, **Instagram accounts run by maiko under okiya supervision**, **online shamisen classes for diaspora students**, **livestreamed odori for fans abroad**. A handful of hanamachi now accept short-term cultural exchange placements for foreign students of Japanese dance.

Whether all five Kyoto hanamachi exist in 2050 is genuinely unclear. Whether the art forms themselves survive — the Inoue-school dance, the Kyoto shamisen styles — is a separate and slightly more optimistic question, because those are taught outside the hanamachi too. What is at risk is the specific live institution: women, trained from adolescence, performing in wooden rooms in cities that were built around the assumption they would always be there.

## TL;DR

- About **1,000 geisha nationwide** in 2026, down from 80,000 a century ago
- Kyoto: **5 hanamachi, ~250 geiko/maiko**; Tokyo: **6 hanamachi, ~200 geisha**
- Five-year apprenticeship starting at 15-20, living at an okiya
- Private banquets cost **¥80,000-¥200,000 per person**, historically by introduction only
- Geisha are **not** sex workers; oiran were a separate Edo-era class, abolished 1958
- Tourists: **Gion Corner ¥7,000**, **Asakusa Geisha Show ¥6,000**, or curated dinners via Wabunka / Inside Kyoto / IsshoKey
- Don't chase, don't photograph without consent, don't tip in cash on the street
