# Why Ghibli Museum Tickets Vanish in Minutes (And How to Actually Get In)

Tickets to the Ghibli Museum go on sale on the **10th of each month at 10am JST** — and they're typically gone within **minutes**. The museum caps attendance at roughly **2,400 visitors per day**, which works out to about 60,000 tickets a month against demand that runs many times higher. Here's why — and what you can actually do about it.

If you've ever tried to book a Friday-night izakaya in Kyoto during cherry blossom season, you already know that Japan has a complicated relationship with demand. The Ghibli Museum, tucked into the leafy suburb of Mitaka in west Tokyo, is the most extreme version of that relationship anyone has ever built. It is a small museum — fewer than 2,400 daily visitors allowed — devoted to a single animation studio, surrounded by a park, and run with the quiet conviction that the experience matters more than the money.

The result is a ticketing system that breaks every sensible person who attempts it.

Here is how it actually works, what's inside that nobody photographs (because they can't), and the realistic backup plan if the clock beats you.

## How Tickets Actually Work

The Ghibli Museum doesn't do walk-ups. It doesn't do same-day. It doesn't do "we'll squeeze you in." The rules are inflexible by design, and they're worth memorizing before you try.

- **Four time slots per day**: 10:00, 12:00, 14:00, and 16:00. You pick one. You arrive within 30 minutes of that slot. You stay as long as you like.
- **Tickets go on sale on the 10th of the month**, for the entire following month. So tickets for June go live on May 10th at 10:00 JST.
- **Prices**: ¥1,000 for adults, ¥700 for ages 13–18, ¥400 for ages 7–12, ¥100 for ages 4–6, free for under-4s.
- **The ticket has your name on it.** Non-transferable. The staff will check ID at the gate. If your passport doesn't match the booking, you do not get in. There is no appeal.
- **No re-entry.** Once you leave, you're done for the day.
- **No interior photography.** This is the museum's most-broken rule and the most strictly enforced. They will ask you to delete photos.

Those ~60,000 monthly tickets are spread across all four slots over roughly 25 open days (the museum closes Tuesdays and for maintenance). Demand routinely outstrips supply by an order of magnitude. The domestic Japanese site, run by Lawson, regularly crashes on the 10th. People who actually live in Tokyo set alarms for 9:55 and still miss out.

## The International Ticket Workaround

Here is the part the official website does not advertise loudly: **most foreigners do not buy through the Japanese domestic site.** They buy through **JTB**, Japan's largest travel agency, which gets an allocation of tickets specifically for international visitors.

JTB releases its overseas-ticket batch on the 10th of each month as well, but the system is in English, uses a passport-name booking flow, and tends to hold its allocation slightly longer than the Lawson domestic site. You'll see the JTB allocation called things like "Ghibli Museum e-Ticket" or "Lawson e-tickets via JTB." Both names point to the same thing.

The trick: refresh the JTB ticket page at exactly 10:00 JST on the 10th. Have your passport open in another tab. Have a credit card ready. Do not navigate away. If you see the booking form, type fast — the slot you want will vanish in less than two minutes.

If you fail the first refresh, **keep refreshing for the next four hours**. People do drop tickets when their plans collapse, and JTB occasionally releases small secondary batches throughout the day.

## What's Inside That They Don't Show You

The no-photography rule is the reason the Ghibli Museum is still magical in 2026. You walk in cold. Nobody has spoiled the rooms on TikTok. Here is what you actually find:

- **The giant Totoro at the ticket booth.** Not the real ticket booth — a *fake* one, run by a 2.5-meter-tall plush Totoro. Children scream. Adults take photos. (This one is allowed; it's outside.)
- **The cat-bus playroom.** A walk-in, climb-on, plush cat-bus the size of a small van. The rule: **only children under 12 are allowed inside.** Adults stand at the rope and watch. The rage of childless tourists at this rule is itself a kind of attraction.
- **An original Ghibli short film** — and this is the secret weapon. The basement Saturn Theater plays a rotating set of short films **made exclusively for the museum** that have never been released on Blu-ray, never been streamed, and never will be. Your ticket includes one screening. The film changes every few months. People plan repeat visits around the rotation.
- **The giraffe staircase.** A wrought-iron spiral wrapped around the central atrium, narrow enough that two adults can't pass. It exists for the *feeling* of climbing into a Miyazaki sketchbook.
- **The rooftop garden** with the life-sized **Robot Soldier from Castle in the Sky**, five meters of weathered bronze standing in a roof-garden of moss and wildflowers. This one *is* photographable. It is the single most-Instagrammed object in the building.
- **The "Where a Film Is Born" rooms** — a recreated animator's studio, walls covered floor-to-ceiling with reference sketches, paint-splattered desks, books in twelve languages, and a stuffed pig modeled on Porco Rosso's plane. Most visitors spend 40 minutes in this room alone.

The museum is small. You can technically walk through it in 90 minutes. Most people stay for three hours and leave wishing they'd booked the 12:00 slot instead.

## If You Fail to Get Tickets

You probably will. Here is the realistic backup tier:

1. **Donguri Republic** — Studio Ghibli's official retail chain. Locations in Tokyo Station's Character Street, Shibuya Hikarie, and most major cities. You can't see the films, but you can buy literally every piece of merchandise the museum sells, often with better stock.
2. **Studio Ghibli Park in Aichi.** Opened in 2022, this is the new park — five themed areas (Howl's Castle, Mononoke Village, the Witch's Valley) built into Aichi Expo Park outside Nagoya. It's larger, newer, less famous, and **dramatically easier to book.** Tickets release on the 10th of the month three months ahead (so May 10 for August dates) and they usually last several hours, not seconds.
3. **TOHO Cinemas Ghibli re-releases.** TOHO and Toho Cinemas Roppongi Hills periodically rerun the full Ghibli catalog on the big screen, often with English subtitles. Check the TOHO schedule under "リバイバル" (revival). *Spirited Away* and *Princess Mononoke* return every few years.

## The 2-Day Tokyo Ghibli Pilgrimage

If you actually scored tickets — or you're doing the backup itinerary — here's the route that works:

**Day 1: Tokyo (Mitaka)**
- Morning: **Mitaka Station, JR Chuo Line** from Shinjuku, about 20 minutes. Exit south.
- 10:00 ticket slot at the **Ghibli Museum** (about 15 minutes' walk from the station, or take the yellow Ghibli shuttle bus for ¥210).
- Allow **3 hours** inside.
- Walk south through **Inokashira Park** — about 1 hour. This is the park Miyazaki actually used as reference for several scenes. Swan boats, a small zoo, and a shrine in the middle of the pond.
- Dinner in **Kichijoji**, the next neighborhood over. Harmonica Yokocho's tiny alley bars.

**Day 2: Aichi (Studio Ghibli Park)**
- Morning **shinkansen from Tokyo to Nagoya** (1h 40m on the Nozomi).
- Local Linimo line to Aichikyuhaku-kinen-koen station, about 30 minutes.
- **2 hours minimum** in Studio Ghibli Park. The five areas are spread out; bring walking shoes.
- Return shinkansen to Tokyo same evening (catchable until ~21:00).

A JR Pass pays for itself on this Day 2 round-trip.

## TL;DR

> The Ghibli Museum caps daily attendance at ~2,400 visitors. Tickets release on the 10th of each month and tend to vanish within minutes, with strict ID-checked entry and no photos inside. Foreigners should book through JTB's overseas allocation. If you miss out, Studio Ghibli Park in Aichi is the easier, larger, and dramatically less famous backup. The cat-bus is for children only. Adults rage. That's part of the deal.
