# The Apartments Nobody Talks About — And the Stickers That Quietly Tell You Anyway

In most of Tokyo, an apartment listing tells you the rent, the layout, and the walking distance to the nearest station. In a smaller subset of listings, there is a fourth piece of information, usually buried at the bottom in tiny print: 告知事項あり — "matters to be disclosed." It is the polite, bureaucratic way of saying that someone died in the unit. The rent will be cheaper. The realtor will be vague. And somewhere in the building's stairwell, there may be a small, peeling sticker placed by a previous tenant or a local activist, marking the floor for anyone who knows to look.

This is the world of the *jiko-bukken* (事故物件) — the "incident property" — and it sits at an unusually Japanese intersection of property law, Buddhist folk belief, smartphone data, and discount-hunting renters who do not particularly care about ghosts.

## TL;DR

- *Jiko-bukken* are properties where a tenant died unnaturally — suicide, homicide, undiscovered natural death, or fire.
- Japan's Real Estate Transaction Business Act (宅地建物取引業法) requires landlords to disclose the event, but only to the first new tenant. After that, the obligation often resets.
- 2021 MLIT guidelines tried to standardize a three-year disclosure window for non-violent deaths in rentals. It is guidance, not statute.
- Rent on a confirmed *jiko-bukken* typically runs 20–30% below market.
- A crowdsourced map called Oshima Teru (大島てる) has tracked these properties publicly since 2005. Informal "ghost stickers" — small markers placed on building floors — extend the same warning offline.

## What Counts as a Jiko-bukken

There is no single legal definition. In practice, real estate agents and the courts treat a property as *jiko-bukken* when something has occurred inside that a reasonable future tenant would want to know about. Suicide and homicide are the unambiguous cases. A *kodokushi* — a "lonely death," where an elderly resident dies alone and is discovered only after the body has begun to decompose — also qualifies, particularly if the unit required specialist cleaning. Fatal fires count. A peaceful death from old age, discovered the same day by family, generally does not.

The category exists because Japanese real estate law recognizes the concept of a *shinriteki kashi* (心理的瑕疵), a "psychological defect" in the property. The defect is not structural. The walls are intact, the plumbing works, the rent is collected on time. The defect is in the tenant's head — the knowledge that something happened here, and the discomfort that follows them across the threshold each evening.

## The Disclosure Law, and Its Famous Loophole

The Real Estate Transaction Business Act (宅地建物取引業法, *Takuchi Tatemono Torihiki Gyōhō*) places an obligation on licensed brokers and landlords to disclose material facts to prospective tenants and buyers. Since a psychological defect can lower a property's value and affect a tenant's decision, the courts have repeatedly held that *jiko-bukken* status is a material fact.

For decades, however, the duty has been read narrowly. The landlord must tell the next tenant. Once that tenant signs a lease and moves in — even briefly — many in the industry have treated the disclosure obligation as discharged. Tenant number three, who arrives a year later and asks no questions, may never be told that anything happened.

The result is a well-known piece of industry folklore: the *tanki keiyaku*, or short-term contract, used to "burn off" disclosure. A property changes hands once, on paper, sometimes to a relative or a paid placeholder. By the time it reaches a real renter, the unit is, in the broker's careful phrasing, just an apartment.

## The 2021 MLIT Guidelines

In October 2021, Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT, 国土交通省) issued the first nationwide guidelines attempting to clarify how long the disclosure obligation should last. The headline rule: for rental properties, non-violent deaths — natural causes, *kodokushi* discovered quickly without significant damage to the unit — should be disclosed for approximately three years after the event. Suicides and homicides, the guidelines suggested, should be disclosed without a fixed time limit if the tenant asks.

The guidelines do not have the force of statute. They are, in classic Japanese regulatory style, a strong suggestion. Brokers who ignore them risk civil liability if a tenant later sues. Brokers who follow them have a defensible position in court. In practice, the 2021 framework has tightened disclosure at the margins — and left the central loophole, the "first new tenant" reset, mostly intact.

## The Discount, and Who Takes It

A *jiko-bukken* in central Tokyo generally rents for 20–30% below comparable units in the same building. In a market where a one-room apartment near a major station might list at ¥110,000 a month, that can be ¥30,000 of monthly savings — roughly ¥360,000 a year. For students, foreign workers on short contracts, and anyone indifferent to the building's history, the math is straightforward.

A small specialty brokerage in Tokyo called MARKS (Mark Inc.) has built its business around this indifference. MARKS handles *jiko-bukken* exclusively. Its listings are blunt where ordinary listings are euphemistic: the cause of death, the year, the room, the cleaning that was done. The pitch is not that nothing happened. The pitch is that the customer already knows, has accepted it, and would now like to discuss the rent.

## Oshima Teru: The Public Map

Anyone curious about a specific address can consult Oshima Teru (大島てる), a crowdsourced website launched in 2005 by a Tokyo entrepreneur of the same name. The site overlays a map of Japan with small flame icons. Each flame marks a reported incident — suicide, homicide, fatal fire, *kodokushi* — at that address. Click the flame and a short note appears: year, floor, summary, and the source the contributor used.

Oshima Teru operates in a legal grey zone that Japan has, so far, declined to clarify. Property owners have sued. Most have lost or settled. The site continues to grow, fed by newspaper clippings, police blotters, and tenants who moved out and decided their next neighbor deserved a warning.

## The Sticker Phenomenon

The offline equivalent is older and stranger. In certain apartment buildings — especially the dense, mid-century *manshon* blocks in Tokyo's working-class wards — small adhesive markers occasionally appear in stairwells, by elevator buttons, or on the inside of mailbox doors. Sometimes they are professionally printed: a stylized flame, a single kanji, a website URL. Sometimes they are hand-drawn on packing tape. The convention is loose, but the meaning is consistent: *something happened on this floor*.

The stickers are not endorsed by anyone. They are not removed by anyone either, which is part of the point. Building management could peel them off in an afternoon. The fact that they are usually left in place, quietly accumulating a layer of dust, is its own form of acknowledgment.

## The Buddhist Backdrop

The cultural ground all of this rests on is older than the property law. In the Buddhist tradition that shaped Japanese folk practice, a death that is sudden, violent, lonely, or unmourned can leave the spirit unsettled — a *muenbotoke*, a "wandering soul with no connections," who lingers in the place of death until proper rites are performed. The forty-nine-day mourning cycle, the household altar, the annual visit at *Obon* — these are not theatrical. They are maintenance.

A *jiko-bukken* is, in this framework, a property where the maintenance was interrupted. The discount on the rent is, in one reading, a small fee paid by the new tenant for occupying space that has not yet been spiritually resolved. Few young Tokyoites would phrase it this way out loud. Most would also not be the first to volunteer for the room.
