# Kanazawa Sells Coffee Topped With Edible Gold Leaf for ¥2,500

The first sip tastes like espresso. The second sip tastes like espresso with something faintly metallic stuck to your lip. The metallic thing is a full sheet of 24-karat gold, draped across the crema like a tiny shimmering tablecloth, and it cost you ¥800 at a counter in the old geisha district of Kanazawa. If you upgraded to the latte-and-parfait set, you paid closer to ¥2,500. Either way, you ate gold for breakfast, and a man in a clean apron bowed when you left.

Kanazawa produces roughly **99% of Japan's gold leaf**, according to Ishikawa Prefecture's own industry figures — a near-monopoly so total that the city has stopped pretending to be modest about it. Gold ends up on ice cream, on sake, on sushi, on ramen, on face masks, in bathwater, and, since at least the 2010s, on the foam of your morning coffee. None of this is ironic. Nobody is winking. The shop assistants explain the micron thickness of the leaf with the same tone a French sommelier uses for terroir.

This is the story of how a 16th-century castle decoration order turned into a regional craft economy, then a souvenir industry, then a parfait.

## Why Kanazawa Owns the Gold (and Has Since 1593)

The official origin story is dated and specific. In **1593**, the warlord **Maeda Toshiie**, lord of the Kaga Domain, ordered gold and silver leaf production to begin in Kanazawa to decorate Kanazawa Castle and the temples his family was busy patronising. The Maeda clan were the second-richest daimyo in Japan after the Tokugawa, and they spent their money the way newly rich people always do — on lacquer, on tea ceremony, on gilding things that did not strictly need to be gilded.

For most of the Edo period, the shogunate restricted gold leaf production to the capital. Kanazawa kept making it anyway, partly because the climate cooperated — the cold, humid air of the Hokuriku coast turns out to be unusually good for the beating process — and partly because the Maeda had the money and the political cover to ignore the rules. By the Meiji era the restrictions collapsed, and Kanazawa simply kept going while everyone else stopped. Hence the 99%.

You can see the entire industrial heritage condensed into a few square blocks of the **Higashi-Chaya District**, the preserved teahouse quarter on the east bank of the Asano River. The wooden facades are the same dark lattice they were in 1820. The businesses behind them are, increasingly, selling gold-leaf cheesecake to tourists from Taipei.

## The Chemistry: 0.1 Microns, 100 Sheets to a Millimetre

The reason gold leaf works as a food topping — and the reason it works at all — is that it is almost not there.

A finished sheet of Kanazawa gold leaf, known as **kinpaku**, is about **0.1 micron thick**. Stack **one hundred sheets** and you get a layer roughly **one millimetre** high. To produce that thinness, artisans take a small ingot of 24-karat gold (alloyed with a trace of silver and copper for workability), roll it into a ribbon, cut it into squares, and then beat it. The beating is the part the tour guides like.

The gold squares are interleaved between sheets of specially made **washi paper**, treated with persimmon tannin and aged for months so the fibres are smooth enough not to tear the metal. The stack is then hammered — historically by hand for hours, now mostly by automated mallets supervised by humans — until the gold has spread to the edges of the paper. The washi itself becomes a luxury byproduct: used sheets are sold as oil-blotting papers in cosmetics shops for about ¥400 a pack, which is its own minor racket.

Because the leaf is so thin, it has essentially no flavour and zero nutritional value. Gold is biologically inert; it passes through you unchanged. You are not eating it in any meaningful sense. You are wearing it briefly, on the inside of your mouth, for the camera.

## Where to Actually Drink the Coffee

The gold-leaf coffee experience is concentrated in a four-block radius in Higashi-Chaya, and the operators have organised themselves into a tidy hierarchy.

- **Hakuichi Higashiyama Branch** (箔一 東山店) — *1-15-4 Higashiyama, Kanazawa.* The flagship gold-leaf tourist parlour, run by the city's largest gold-leaf manufacturer. The signature **espresso topped with a full sheet of edible 24k gold leaf is ¥800**, served in a small ceramic cup so the gold dominates the visual frame. Their **gold-leaf soft-serve ice cream** — the photo every visitor takes — is **¥1,500** and arrives with the entire square sheet draped over a cone of vanilla milk soft serve. There is a queue. There is always a queue.
- **Hakuza Hikarigura** (箔座ひかり藏) — *1-13-18 Higashiyama, Kanazawa.* Hakuichi's main competitor. Less of a café, more of a gallery; the back garden contains a small storehouse plated entirely in **platinum-backed gold leaf**, which is free to look at and exists primarily as an Instagram engine. They sell gold-leaf coffee and tea sets in a quieter upstairs room.
- **Kanazawa Yasue Gold Leaf Museum** (金沢市立安江金箔工芸館) — *1-3-10 Higashiyama, Kanazawa.* The actual museum. ¥310 entry. No coffee, but a serious presentation of the tools, the washi, and the artisan lineage. Useful as a hangover from the parfait queue, and a reminder that this was a craft before it was a content opportunity.
- **Kaikaro Teahouse** (懐華樓) — *1-14-8 Higashiyama, Kanazawa.* A working ochaya (geisha house) that opens to the public during the day and serves matcha in a tatami room with a tokonoma alcove tiled in gold leaf. ¥2,500 for the matcha-and-sweet set. The gold here is architectural, not edible, which somehow feels more honest.

The honest assessment, after three rounds of testing: the espresso at Hakuichi is fine. The gold adds nothing to the cup. You are paying for a photograph.

## Gold Sake, Gold Masks, Gold Bathwater

Coffee is only the entry point. The Kanazawa gold-leaf industry has, over the last two decades, located gold on almost every surface a tourist might reasonably touch.

- **Gold-leaf sake.** Sold in branded bottles across the prefecture, with flakes suspended in the liquid. Pours like a snow globe. Tastes like sake.
- **Gold-flecked Yamato Sui mineral water.** A premium bottled water from Hakusan with tiny gold flakes drifting through it. Marketed for weddings and corporate gifts.
- **Gold facial sheet masks.** Sold at every Hakuichi and Hakuza outlet, with cosmetic claims about "metal ion penetration" that are best read with the lights off.
- **Gold-leaf onsen.** A small number of hotels in nearby Yamashiro Onsen offer rotenburo (open-air baths) sprinkled with gold flakes before guests enter. The flakes float, stick briefly to skin, then drain.
- **Gold-leaf wagashi, anko, castella, cheesecake, dorayaki, ramen broth, soba toppings, and at least one regional curry.** The principle is: if you can plate it, you can gild it.

The export market is where the money actually is. Kanazawa gold leaf ends up in **luxury hotel patisseries from Dubai to Singapore**, on Michelin-starred dessert plates in Paris, and in the gold-detailed interiors of a few Gulf-state royal residences. Most of the leaf does not stay in Japan. The parfaits are the marketing.

## Getting There, and What Else Kanazawa Quietly Owns

Kanazawa is genuinely easy to reach. The **Hokuriku Shinkansen** runs from **Tokyo Station to Kanazawa Station in about 2 hours 30 minutes**, with a reserved seat costing roughly **¥14,000** one way. The station itself is worth a few minutes — the wooden Tsuzumi-mon gate at the east exit is one of the more photographed pieces of contemporary Japanese architecture. From the station, a flat ¥200 loop bus reaches Higashi-Chaya in 12 minutes.

The reason to stay overnight is that gold leaf is not the only craft monopoly Kanazawa is sitting on. The Kaga Domain spent three centuries deliberately cultivating a craft economy as a form of soft political insurance — the theory being that a domain too busy making expensive beautiful objects would not look threatening to the shogunate. It worked. The legacy is still there:

- **Kutani porcelain** (九谷焼), with its overglaze enamels in green, yellow, purple, blue and red — produced in southern Ishikawa since the 1650s.
- **Kaga yuzen** (加賀友禅), the hand-painted silk kimono dyeing style, distinguished from Kyoto yuzen by its more naturalistic, slightly melancholy palette.
- **Wajima lacquerware** (輪島塗), from the Noto Peninsula north of the city — built up in dozens of layers over months, traditionally one of the most expensive lacquer traditions in Japan.

Walk Higashi-Chaya in the right order and you can see all of them in an afternoon, then have a gold espresso, then get back on the Shinkansen and be in Tokyo for dinner. This is, more or less, the official Ishikawa Prefecture tourism plan.

## TL;DR

Kanazawa makes about 99% of Japan's gold leaf, a monopoly dating to a 1593 order from the Maeda clan to gild Kanazawa Castle. The leaf is 0.1 microns thick — 100 sheets stack to 1mm — beaten between sheets of washi paper until it becomes almost transparent. In the Higashi-Chaya district, Hakuichi sells espresso topped with a full sheet of edible 24k gold for ¥800 and soft serve for ¥1,500. The gold is tasteless and inert. You are paying for the photograph, and Kanazawa knows it.
