# Gotokuji: The Tokyo Temple Buried Under 10,000 Lucky Cats

Twenty minutes by train from Shibuya, in a quiet residential pocket of Setagaya ward, there is a Buddhist temple whose grounds are slowly being consumed by ceramic cats. They are stacked on wooden shelves, lined along the base of stone lanterns, perched on the steps of the bell tower, and arranged in rows in front of a small wooden hall built for the express purpose of housing them. Every cat is white. Every cat has its right paw raised. Estimates of how many statues are present at any given moment range from **roughly 10,000 on a normal weekday to well over that after a holiday weekend**, and the number rises with every visitor who buys one at the temple office.

This is **Gōtoku-ji (豪徳寺)**, the temple that most Japanese sources credit as the birthplace of the **maneki-neko (招き猫)** — the beckoning cat that now sits in cash registers from Yokohama to Yokosuka, from Honolulu to Houston. The legend behind the cult is small, specific, and dated to the early Edo period. The infrastructure built around it is doing a lot more work than the legend itself.

> **TL;DR.** Gōtoku-ji in Setagaya, Tokyo is one of three temples that claim to be the birthplace of the maneki-neko. A 1633 storm legend involving the feudal lord Ii Naotaka and a beckoning cat made the temple the Ii family's funerary site, and ever since pilgrims have returned thanks for granted wishes by leaving small white cat figurines. Today over 10,000 of them occupy the grounds at any time. Entry is free. The temple is a 10-minute walk from Gōtokuji Station on the Odakyū Line.

## The 1633 Storm That Started It

In the year **1633**, the legend goes, **Ii Naotaka (井伊直孝)** — second daimyō of Hikone Domain and one of the most powerful warlords of the early Tokugawa shogunate — was riding through the Edo countryside in what is now Setagaya. A sudden thunderstorm broke. Naotaka took shelter under a large tree near a poor, ramshackle temple called Kōtoku-in (the temple's earlier name).

Standing at the temple gate was the head priest's cat, and according to the legend, the cat raised its right paw and beckoned the lord inside. Naotaka, intrigued, walked toward the cat. The moment he stepped away from the tree, lightning struck the tree and split it. The cat had saved his life.

Naotaka, grateful, designated the impoverished temple as the **Ii family's funerary temple (bodaiji, 菩提寺)** and funded its rebuilding. The temple was renamed **Gōtoku-ji** in 1659 after Naotaka's posthumous Buddhist name. When the priest's cat died years later, it was buried on the grounds, and a small **Manekibyō (招福堂)** — Hall of Beckoning — was eventually built to enshrine it. Pilgrims who came to the temple to thank the cat for answered prayers began leaving small ceramic cat statues. They never stopped.

## How the Statues Actually Pile Up

The cats are small — most are between **5 cm and 30 cm tall** — and almost all are pure white, with a red collar, gold bell, and right paw raised at roughly head height. (Connoisseurs will tell you that a *right* paw beckons money, a *left* paw beckons customers. Gōtoku-ji is a money-and-fortune temple, so right paws dominate.) They are sold at the temple office in nine standardised sizes:

- **Size 1 (smallest, around 4 cm):** ¥500
- **Mid sizes:** ¥800 to ¥3,000
- **Size 9 (largest, around 30 cm):** ¥5,000

Visitors buy a cat, write a wish on a wooden ema plaque if they wish, and then place the cat anywhere on the temple grounds — on the shelves, beside other cats, in any empty space they can find. There is no formal arrangement. The temple staff periodically rearrange the stacks for crowd flow and to make room for new arrivals, but they do not throw cats away. Older statues are gradually moved to less prominent shelves toward the back of the Manekibyō hall, where they collect the kind of soft grey patina that only Tokyo air can produce.

The visual effect is genuinely strange. From a distance the shelves look like architectural ornament. Up close, the cats are individual: chipped paws, faded gilt, the occasional one tipped sideways. The Edo-period priest's cat, dead for roughly four centuries, has accumulated a small army of mass-produced descendants who all look more or less the same.

## The Birthplace Dispute Nobody Settles

Gōtoku-ji is the most popular candidate, but it is not the only one. The maneki-neko's true origin is genuinely contested, and the major rivals are both in Tokyo:

- **Imado Shrine (今戸神社)** in Asakusa claims the cat originated there in the late Edo period, supposedly invented by a poor old woman who was instructed in a dream to mould clay figurines of her departed cat. Imado's grounds are dominated by paired cat statues, not single ones, and the shrine markets itself heavily to people hoping to attract a romantic partner.
- **Jishōin (自性院)** in Shinjuku stakes its claim on a different fifteenth-century legend involving the warlord Ōta Dōkan, who was reportedly led to victory by a black cat — though Jishōin's cat connection is celebrated only on a single annual day in February.

Folklore researchers, including those cited in the Japanese-language Wikipedia entry on maneki-neko, generally regard the three claims as irreconcilable. None of the three temples has documentary evidence from before the Meiji era; the modern maneki-neko design itself is most reliably traced to **late-nineteenth-century Tokoname and Seto pottery towns**, not to any single temple. What Gōtoku-ji has — and what the other two do not — is the visual density. Ten thousand cats on display will win most arguments about origin, fairly or otherwise.

## The Ii Family Cemetery Next Door

Tourists who come for the cats and then wander deeper into the grounds usually do not expect what is in the back: a large, well-tended **Edo-period samurai cemetery** belonging to the Ii family, the same line that produced **Ii Naosuke (井伊直弼)**, the Tairō (chief minister) of the Tokugawa shogunate assassinated outside Edo Castle's Sakurada Gate in **1860** — the so-called **Sakuradamon Incident** that helped trigger the slide toward the Meiji Restoration.

Naosuke's grave is at Gōtoku-ji. So is Naotaka's. The cemetery contains the tombs of **multiple generations of Ii daimyō** along with their wives and retainers, and several of the funerary monuments are designated **Tangible Cultural Properties of Setagaya Ward**. The juxtaposition is unique to this site: hundreds of ceramic white cats at the front gate, the unmarked weight of late-shogunate political history at the back.

A three-storey pagoda was added to the grounds in **2006**, with carved cats integrated into the bracketing — a modern flourish that ties the two halves of the temple's identity together. It is among the more recent major pagodas built in Tokyo, and one of the only ones with cats in the structural decoration.

## The Setagaya Line and the Maneki-neko Tram

Gōtoku-ji is most easily reached on the **Odakyū Odawara Line**, ten minutes from Shinjuku to Gōtokuji Station, then a ten-minute walk through quiet residential streets. There is a less obvious second option that is more on-brand: the **Tōkyū Setagaya Line (東急世田谷線)**, a small two-car tram that runs through Setagaya from Sangenjaya to Shimo-takaido. The line stops at **Miyanosaka Station**, a five-minute walk from the temple.

Since around 2017 the Setagaya Line has run a permanent **maneki-neko-liveried tram** — exterior wrapped in white with a giant cartoon paw, interior fitted with cat-shaped grab-handles, cat-print upholstery on the seats, and small ceramic cat figurines mounted near the driver's cab. There are usually one or two such trams in rotation on the line at any time; checking the operator's website beforehand is the only way to guarantee catching one. Adult fare is currently **¥160 per ride** (cash or IC card), the same as any other Setagaya Line train.

For travellers who want to chain the visit with other quiet, slightly weird Tokyo neighbourhoods, Gōtoku-ji pairs naturally with [Yanaka's cat village](/blog/yanaka-cat-village/) on the opposite side of central Tokyo, both reachable in a single day with a rail pass.

## Practical Information for Visitors

- **Address:** 2-24-7 Gōtokuji, Setagaya-ku, Tokyo
- **Nearest stations:** Gōtokuji (Odakyū Odawara Line, ~10 min walk); Miyanosaka (Tōkyū Setagaya Line, ~5 min walk)
- **Opening hours:** Grounds open daily, roughly **6:00 to 18:00**; the temple office (where the cats are sold) typically opens **9:00 to 16:30** with a lunch closure around noon
- **Entry fee:** Free
- **Cost of a cat:** ¥500 (smallest) to ¥5,000 (largest)
- **Photography:** Allowed on the grounds; ask before photographing inside any hall
- **Quiet hours:** Visitors are asked to keep voices down, especially around the Ii family cemetery

The temple does not have on-site parking for tourists and discourages tour buses. The neighbourhood is dense and residential, so visitors who arrive in large groups are politely asked to split up before entering the grounds. There are a small number of cafés and a long-running monaka (cat-shaped wafer-sweet) shop on the walk from the station.

For travel planning, Setagaya-area hotel inventory is best searched on [Booking.com's Tokyo Setagaya page](https://www.booking.com/district/jp/tokyo/setagaya.html), and English-language guided cultural tours of the area surface occasionally on [Klook's Tokyo experience listings](https://www.klook.com/en-US/city/28-tokyo-things-to-do/). For visitors who want to bring a cat home as a souvenir rather than buy one at the temple, decent maneki-neko reproductions are available on [Amazon US](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=maneki+neko+ceramic&tag=weirdjapan20-20) under ¥3,000 equivalent.

## Why the Cat Cult Keeps Growing

Maneki-neko sales in Japan have been steady for decades, but Gōtoku-ji's visitor numbers spiked noticeably after **2010**, when foreign-language travel blogs and Instagram surfaced the temple to a Western audience that had only known the cat as a generic Chinatown ornament. The 2015 introduction of Setagaya's cat tram, the 2018 release of multiple high-profile English-language guidebooks featuring the temple, and the post-2022 return of inbound tourism have all pushed traffic upward.

The temple staff have not commercialised in response. The cats are still standardised, the prices are still posted in yen-only, and the office still closes for lunch. No paid photography slots, no English-only tour add-ons, no online ordering. Visitors buy a cat, place it on a shelf, write a wish, and walk back to the station. The system has worked, more or less unchanged, since the year **Ii Naotaka was buried here in 1659**.

If the cat really did beckon the lord away from the tree, the temple has been quietly thanking him for it ever since — at a current rate of several thousand new ceramic descendants per year.
