# 9,000 Men Run Naked Through Okayama. It's a Religion.

The English-language headlines call it the "Naked Festival." The Japanese name — **hadaka matsuri** (裸祭り) — does say "naked." But nobody is naked. They are wearing a fundoshi (a white cotton loincloth folded in a specific Shinto-approved way), split-toed tabi socks, and usually a short happi over-jacket against the February air. In Shinto purification terms, that outfit is liturgical dress. It is closer to a priest's vestments than to swimwear, and reading it any other way is a category error the festival has been quietly correcting for five centuries.

That correction is getting harder to make, because the most famous version of the festival just ended.

## What Hadaka Matsuri Actually Is

Hadaka matsuri are Shinto purification rites in which men strip down to a fundoshi, immerse themselves in cold water, and then physically compete — usually for a sacred object, usually at night, usually in midwinter. The cold is the point. The water is the point. The near-nudity is the point. Stripping away ordinary clothing is a ritualized act of removing pollution (**kegare**, 穢れ), the same logic that underwrites the bath at a shrine entrance and the salt thrown into a sumo ring.

The "naked" in the name refers to the absence of regular street clothes, not the absence of all clothes. In village dialects of the 16th century, **hadaka** simply meant "stripped to the loincloth" — the working state of a fisherman, a porter, a man about to do something physical and consecrated. Reading it as nudity, in the modern Western sense, is what happens when you machine-translate a 500-year-old Shinto vocabulary into a tabloid headline.

Most participants are local men aged 18 to 60. They train for weeks. They fast. They eat together. They pour buckets of icy water over each other in temple courtyards as a warm-up. By the time they enter the main hall, the loincloth has stopped looking like underwear and started looking like a uniform.

## The Flagship: Saidaiji Eyo

The reference point for the entire genre is **Saidaiji Eyo Hadaka Matsuri** in Okayama, held at Saidaiji Kannonin Temple on the third Saturday of February since **1510**. The structure is consistent year to year: thousands of fundoshi-clad men spend the evening in purification rinses, then converge on the main hall just before midnight, where the lights are cut and the priest hurls two **shingi** — small cylindrical sticks of consecrated willow, each about 20 cm long — into the crowd.

Whoever surfaces with a shingi and plants it upright in a tub of rice outside the hall becomes the **fukuotoko** ("lucky man") for the year. Pre-pandemic peak attendance was around **9,000 men** packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the hall, with crowd surges visible from the rafters. It looked, on television, like a single organism breathing.

In **2023**, Saidaiji announced that the men-only fundoshi version of Eyo would not return at scale. The temple cited two structural reasons: the parishioner base — overwhelmingly aging rural men — could no longer field the bodies, and ongoing #MeToo-era scrutiny made the all-male, low-visibility, high-contact format harder to sponsor. Women now participate at Saidaiji, fully clothed, in a reworked ceremony. The shingi are still thrown. The naked rite, after **513 years**, is not.

International press treated this as a Japan-is-changing story. Inside the prefecture it was treated as a funeral.

## The Other Major Festivals (Still Running)

Saidaiji was the loudest, but it was never the only one. The current 2026 calendar still includes:

- **Konomiya Hadaka Matsuri** (Inazawa, Aichi) — held on the 13th day of the lunar new year, this is now the largest surviving naked festival, with roughly **10,000 male participants** mobbing a designated **shin-otoko** (神男, "sacred man") who absorbs the year's misfortunes by being touched.
- **Sumiyoshi Hadaka Matsuri** (Osaka) — a smaller, older rite tied to Sumiyoshi Taisha's purification calendar.
- **Hofu Tenmangu Hadaka-bo Matsuri** (Yamaguchi) — November, with a sake-cask procession; **international observers are explicitly welcomed**, which is rare for this genre.
- **Yotsukaido Naked Festival** (Chiba) — the mud-throwing variant, in which fundoshi-clad men carry children through a paddy of cold mud as a blessing for healthy growth. Visually it is the most shocking. Ritually it is the gentlest.

The unifying mechanic across all of them is the **shin-otoko transfer**: by touching the consecrated man, participants offload a year's accumulated **yaku** (misfortune) onto a single body, who then carries it out of the village. This is scapegoat theology in its original sense — anthropologically related to rites documented from Mesopotamia to the Pyrenees — but in Japan it survived into the smartphone era largely because the temples kept the paperwork.

## The Cost of Doing It

Running into a crowd of 9,000 men in February, wet, in the dark, has predictable consequences. Local hospitals near major hadaka matsuri sites report roughly **50 admissions per festival** in a typical year: hypothermia, lacerations from being stepped on, the occasional fractured rib from a crowd surge. Deaths are rare but not unprecedented. Konomiya keeps a triage tent on site. Saidaiji used to.

These numbers are part of the reason the Saidaiji transition happened when it did. The other reason is demographic. The median age of an active fundoshi parishioner in rural Okayama is now over 55. A festival that requires 9,000 men under 60 cannot be staffed by a town whose under-60 population is 4,000 and shrinking.

## Women's Hadaka Matsuri

The English-language assumption that hadaka matsuri are categorically male is wrong. **Sumida Maeda Mizu Kake Matsuri**, held in Asakusa (Tokyo), features women in white juban and short hakama participating in a water-dousing purification rite that is structurally identical to the male version, minus the fundoshi. There are smaller women's rites in Fukuoka and Iwate. None of them attract Saidaiji-scale crowds, but they have been operating, without fanfare, for decades — which complicates the popular narrative that 2023 was the year women "broke into" the genre.

## The Wider Matsuri Logic

Hadaka matsuri are an extreme node on a much larger graph. Japan runs an estimated **300,000 matsuri** per year. They are the country's primary engine of neighborhood cohesion: the **chonaikai** (block association) organizes the parade, the local izakaya stays open late, the kids who carry the mikoshi this year will organize it in twenty. Remove the festival and the neighborhood loses one of its few unscripted face-to-face institutions. This is why even small towns fight hard to keep a matsuri running long after the math has stopped working — and why Saidaiji's end registered, locally, as civic damage rather than progress.

## Going as a Foreigner: What's Allowed, What Isn't

Foreign attendance is welcomed at the surviving major festivals, with caveats.

- **Konomiya** is the most accessible — Inazawa is 20 minutes from Nagoya by train, the festival is in daylight as well as night, and the surrounding streets become a fairground.
- **Hofu Tenmangu** publishes English signage and treats international observers as guests.
- **Yotsukaido** is an easy day trip from Tokyo.

The etiquette is non-negotiable:

1. **Do not photograph participants' faces without consent.** Many are local civil servants and schoolteachers; the festival is religious, not promotional.
2. **No flash, ever**, near the cold-water rinse zones — the participants are mid-rite.
3. **Stay outside roped-off participant areas.** These are not crowd-control fences; they mark consecrated ground.
4. **Do not wear a fundoshi as a tourist.** Participation requires temple registration, a sponsor, and in most cases parish affiliation.
5. **Bring cash for the temple offering box.** ¥500 to ¥1,000 is appropriate.

## 2026 Status

As of this spring, roughly **six** hadaka matsuri are still operating at full historical scale, down from **more than thirty** in the early 1990s. Konomiya, Hofu, Yotsukaido, Sumiyoshi, and two smaller Tohoku rites are the survivors. The rest have either ended, scaled down to symbolic attendance, or quietly converted to clothed processions.

The pattern is the same one bending every Japanese tradition that depends on a critical mass of young rural men: not opposition, just arithmetic. The fundoshi has not become controversial. There are simply fewer people willing to wear one at 11:50 p.m. in a temple courtyard in February — and the country that invented the rite is, for the first time in 500 years, having to decide which versions are worth subsidizing into the next generation.

## TL;DR

- **Hadaka matsuri** are Shinto purification rites; the fundoshi loincloth is religious dress, not nudity.
- The flagship Saidaiji Eyo (Okayama, since 1510) ended its men-only naked format in 2023 after demographic decline and #MeToo-era pressure.
- Konomiya (Aichi), Hofu Tenmangu (Yamaguchi), Yotsukaido (Chiba), and Sumiyoshi (Osaka) are still running.
- Women's versions exist, most visibly Sumida Maeda Mizu Kake in Asakusa.
- Roughly 50 hospitalizations per major festival per year for hypothermia and crowd injuries.
- Roughly six festivals remain at historical scale in 2026, down from 30+ in the 1990s.
