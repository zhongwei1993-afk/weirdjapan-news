# Hanko: Japan's Personal Stamps Still Outrank Your Signature in 2026

A Tokyo salaryman opens his desk drawer and counts four small cylinders, each about the size of a lipstick. One is plastic, bought for ¥300 at a Daiso. One is cheap acrylic from a vending machine in a Don Quijote, picked up because his bank suddenly asked for a "different" stamp. One is hand-carved from black water-buffalo horn, ordered through a Nihonbashi seal shop, used only for his apartment lease. The fourth is locked in a small wooden box at his parents' house and almost never moves; that one is registered with his ward office.

This is not an antique collection. It is a working set of legal instruments. In 2026, almost five years after the Japanese government promised to abolish the personal seal, the **hanko (判子)** is still how a meaningful portion of Japanese adult life gets signed.

## The Three Tiers Nobody Tells Tourists About

The English word "stamp" lumps together objects that, in Japanese practice, are not interchangeable. The hanko system has three legally distinct tiers, and using the wrong one can void a document.

- **Mitome-in (認印).** The everyday seal. Used to acknowledge receipt of a package, sign for an internal memo, or stamp a routine workplace form. Anyone can buy one off a rack, usually for ¥100 to ¥1,000, with their family name pre-carved in any of the country's roughly 5,000 most common surnames.
- **Ginkō-in (銀行印).** The bank seal. Registered against a specific account at account-opening, used only for transactions on that account. Distinct from the everyday seal by convention, never by law — but a bank will reject a transaction if the imprint does not match the registered design.
- **Jitsuin (実印).** The registered personal seal. The legal-grade one. Each adult is allowed exactly one jitsuin, registered at their city or ward office under their full legal name. A jitsuin paired with an **inkan-shōmei (印鑑証明)** — a paper certificate from the ward office confirming the seal's registration — has the same legal force as a notarised signature in most Western jurisdictions.

The cultural rule, stated almost everywhere but written almost nowhere: never use your jitsuin for anything trivial. If the wax-quality stamp on the back of a parcel-delivery card matches the seal on the deed to your house, you have done something very Japanese and very stupid.

## The Bureaucratic Ritual of Registering a Jitsuin

To register a jitsuin, a resident must visit their ward office in person with the seal itself, government photo ID, and the appropriate form. Officials inspect the seal for compliance — most municipalities require the imprint to be between **8 mm and 25 mm**, contain the registrant's legal name (kanji or romanised, depending on residency status), and avoid mass-produced designs that could be matched at any stationery shop.

The registration is logged in a municipal database, and the resident is issued an **inkan-tōroku-shō (印鑑登録証)** — a small plastic card that authorises the holder to request inkan-shōmei certificates later. To buy a house, sell a car, finalise an inheritance, or formally guarantee a loan, the certificate is requested from the ward office on the day, attached to the contract, and stamped with the registered seal in front of the relevant officer.

Foreign residents on long-term visas can register a jitsuin too, with a seal carved in romanised characters, katakana, or kanji. The standard advice — repeated by every relocation guide and embassy briefing — is to do it shortly after getting an apartment, because the first time a real-estate agency asks for a jitsuin, the registration office is closed for the weekend.

## The Craft, the Materials, and the Prices

The cheap end of the market is industrialised. A pre-carved family-name seal at Daiso or in a Don Quijote vending machine costs **¥100 to ¥500** and is fine for everyday receipts. The Tokyo subway concourse hanko vending machines, which can produce a generic surname seal in under three minutes, were a small viral curiosity in the late 2010s.

The other end is a guild craft. Custom hanko are still hand-carved by certified seal engravers (**inshō-shi, 印章師**) using a small flat chisel and a steady hand, working from a brush-written character design transferred onto the blank in mirror image. Pricing tracks the material:

- **Boxwood (tsuge):** ¥3,000–¥10,000. Standard mid-tier material, warm-coloured, takes a clean line.
- **Water-buffalo horn (kokusuigyū):** ¥10,000–¥40,000. The traditional jitsuin material; dense, glossy, said to age well.
- **Titanium:** ¥15,000–¥50,000. A modern compromise — extremely hard, theoretically immortal, popular with bank-seal buyers since the 2010s.
- **Ivory:** historically the prestige material at **¥80,000 to ¥500,000+**, but trade restrictions under the 1989 CITES treaty and Japan's tightened 2019 domestic ivory rules have shrunk the market dramatically. Reputable shops now either stop selling new ivory or work only with pre-1989 certified stock.

A great deal of this work is concentrated in a single Yamanashi mountain village. **Rokugō (六郷)**, now part of Ichikawamisato Town, has been a hanko-manufacturing centre since the Meiji era, and at its peak supplied an outsized share of the country's seals. The town's surviving carvers — fewer than two hundred remain, according to recent local-government and craft-association reporting — train apprentices in the traditional brush-and-chisel method through the **Yamanashi Inshō Kyōdō Kumiai (Yamanashi Seal Cooperative)**, and the area now markets itself to tourists as "Inshō no Sato" — Seal Village.

## 2020: The Year the Government Tried to Kill the Hanko

In September 2020, Prime Minister Suga's new Cabinet appointed **Kōno Tarō** as Minister for Administrative Reform with an explicit mandate to digitalise government services. Within weeks, Kōno ordered every ministry to audit every administrative procedure for hanko requirements and justify each one.

The numbers that came back were the kind of bureaucratic comedy the country specialises in. The Cabinet Office identified roughly **14,992 administrative procedures** requiring a personal seal across central government. Of those, the audit found that only **about 83 procedures** — roughly **0.55%** — had any genuine identity-verification or legal-weight justification. The rest were rubber-stamp rituals in the literal sense: paperwork that required a hanko because previous paperwork had required a hanko.

By the end of 2021, **roughly 99% of those procedures had been formally abolished**. The COVID-era image that drove the campaign — salarymen commuting into empty offices solely to stamp a form their boss could not stamp from home — became the standard talking point. In September 2021 the new **Digital Agency (デジタル庁)** opened, with hanko abolition as one of its flagship deliverables.

The reform genuinely worked, inside government. The problem is what lay outside it.

## Why the Hanko Refuses to Die

Five years on, the hanko has lost its grip on central-government paperwork and almost no grip anywhere else. The reasons are partly legal, partly cultural, and partly inertial.

**Banking.** Major banks including **Mitsubishi UFJ, SMBC, and Mizuho** introduced hanko-free account options for new customers in 2021, but legacy accounts mostly still carry a registered bank seal, and a meaningful share of branch transactions still ask for it. Regional banks have moved more slowly. Most Japanese adults' main bank relationship remains, in practice, a hanko relationship.

**Real estate.** Apartment leases, mortgages, and freehold purchases continue to require a jitsuin plus an inkan-shōmei certificate. A signature alone is not, in industry practice, treated as sufficient. The Justice Ministry has not changed the underlying registration law.

**Inheritance and family registry.** Probate filings, marriage paperwork that touches the koseki (family registry), and the formal acknowledgement of a will all still route through ward offices that expect a registered seal.

**Corporate Japan.** Companies maintain their own corporate seals — **kaisha-in (会社印)** and a separate **representative seal (daihyōsha-in)** — which are required for incorporation, board resolutions, and most contracts above a certain threshold. The corporate hanko is, if anything, more entrenched than the personal one, because the cost of changing internal compliance procedures across listed companies is enormous and the benefit accrues to nobody specific.

The deeper reason is structural. A hanko is, at heart, a physical object tied to a registration record at a government office. It is auditable, hard to forge against a registered imprint, and culturally legible across every age cohort in a country with one of the world's oldest median populations. Replacing it requires not just a new technology but a new social contract about what "I agree" looks like on paper. That contract is being written, slowly, in 2026 — but it is being written.

## Where to See, Buy, or Try One in Tokyo

For tourists curious about the craft, the densest concentration of seal shops in central Tokyo runs through **Nihonbashi and Ningyōchō**, where several long-established carvers still operate storefronts that will produce a custom seal in romanised characters within a few days. The **Nihonbashi Mitsukoshi** department store stationery floor and the older shops along **Suitengū-dōri** are easy starting points.

For something faster and cheaper, **Tokyu Hands** and **Loft** branches in Shibuya, Shinjuku, and Ikebukuro carry pre-carved Japanese-surname seals in the ¥500–¥3,000 range plus on-site engraving counters that can cut a katakana foreign name in about half an hour. The **Don Quijote** locations in Shibuya and Akihabara still operate the famous hanko vending machines for under ¥1,000, mostly as a novelty purchase. (See our piece on [Japan's vending machine obsession](/blog/japan-vending-machines).)

If you want to make a day trip of it, the **Yamanashi Inshō Kyōdō Kumiai** in Ichikawamisato Town runs occasional workshops and a small museum-style exhibit on the village's history. It's about two hours by train and bus from Tokyo. [Browse Yamanashi day tours on Klook](https://www.klook.com/en-US/search/?query=yamanashi) — most run via Mt. Fuji and Lake Kawaguchiko but a few stop in the inland towns. For an overnight, [hotels near Kawaguchiko on Booking.com](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Kawaguchiko) put you within reach of the seal-craft area the next morning.

If you are setting up to actually live in Japan and need a working seal, [Amazon Japan personal hanko sets](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=hanko+personal+seal+japan&tag=weirdjapan20-20) sell foreign-name kits with ink pads and storage cases in a single order, which is what most relocating professionals do before discovering they will also need a ginkō-in and a jitsuin within a month.

## TL;DR

> Japan still runs a three-tier personal-seal system in 2026. The everyday seal (mitome-in) is a ¥300 plastic cylinder; the registered seal (jitsuin) is a hand-carved object that, paired with a city-hall certificate, carries the legal weight of a notarised signature. The 2020 Kōno Tarō audit found that 14,992 central-government procedures required a hanko and only 83 had any real justification; roughly 99% have since been abolished, and the Digital Agency opened in September 2021. But banks, real estate, inheritance paperwork, and corporate Japan still run on stamps. The hanko did not die; it just retreated to where it was always strongest — the private-sector documents that touch the actual structure of someone's life.

The thing tourists tend to find odd about the hanko system is not that the stamps exist; it is that the people who use them every day do not find them odd. A salaryman with four seals in a desk drawer is not living in a museum. He is doing the same legal-administrative work an American does with a signature line and a notary, only sliced into separate physical objects, each with its own ward-office paperwork, each with its own social rule about when to bring it out.

The country tried to abolish it. The country mostly failed. Both halves of that sentence are equally Japanese.
