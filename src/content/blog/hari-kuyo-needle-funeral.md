# Tokyo Holds an Annual Funeral for Broken Sewing Needles

On the morning of February 8, in a small wooden hall behind Sensoji in Asakusa, women line up holding tiny plastic bags. Inside the bags are bent needles, snapped needles, and needles that have simply grown tired after a few thousand kimono seams. One by one, the women step forward and push each needle, point first, into a quivering brick of soft tofu. A priest chants. The tofu absorbs the metal like a small white grave. No one in the room appears to think this is strange.

This is Hari-kuyō (針供養), the "needle memorial service," and Japan has been doing it for roughly four hundred years.

## A Funeral in a Block of Tofu

The ritual itself is almost embarrassingly simple. Practitioners — historically seamstresses, today a mix of textile professionals, kimono makers, home sewers, and the occasional curious foreigner — bring their broken or worn-out sewing needles to a designated temple or shrine. There, a slab of fresh tofu, or sometimes a slab of konnyaku (a wobbly grey yam jelly), sits on an altar. Each needle is pressed gently into the soft surface and left there for the duration of the service.

The choice of tofu is not whimsical. Sewing needles spend their working lives forced through tough, resistant material: stiff silk, layered cotton, hemp, leather. The logic of Hari-kuyō is that after a lifetime of hard labor, the needles deserve, finally, somewhere soft to rest. A priest reads sutras. The seamstresses pray for the repose of the "needle spirits" that, by the local theological accounting, have been quietly working alongside them all along.

It is held on February 8 in the Kantō region (Tokyo and east), and on December 8 in much of Kansai (Kyoto, Osaka, the west). The split is liturgical, not political, though Japan rarely passes up an opportunity to do the same thing on two different days in two different places.

## Four Hundred Years of Pricked Fingers

The tradition is usually traced to Wakayama Prefecture, where Sendai Daihachi-ji is named in regional records as one of the earliest sites to formalize the practice in the 17th century. From there it spread along trade and pilgrimage routes, attaching itself to Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines that already had a sideline in commemorating objects rather than people.

By the Edo period (1603–1868), the ritual was a fixture of urban life. Sewing was not a hobby. It was infrastructure. Edo-era seamstresses' guilds — small, female-run workshops that produced everything from samurai underclothes to merchant-class wedding kimono — depended on needles the way a carpenter depends on a saw. A snapped needle meant a missed deadline. A whole basket of snapped needles, accumulated over a year, represented a small economic biography of one woman's labor.

It was not only the seamstresses who showed up. Practitioners of ohaguro, the now-extinct cosmetic art of tooth-blackening fashionable among married women and certain courtesans, would bring their own delicate tools to the same services. The pattern was consistent: any trade that lived or died by small metal implements eventually wandered, on the appointed day, toward a temple with a block of tofu waiting on the altar.

## Where It Still Happens

The most accessible service for visitors is Awashima-dō, a small subsidiary hall on the grounds of Sensoji in Asakusa, central Tokyo. Awashima-dō is officially the patron site for women's concerns — fertility, sewing, anything traditionally feminine in the early-modern Japanese imagination — and it has hosted Hari-kuyō continuously for generations. The hall is open to the public, no reservation required, and the service is conducted in the open courtyard.

Other primary venues include:

- **Wakamiya Hachimangū**, in Kamakura, where the service takes place in a wooded shrine compound about an hour south of Tokyo
- **Tōfuku-ji**, a large Zen temple in southeastern Kyoto, which holds the Kansai-calendar service on December 8
- **Senkō-ji**, on the small offshore island of Awashima in Kanagawa, where the rite is performed in a fishing-village setting that has changed remarkably little since the Meiji era

Each site does it slightly differently. Sensoji uses tofu; some rural temples prefer konnyaku, which holds the needles more firmly. Some chant Pure Land sutras; others perform Shinto purifications. The shared element is the soft material, the broken metal, and the unembarrassed sincerity of the participants.

## The Spirits in Your Sewing Box

To understand why anyone would bother holding a funeral for a needle, it helps to know about tsukumogami (付喪神). The doctrine, which sits somewhere between folk Buddhism and Shinto animism, holds that ordinary household objects, after roughly one hundred years of use, acquire a spirit of their own. They develop preferences. They develop grievances. A teapot that has been faithfully heated every morning for a century is, by this account, no longer entirely a teapot.

Most needles do not survive a hundred years. But the same logic applies in miniature: an object that has labored on your behalf — that has, in a literal sense, bled with you — accrues a kind of moral weight. To throw it in the trash is to deny that weight. To deny that weight is to forget gratitude. And the forgetting of gratitude, in the older Japanese reading of these things, is a small but real spiritual failure.

This is the same intuition that underwrites mottainai, the much-quoted Japanese concept usually translated as "what a waste" but more accurately rendered as "a regret at the squandering of a thing's essence." Hari-kuyō is mottainai with a candle and a priest.

## A Quiet Tradition, Quietly Disappearing

The textile industry's own estimates put regular hand-sewing among Japanese women at around five percent of the female population. That is a generous figure; it includes anyone who patches a button or repairs a hem. The number of women who own enough needles to fill a memorial bag is far smaller. Attendance at the major Hari-kuyō services has shrunk accordingly over the past three decades — though, in a peculiarly Japanese twist, the average age of the people attending has risen while the total never quite drops to zero.

The practice survives partly because it has cousins. Japan holds memorial services for an alarming variety of inanimate objects, almost all of them on or near February 8:

- **Ningyō-kuyō** at Iwatsuki, Saitama — the doll-funeral capital of Japan, where unwanted hina dolls and stuffed animals are ritually retired by the thousand
- **Hasami-kuyō** at Iwabuchi-area shrines — a memorial for retired scissors, attended largely by hairdressers and tailors
- **Ningyō-kuyō at Awashima-jinja**, Wakayama — a separate, larger doll service held on the same February 8 date as Hari-kuyō, in which thousands of dolls are loaded onto a small boat and sent out to sea

Beyond the calendar, there are tofu memorials at Iwakura, chopstick-disposal ceremonies at several temples (held on August 4, *hashi no hi*, "chopstick day"), and the occasional fountain-pen memorial organized by stationery companies. The category is broad. The underlying instinct is consistent.

## How to Attend Without Being That Tourist

Foreign visitors are welcome at the major services, and the etiquette is forgiving so long as you arrive in the right register. A few practical notes for the Awashima-dō service at Sensoji, which is the easiest to attend:

- Arrive between roughly 10:00 and 14:00 on February 8. The service runs in waves, not as a single sit-down ceremony.
- You may bring your own broken needle if you have one. A single needle in an envelope is fine. You do not need a basketful.
- A customary offering is ¥100, dropped in the wooden offertory box. Larger offerings are accepted but not expected.
- Dress modestly. This is not a costume event. Avoid kimono cosplay unless you actually wear kimono in daily life.
- Do not photograph the women praying. Photograph the tofu if you must.
- If you do not have a needle, you may still observe quietly from the edge of the courtyard. The priests will not stop you. No one will speak English to you, and that is part of the point.

The service is small. It does not announce itself. It happens, every year, in the gap between New Year's tourism and the cherry-blossom rush, attended by a few dozen women with very specific reasons for being there. You are not the audience. You are, at best, a respectful witness to four hundred years of someone else's gratitude.

## TL;DR

Hari-kuyō is Japan's annual funeral for broken sewing needles, held February 8 in Tokyo and December 8 in Kyoto. Practitioners push used needles into soft tofu or konnyaku at temples like Sensoji's Awashima-dō, thank them for a lifetime of hard work, and pray for the repose of the "needle spirits." It is four hundred years old, slowly fading, and entirely sincere.
