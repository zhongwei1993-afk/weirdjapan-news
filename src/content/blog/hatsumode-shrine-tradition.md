# 13 Million Japanese Visit a Shrine on January 1st. Together.

At 2:58 a.m. on January 1st, the Yamanote line is doing something it never does the other 364 nights of the year: running. Salarymen in heavy coats are pressed against teenagers in puffer jackets, all of whom are pretending this is fine. They are headed to a shrine. So is roughly 80% of the country, according to Cabinet Office surveys — give it a day or three, and most adults will have queued, bowed, clapped twice, and thrown a ¥5 coin into a wooden box for reasons most of them have never been formally asked to explain.

This is hatsumōde (初詣), the year's first shrine visit, and it is the largest coordinated act of unforced ritual participation in the developed world. It does not feel that way from the inside. From the inside, it just feels like January.

## What Hatsumōde Actually Is

Hatsumōde happens during *sanganichi* (三が日), the first three days of the year — January 1, 2, and 3. Technically you can extend it through the 7th, or even the whole month, and shrines will not turn you away. Practically, the bulk of visits land in those first 72 hours, which is why the queues at major sites stretch the length of small countries.

The act itself is straightforward: you visit a shrine (or temple — both work, despite the name implying Shinto exclusivity), you offer a coin, you ring a bell, you bow, you clap, you bow again, and you ask the kami for something reasonable. Health. A passed exam. A child. A job. Nothing too greedy. The kami are not venture capitalists.

What is striking is the scale. Cabinet Office surveys have consistently put participation around 80% of the population, which means hatsumōde is more universally observed than voting, marriage, or finishing high school. It is, statistically speaking, the most Japanese thing the Japanese do.

## The Big Four (and Why They're Always the Big Four)

Visitor counts are tracked, ranked, and discussed in newspapers the way Americans discuss Super Bowl ratings. The top destinations rarely change:

- **Meiji Jingū** (Shibuya/Yoyogi, Tokyo) — roughly **3 million visitors** across the three days. The undisputed champion. A forest in the middle of Tokyo that briefly becomes the densest pedestrian zone in the country.
- **Naritasan Shinshōji** (Chiba) — also around **3 million**. A Buddhist temple, not a shrine, which nobody minds. Conveniently next to the airport, less conveniently mobbed by every Chiba resident at once.
- **Kawasaki Daishi** (Kanagawa) — another **3 million** or so. Famous for traffic-safety amulets, which is why half of Tokyo's drivers feel obligated to show up.
- **Fushimi Inari Taisha** (Kyoto) — about **2.5 million**. The one with the orange torii gates you've seen on Instagram. In January, you cannot see the gates. You can see the back of the person in front of you.

Add Sumiyoshi Taisha (Osaka), Atsuta Jingū (Nagoya), and Dazaifu Tenmangū (Fukuoka), and you have explained where roughly 20 million people spent their New Year holiday.

## The Ritual, Step by Step

The choreography is precise enough that most Japanese learn it without remembering being taught.

1. **Chōzuya** (手水舎). Wash your left hand, then your right, then cup water in your left hand and rinse your mouth. Do not drink from the ladle. Do not put the ladle back in the water. The pandemic killed the mouth-rinsing step at many shrines; some have quietly brought it back, some have not.
2. **Approach the hall.** Walk on the side of the path, not the center — the center is reserved for the kami, who, by tradition, do not appreciate being elbowed.
3. **Ring the bell**, if there is one. Not to summon the kami, exactly. More to announce you're here.
4. **Throw a ¥5 coin** into the offertory box. Five yen is *go-en* (ご縁), a pun on the word for fate, connection, or relationship. ¥45 is "lasting good fortune." ¥500 is the largest coin, and offers nothing larger than itself. ¥10 is *tōen* (遠縁) — distant fate — and is considered bad luck. The Japanese have opinions about coin denominations that would alarm a Swiss banker.
5. **Two bows, two claps, one bow** (二礼二拍手一礼). At temples, no clapping — just bowing and a quiet prayer. Confusing the two is the kind of mistake locals will notice and politely not mention.

## Omikuji, Omamori, and the Annual Burn

After the prayer comes the shopping. *Omikuji* (おみくじ) are paper fortune slips, ¥100 to ¥500, ranging from *dai-kichi* (great blessing) down through several gradations of "things will be tolerable" to *dai-kyō* (great curse). A bad one is traditionally tied to a designated rack or tree, so the bad luck stays at the shrine. We've written about the [knot-tying ritual for bad omikuji](/blog/omikuji-bad-fortune-tied) and why it persists despite being, on paper, profoundly silly.

*Omamori* (お守り) — small cloth amulets for everything from traffic safety to passing entrance exams — operate on a one-year warranty. Last year's amulets are supposed to be returned to the shrine to be burned in *dondo-yaki* (どんど焼き), a mid-January bonfire that disposes of expired luck in a controlled, ceremonial fire. Hanging onto an amulet past its expiration date is not technically a sin. It is, however, a small social embarrassment, like wearing a 2023 conference lanyard to a 2026 conference.

## Tokyo Crowd-Survival Strategy

Meiji Jingū at 3 a.m. on January 1st is an experience. It is not a *good* experience. The shrine opens at midnight, the crowd peaks in the first three hours, and by sunrise the wait to approach the offertory hall can run 90 minutes in temperatures that hover around freezing.

The Tokyo locals who actually enjoy hatsumōde have a tiered fallback system:

- **Yasukuni Jinja** (Chiyoda) — politically loaded, historically heavy, but markedly quieter than Meiji. If you understand the controversy and want a calmer queue, it's there.
- **Hie Jinja** (Akasaka) — beautiful red torii tunnel up the hillside, manageable crowds, salarymen in suits doing this on their lunch break by the 4th.
- **Kanda Myōjin** (Akihabara) — the patron shrine of Tokyo's IT industry, which sells *omamori* for laptops and server uptime. We're not making that up.

There is also the **Tōkyō Jūsha Mairi** — the "Tokyo Ten Lucky Pilgrimage," confusingly comprising twelve shrines (the original ten plus two later additions, because Japan does this). The Edo-era version, *Edo Jūnisha Mairi* (江戸十二社参り), is a more devout day-long circuit. It is exactly the kind of completionist ritual that appeals to people who also collect [tiny temple stamps in Kyoto](/blog/tiny-temple-kyoto).

## The Yamanote Runs All Night, Because of Course It Does

JR East and Tokyo Metro run the Yamanote, Chūō, and several other lines continuously from the evening of December 31st through the morning of January 1st. The first trains do not "start" because the last trains never stopped. Conductors work shifts that would violate labor law in most jurisdictions. Convenience stores at major stations operate on emergency staffing. Everything functions, because the alternative — millions of people stranded at shrines at 4 a.m. — is unthinkable in a country where public infrastructure is essentially a moral commitment.

## The Tradition That Isn't That Old

Here is the part nobody tells you: hatsumōde, as a mass national event, is a Meiji-era invention. Before roughly 1880, you visited your local *ujigami* (氏神) — the kami of your specific neighborhood — and the idea of trekking across Tokyo to Meiji Jingū for a national act of devotion would have been incomprehensible. The modern version was scaffolded by railway companies (who needed off-peak ridership), state Shinto (which needed centralized devotional sites), and an emerging middle class with leisure time.

In other words: a ritual that feels thousand-year-old is, at most, 150 years old. This is a recurring pattern in Japanese tradition. The kimono-clad young women on [Coming of Age Day](/blog/coming-of-age-day-20) and the families dressing children for [Shichi-Go-San](/blog/shichi-go-san-kimono) are also participating in customs that congealed into their current form surprisingly recently. Authenticity in Japan is less about age than about consistency of practice.

## Amazake, Oden, and the Food Stalls

Shrines during hatsumōde are also informal food festivals. *Amazake* (sweet, low-alcohol fermented rice) is distributed free or for ¥100 to warm visitors. *Oden* stalls steam in the cold. Sweet skewered *dango* in three colors. *Yakitori*, *taiyaki*, roasted chestnuts. The food is not better than what you'd find at [Setsubun bean-throwing festivals](/blog/setsubun-bean-throwing) or summer matsuri, but it is significantly more welcome at 4 a.m. in January.

## The Generational Quiet Part

Cabinet Office surveys and private pollsters have noted a steady decline in hatsumōde participation among Japanese under 30 — closer to 60% in recent years, down from the near-universal participation of their parents' generation. The reasons are familiar: weakening religious literacy, urban displacement from ancestral neighborhoods, the rise of New Year travel abroad, and a creeping sense among younger Japanese that ritual without belief is theater. Shrines have responded with Instagram-friendly *omamori*, anime collaborations, and QR-coded fortune slips, which works about as well as you'd expect.

For now, the queues remain mythological. In 30 years, perhaps not.

## How Foreigners Can Participate Without Embarrassing Anyone

Hatsumōde is open to everyone. Nobody will check your passport at the torii. The etiquette is the etiquette regardless of nationality: wash your hands, walk on the side of the path, bow twice, clap twice, bow once, do not photograph the inner sanctum, do not eat or smoke on shrine grounds, do not be loud. Bring small coins. Dress warmly. If you don't know what to ask for, asking for nothing is acceptable — standing quietly is a form of participation.

If you're in Tokyo specifically, skip Meiji Jingū on January 1st. Go on the 4th. Go to Hie. Go to a neighborhood shrine where the priest knows the regulars by name. That's where the actual ritual lives. The 3 million people at Meiji are doing something real, but they are also doing something televised. The quieter shrines are where you'll see hatsumōde the way it works for the people who do it every year without thinking about it.

## TL;DR

Hatsumōde is the year's first shrine visit, performed by roughly 80% of Japanese during the first three days of January. Meiji Jingū, Naritasan, and Kawasaki Daishi each pull around 3 million visitors. The ritual is simple — bow, clap, coin, prayer — but the infrastructure around it (all-night trains, food stalls, omamori turnover, dondo-yaki bonfires) is the real spectacle. The tradition feels ancient and is, in its modern mass form, about 150 years old. Younger Japanese are doing it less. The Yamanote still runs all night.
