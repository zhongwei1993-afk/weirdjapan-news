# Why a Generation of Japanese Men Stopped Dating

In October 2006, a columnist named Maki Fukasawa filed a short piece for Nikkei Business Online describing a new kind of young Japanese man: polite, hobby-oriented, not particularly interested in chasing women, and entirely unbothered by that fact. She called them 草食系男子 — *sōshokukei danshi*, "herbivore-type men." The phrase was meant as light social observation. Within four years it had become a sociological category, a marketing segment, and an unofficial diagnosis for the country's falling birthrate.

**TL;DR:** *Sōshokukei danshi* ("herbivore men") was coined by columnist Maki Fukasawa in 2006 to describe young Japanese men uninterested in aggressive courtship. A 2010 Hakuhodo survey found roughly 75% of men in their 20s and 30s identified with the label. The trend tracks the post-bubble collapse of lifetime employment, falling marriage rates, and Cabinet Office data showing record numbers of unpartnered adults. Twenty years on, the term has stopped being an insult and started looking like a description of the default.

## The Term Nobody Meant to Coin

Fukasawa's column ran in a weekly series about generational shifts in consumer behavior. She had been interviewing young men for marketing research and noticed a pattern: they spoke about romance the way previous generations had spoken about *omiai* — politely, at a slight remove, as if discussing someone else's hobby. They weren't celibate or anti-social. They had female friends, went to mixers, occasionally dated. They simply did not pursue. The hunt, the chase, the *nikushoku* (carnivorous) energy that had defined the bubble-era salaryman was missing.

She borrowed the food metaphor from Japanese dietary classification — herbivores graze, carnivores hunt — and applied it to courtship. The label was sticky because it described something readers already half-recognized. By 2008 it was in mainstream weeklies. By 2009 it had a Wikipedia entry, a publishing micro-industry of self-help books, and a counterpart for women: 肉食女子, *nikushoku joshi*, "carnivore girls," who allegedly did the asking out.

## What the Label Actually Means

In Fukasawa's original framing, *sōshokukei danshi* are not asexual or anti-marriage. They are men who do not organize their identity around sexual conquest. They are comfortable with female friendships that stay platonic. They spend disposable income on themselves — clothes, skincare, gaming setups, single-occupancy travel — rather than on courtship signaling. They tend to live with parents longer, marry later or not at all, and express little anxiety about any of this.

The label is behavioral, not biological. A herbivore man might be in a relationship; he simply did not pursue it with the urgency his father would have. Japanese commentators have noted that the description fits a sizeable chunk of post-bubble men regardless of whether they accept the term. Foreign press coverage often flattened this into "Japanese men have given up on sex," which Fukasawa has spent two decades politely correcting.

## The 2010 Hakuhodo Number

The statistic that made the term inescapable came from the advertising agency Hakuhodo. In 2010, its research arm surveyed Japanese men aged 20–34 and reported that roughly 75% of respondents identified themselves as *sōshokukei* — either fully ("I am a herbivore man") or partially ("I have herbivore tendencies"). The number startled marketers because it implied that the carnivorous salaryman archetype, which had anchored decades of beer, car, and watch advertising, was now a minority position among the men those products targeted.

Subsequent surveys produced similar ranges. A 2011 Lifenet Insurance poll found 61% of unmarried men in their 20s and 30s self-identified as herbivores. A 2013 Meiji Yasuda study found that around a third of single men in their 30s had never dated anyone. The numbers fluctuated with methodology, but the direction was consistent: a generation of men was opting out of the romance script their parents had followed without much debate.

## The Sociology Underneath

The herbivore phenomenon did not emerge from nowhere. It tracks almost perfectly with the collapse of the post-war Japanese employment model. The bubble economy burst in 1991. By the late 1990s, lifetime employment — the implicit contract that had let young men marry early because their salary would only rise — was eroding. Non-regular employment, which paid less and offered no security, climbed from about 20% of the workforce in 1990 to over 38% by the 2010s. Young men in precarious jobs could not credibly promise the household-provider role that traditional courtship still implicitly required.

The Cabinet Office's recurring Survey on the Declining Birthrate captured the downstream effects. By the mid-2010s, roughly 42% of unmarried men aged 18–34 reported they had no sexual experience. The lifetime unmarried rate for men — the percentage who reach age 50 having never married — rose from 5% in 1990 to over 28% by 2020. Cabinet Office researchers consistently linked these numbers to income precarity and shifting attitudes rather than to any failure of desire. The herbivore label became the cultural shorthand for the statistical pattern.

## Carnivore Women and the Inverted Script

The *nikushoku joshi* counterpart appeared in print around 2007 and was popularized by a 2009 book of the same name. The carnivore-woman figure was framed as a young professional who openly initiated dates, was direct about wanting a partner, and treated romance with the same explicit goal-setting she applied to her career. Media coverage tended to present the pair — passive herbivore men, assertive carnivore women — as a gendered inversion of the bubble-era default.

The framing was tidy and largely manufactured. Surveys never found a symmetrical rise in female assertiveness; what they found was women dating later, marrying later, and reporting frustration with male partners who would not state intentions. The carnivore-woman label functioned mostly as a marketing category for women's magazines and a comedic foil on variety television. It has aged less well than the herbivore term, which at least described an observable cohort.

## What It Did to the Economy

Marketers noticed before sociologists did. Japanese beer consumption peaked in 1994 and has fallen almost every year since; per-capita alcohol consumption among men in their 20s is roughly half what it was in 1995. Domestic new-car sales among drivers under 30 dropped so sharply that Toyota ran internal studies in the late 2000s on why young men no longer wanted cars. Luxury watch sales softened. Hostess-club revenue declined. Department stores reorganized men's floors around skincare, slim-cut fashion, and single-serving cookware.

Industries that had assumed male customers wanted to signal status to potential mates had to rebuild their pitches around men buying for themselves. Some adapted — Suntory's *Highball* revival in 2008 was explicitly aimed at men who drank alone at home. Others, like the *kyabakura* (hostess club) sector, simply contracted. The herbivore consumer was real enough to move balance sheets.

## Media, Dating Apps, and the 2020s

For most of the 2010s, herbivore men were a recurring target of variety-show ridicule and *josei jishin* magazine think-pieces. The framing was usually that something had gone wrong with Japanese masculinity and someone — mothers, schools, anime, the economy — was to blame. Dating apps complicated the picture. Pairs, Tapple, and Omiai grew rapidly in the late 2010s, suggesting that low-friction, low-confrontation matching suited the demographic far better than nightclubs ever had. App-mediated marriages now account for a meaningful share of new unions, and the men using these platforms map closely onto the herbivore profile Fukasawa described.

By the mid-2020s, the term has aged into something close to neutral. Younger writers note that "herbivore" describes most of their male peers, which makes it less of a label than a baseline. Government white papers no longer treat the phenomenon as a crisis of masculinity; they treat it as a long-running shift in how Japanese adults form households, or don't. Fukasawa, in recent interviews, has pointed out that she never thought the herbivores were the problem. She thought they were the early signal.

The signal turned out to be loud.
