# Japan Has 1.5 Million 'Hikikomori' — Adults Who Don't Leave Their Rooms

There is a Japanese word for the man in the back bedroom. He is fifty-two. He has not held a job since the 2008 Lehman shock, and he has not left the apartment, except for late-night convenience-store runs, since the spring of 2017. His mother is eighty. She brings him rice on a tray. The word, written 引きこもり, is **hikikomori** — literally "pulling inward, confining." It is not slang. It is a diagnostic category recognized by Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, and as of the Cabinet Office's 2023 nationwide survey, an estimated **1.46 million people** in Japan meet the criteria — roughly **1.2 percent of the population**.

That is not a curiosity. That is a city the size of Munich, indoors.

## What the Word Actually Means

The official Ministry of Health definition is narrower than the foreign press usually suggests. To be classified as hikikomori, a person must have withdrawn from social participation — school, work, friendships outside the household — for **at least six consecutive months**, with the only permitted excursions being solo errands such as a midnight trip to the conbini. They are not agoraphobic in the clinical sense. They are not necessarily depressed, though many are. They are, in the language of the ministry's white papers, "in a state of prolonged social retreat."

The term was coined in 1998 by the psychiatrist **Tamaki Saito**, whose book *Shakaiteki Hikikomori: Owaranai Shishunki* ("Hikikomori: Adolescence Without End") gave the country a vocabulary for something families had been hiding for a decade. Saito's clinical impression at the time was that he was seeing a few thousand cases. The number kept climbing. Twenty-eight years later, it has not stopped.

## The Numbers, and the 8050 Problem

The 2023 Cabinet Office survey, which sampled 30,000 households, produced figures that quietly horrified everyone who read them. Of the 1.46 million estimated hikikomori:

- Roughly **800,000 are aged 40 to 64**.
- Roughly **660,000 are aged 15 to 39**.
- The gender split, historically reported as 70 percent male, has narrowed sharply. The latest survey shows women now make up nearly half of middle-aged cases, in part because female withdrawal had long been dismissed as "housewife" or "caregiver" status and uncounted.

The middle-aged figure is the one the Japanese press calls the **8050 mondai** — the "8050 problem." Eighty-year-old parents on a fixed pension, supporting fifty-year-old children who have not worked in two decades. When the parent dies, the child often has no income, no social-insurance record, no resume to update, and frequently no one who knows they exist. Welfare workers in Saitama and Osaka now train specifically for the apartment-discovery cases: the body of the parent, and the survivor in the next room, who has not spoken to anyone in years.

Onset typically clusters in the late teens and twenties, between ages 15 and 29 — the moment a Japanese life is expected to commit to a university, a company, or a marriage track. Miss that window, the sociologists say, and re-entry becomes structurally difficult in a way Western labor markets do not quite replicate.

## How People Get There

The pathways are not mysterious. They are catalogued. The Cabinet Office asks every respondent to identify the trigger, and the answers come back in roughly the same proportions every cycle:

- **Academic burnout and *futoko*** (school refusal), often beginning in middle school after bullying.
- **Workplace harassment** — *pawahara* (power harassment) and *sekuhara* (sexual harassment) — driving a first-job departure that no second employer will forgive.
- **Post-Lehman layoffs.** A measurable spike of withdrawal in the 2009–2011 cohort, men in their late twenties who never returned to the labor force.
- **Post-COVID re-isolation.** The 2020–2022 lockdowns gave hundreds of thousands of borderline cases permission to stop pretending, and many never re-emerged. The 2023 survey showed a 30 percent increase over 2018.

A subtype the Japanese media calls the **"Mobile NEET"** has emerged: hikikomori who are awake from midnight to dawn, livestreaming, gaming, or working remote piecework, sleeping through the daylight hours when family members are out. They are technically connected — sometimes with thousands of online followers — and technically alone. The smartphone has not solved the problem; in many cases it has made the room more livable, and the door less necessary.

The Cabinet Office puts the economic cost — lost productivity, welfare burden, and unrealized tax revenue — at roughly **¥20 trillion**. That is around 3.5 percent of GDP, sitting quietly in bedrooms.

## The Support Infrastructure Most People Never Hear About

Japan's response, characteristically, has been to build a polite and underfunded system around the problem rather than confront it. It is not nothing.

- **KHJ National Federation of Hikikomori Family Groups**, founded in 1999 by parents who could no longer pretend they were the only ones, now runs chapters in every prefecture.
- The **Tokyo Metropolitan Hikikomori Support Centre** operates a free consultation line at **03-5980-5358**, weekdays, in Japanese.
- Municipal *ibasho* ("a place to be") drop-in cafés — including the well-known **Ibasho Tokyo** — provide low-pressure rooms where withdrawn adults can simply sit near other people without obligation to speak.
- Private outfits offer **"rental sister"** and **"rental brother"** services: trained workers who write letters, then visit, then walk the client to a convenience store, then to a part-time interview, over a process that can take years. (For the lighter, non-clinical cousin of this industry, see our piece on [rent-a-friend services](/blog/rent-a-friend).)

If you are a foreign resident or family member in crisis, the English-language line that mental-health professionals in Tokyo most often recommend is **TELL Japan: 03-5774-0992**. They handle suicide prevention and general support and will refer onward to Japanese-speaking services.

## The Dark Side: "Pull-Out" Operators

Not every intervention is benevolent. A small industry of so-called **hikidashi-ya** ("pull-out" operators) emerged in the 2000s, charging desperate parents millions of yen to forcibly remove an adult child from the home and confine them in a "rehabilitation" facility. The **2016 Ahimu Centre scandal** exposed conditions inside one such operation — restraint, food deprivation, denial of contact with families — and led to a series of Tokyo District Court rulings against the operators, including substantial damages awarded to former detainees. KHJ and the Japan Federation of Bar Associations have since campaigned for tighter regulation. As of 2025, the industry is smaller, but not extinct.

## Is This a Japanese Thing, or a Human Thing?

For two decades, hikikomori was discussed in foreign media as a uniquely Japanese pathology — a symptom of *amae*, of saving face, of the famously rigid salaryman track. (We have written about the more visible end of that track in our [salaryman drunk on the sidewalk](/blog/salaryman-drunk-sidewalk) and [herbivore men](/blog/herbivore-men-soshoku) pieces, both of which sit on the same continuum.)

That framing is collapsing. The **World Health Organization formally recognised hikikomori as an international phenomenon in 2018**. Clinical case reports now exist from **South Korea, Spain, Italy, France, Oman, and the United States**. South Korea has imported the term wholesale (*hikikomori* in katakana, then transliterated into Hangul). Italian psychiatrists have published a domestic prevalence estimate. The pandemic added a Western cohort that fits the criteria precisely.

The honest read is that Japan invented the word, the diagnostic threshold, and the support infrastructure first because Japan noticed first. Whatever combination of post-industrial precarity, digital sufficiency, and shrinking job ladders produces the condition, it is not staying offshore.

## Bridges Back: OriHime and the New Re-Entry Stories

The most quietly hopeful corner of the system is technological. **OriHime**, the telepresence robot developed by Ory Laboratory, lets a hikikomori or bedridden worker remotely operate a small avatar in a Tokyo café — taking orders, chatting with customers, earning a paycheck without leaving the bedroom. We covered the staff of the [DAWN Avatar Robot Café in detail here](/blog/orihime-robot-workers); a meaningful share of OriHime pilots are hikikomori, and several have used the gig as a first rung back into in-person work.

The Japanese press has begun running a different kind of profile, too: the hikikomori-turned-coder employed remotely by a Tokyo startup; the streamer with 200,000 YouTube subscribers who has not been outside since 2019; the manga translator working overnight for a Shueisha subcontractor. These are not redemption arcs in the Hollywood sense. The room is still the room. But the room can now hold a livelihood, and sometimes that is enough to turn the bolt on the door.

## TL;DR

Hikikomori is the official Japanese term for prolonged social withdrawal — six months or more, no school, no work, no friendships. The Cabinet Office's 2023 survey counted 1.46 million people, including 800,000 between 40 and 64, the demographic at the centre of the "8050 problem" of ageing parents supporting middle-aged shut-in children. Causes range from school bullying and workplace harassment to post-Lehman layoffs and post-COVID isolation. The cost is roughly ¥20 trillion. Help exists — KHJ Family Network, the Tokyo helpline 03-5980-5358, TELL Japan 03-5774-0992 in English — but the system is undersized, and the condition is no longer confined to Japan. The WHO recognised it internationally in 2018. The man in the back bedroom is, increasingly, anywhere.
