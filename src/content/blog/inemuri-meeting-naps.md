# Sleeping in Meetings is a Status Symbol in Japan

A junior manager dozes off during the quarterly review. His section chief, mid-PowerPoint, glances over, registers the slumped tie and parted lips, and continues without comment. Nobody nudges him. Nobody jokes about it afterward. On the elevator down, a colleague tells him, approvingly, that he must be working too hard.

This is **inemuri** (居眠り) — literally "sleeping while present" — and it is the closest thing Japanese office culture has to a productivity merit badge. The word itself is constructed from *i* (to be present, to exist in a place) and *nemuri* (sleep). The grammar is doing the cultural lift: you are not absent. You are here. You are simply also asleep.

## The Anthropologist Who Took It Seriously

Most foreign observers who notice inemuri describe it the way you'd describe a man eating cereal on the subway — strange, mildly amusing, filed away. The Austrian-born anthropologist **Brigitte Steger** went the other direction. Her 2003 paper *"Getting Away with Sleep — Social and Cultural Aspects of Dozing in Parliament"* (followed by years of fieldwork at Cambridge) argued that inemuri is not a failure of attention but a performance of one.

Her framework, condensed: in Japan, public sleep is read as evidence of prior effort. If you fell asleep at a meeting, in a lecture, on a bench outside the konbini at 7am, you must have been exhausted, and you must have been exhausted because you were *working*. The nap is the receipt.

Western workplaces read sleep at the desk as a verdict on the work — you found it boring, or you don't take it seriously. Japanese workplaces read sleep at the desk as a verdict on the worker — they took it so seriously they ran themselves into the ground. The image is the same. The story attached to it is inverted.

## The Rules Nobody Writes Down

Inemuri is not "napping at work." There are conditions, and breaking them turns inemuri back into the embarrassing thing foreigners assumed it was.

- **Posture must be upright.** Head up, or only slightly forward. Spine vertical. The body should still look like it is participating in the meeting, even if the mind has clocked out. Slumping across the table, splaying limbs, or curling up is not inemuri — it is just sleep, and it is judged accordingly.
- **You must wake instantly if addressed.** The polite fiction is that you were merely resting your eyes and tracking the conversation by ear. Snapping to attention without confusion is the price of admission.
- **Rank matters.** Senior staff may inemuri freely. A new hire who dozes off in their first month is not demonstrating dedication; they are demonstrating that they have not yet earned the right to look tired.
- **Context matters.** Long meetings, post-lunch lectures, all-hands assemblies — acceptable. Client-facing pitches, one-on-ones with your boss, ceremonies — not acceptable.

Foreigners often miss this and assume the whole society is asleep at the wheel. It is more accurate to say that everyone in the room is silently grading the sleeper on form.

## The Yamanote Line at 11pm

The most public laboratory of inemuri is the Tokyo commuter rail. Step onto the Yamanote Line on a weekday evening and roughly a third of the seated passengers will be unconscious, briefcases balanced on knees, heads gently bobbing into the rhythm of the carriage. The volume peaks around the **last train near 11pm**, when bodies that have been operating on residual caffeine since 7am finally negotiate a ceasefire.

Train inemuri has its own folk choreography. Sleepers somehow wake one stop before their station. Strangers do not lean on strangers (though the salaryman next to you may briefly forget this rule, then reset). Nobody photographs anyone. Conductors do not intervene. The carriage is, by silent agreement, a mobile dormitory with assigned seating.

## Why Everyone Is So Tired

The structural backdrop to all of this is that Japan, as a country, is not sleeping enough.

The Cabinet Office's annual surveys consistently put average sleep duration at roughly **7 hours 22 minutes** on weekdays — the **second-shortest in the OECD**, trailing only South Korea by a few minutes. A frequently cited **Rand Europe** study estimated that chronic insufficient sleep costs the Japanese economy on the order of **¥15 trillion per year** in lost productivity, equivalent to several percentage points of GDP. Whatever the exact figure, the directional finding is uncontroversial: the country is running a national sleep deficit.

The government has, at least on paper, noticed. The **2018 Karoshi-prevention White Paper** (the annual report on death-from-overwork policy) explicitly called out sleep loss as a structural risk and pushed employers to reduce excessive overtime, encourage paid leave, and consider workplace nap programs. **RIKEN** and university sleep researchers have spent the last decade publishing on the "**power nap**" — the now-familiar finding that a 20-minute daytime sleep restores alertness without crossing into sleep inertia.

Inemuri, in this light, is what happens when 7-hour-22-minute averages meet 90-minute meetings. The biology is going to win. The culture has simply decided not to be embarrassed about it.

## Inemuri Has Famous Practitioners

Diet sessions are televised, and Diet members fall asleep in them with some regularity — a recurring genre of news photograph involves a cabinet minister, eyes closed, mid-budget-debate. The opposition objects on cue. Nobody resigns.

Other reliable inemuri habitats:

- **Juku** (cram schools), where middle-schoolers who arrived at 7pm after a full day of regular school slump into their textbooks around 9.
- **Buddhist temples**, where novices in long zazen sessions sometimes lapse — the *keisaku* stick exists for a reason, but seasoned monks are often given a quiet pass.
- **University lectures**, especially the 1pm slot after the cafeteria.
- **Pachinko parlors**, where the sleep is less culturally honored but commercially tolerated.

## The Foreign Comparisons That Don't Quite Fit

Visitors reach instinctively for analogues. None of them line up.

The American **power nap** is privatized and optimized — Google pods, Arianna Huffington TED talks, a small industry of $1,200 sleep chairs. It is something you do alone, behind a door, and frame as a productivity hack. Inemuri is communal and unspoken. You do it in front of everyone, and nobody acknowledges it.

The Spanish **siesta** is scheduled. Shops close. Streets empty. It is a societal pause. Inemuri is opportunistic — it slots into whatever container is available: a 2pm seminar, a 6:40pm Marunouchi-line carriage, the back row of a conference.

The closest Western relative is probably the European university student sleeping through an 8am lecture, except in Japan that student would be the lecturer, and nobody would mind.

## The Corporate Nap Room Era

The interesting recent shift is that Japanese companies have started building inemuri infrastructure on purpose. The HR firm **Nextbeat** runs a "Strategic Sleeping Room" at its Tokyo headquarters with reclining chairs and aromatherapy. **Okuta** (the home renovation company) and **GMO Internet Group** have installed nap spaces. **Ote Securities** and other financial firms have experimented with designated rest areas for traders coming off overnight shifts. The premise is the same one Steger identified two decades ago — you cannot have a workforce running on 6 hours of sleep and pretend the napping isn't happening — but management has decided to allocate square meters to it instead of pretending.

Tokyo's commercial **nap cafés** (see our [nap-café guide](/blog/nap-cafes-tokyo)) have grown alongside this, monetizing what offices won't yet. ¥500 buys you 30 minutes in a reclining chair near Shimbashi. ¥1,500 buys you a private capsule with white noise.

## Social Media Made It Political

Until smartphones, inemuri happened and nobody photographed it. Then everyone had a camera, and a sleeping politician became a tweet within minutes. Diet nappers now trend. Sleeping salarymen on the Chuo Line become Reddit posts. The visual evidence has accumulated faster than the cultural defense of it.

The result is a slow erosion of the unselfconscious normalcy Steger described. Younger Japanese office workers report being more cautious about visible sleep at work — not because the cultural script changed, but because the audience expanded. What used to be a private agreement inside a meeting room is now potentially a viral image. Inemuri survives. It is just slightly more self-aware than it used to be.

## TL;DR

Inemuri is sleep performed as a status symbol: a sign you exhausted yourself working, executed under strict posture rules (head up, body upright, instant wake-up), tolerated in meetings and on trains but not in front of clients, and quietly endorsed by a country that sleeps less than almost anyone else in the OECD. The napping is not the strange part. The strange part is that everyone in the room has agreed, without speaking, exactly how to read it.
