# Japan Still Eats Whale Meat. Here's the Complicated Reason.

A bowl of "yamato-ni" — whale chunks simmered in soy and ginger — sits on a Shibuya counter for ¥1,500. The man eating it is 71. He grew up on the stuff. So did roughly every other Japanese child between 1947 and the mid-1980s, because for nearly forty years whale was the cheapest protein the country had, and the government put it on every school lunch tray in the nation.

That is the short answer to why Japan still has a whaling industry in 2026, even though almost no one buys the meat at retail. The longer answer involves an American general, a UN walkout, a state-owned ship, and roughly 2,000 tons of frozen minke sitting in warehouses nobody quite knows what to do with.

## How whale ended up on the school lunch tray

In 1946, Japan was hungry. Rice rations had collapsed, fishing fleets had been destroyed in the war, and the Allied occupation authorities under General Douglas MacArthur were looking for a fast, cheap source of animal protein. MacArthur authorized two converted US Navy tankers to be refitted as factory whaling ships and sent into the Antarctic. By the next year, whale meat was being shipped back to Japanese ports in industrial quantities.

The Ministry of Education added it to the *kyushoku* school lunch program in 1947. For the next four decades, generations of Japanese children ate fried whale cutlet, whale curry, and whale meatballs on rotation. At its peak in 1962, Japan consumed roughly **233,000 tons of whale meat a year** — more than its consumption of beef at the time.

By the late 1980s, the trajectory had reversed completely. Cheap imported beef from Australia and the United States flooded the market. The International Whaling Commission imposed a global moratorium on commercial whaling in 1986. Japan continued a smaller "scientific research" hunt under an IWC exemption, but the meat itself fell out of favor. Children who had been served whale at school grew into adults who never bought it again.

Annual consumption today sits at roughly **3,000 tons** — a 98% collapse from the 1962 high.

## The IWC walkout and the Article 65 argument

In December 2018, Japan announced it would withdraw from the International Whaling Commission. The exit took effect in June 2019. From that point onward, Japan resumed openly commercial whaling within its own 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone, ending the awkward "research" fiction that had irritated everyone, including the Japanese.

The legal hook is **Article 65 of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea**, which obliges signatories to "work through the appropriate international organizations" on cetacean management — wording Japan reads as permitting national-level management once the IWC stopped functioning as a fisheries body. Critics, including Australia, New Zealand, and the EU, read it the opposite way.

The hunt is now operated by **Kyodo Senpaku**, a private company that holds the sole commercial whaling license. Annual quotas hover around **330 minke whales** plus a few hundred Bryde's and sei whales, producing roughly 2,000 to 3,000 tons of meat per year. In 2024, Kyodo Senpaku launched a new ¥7.5 billion factory ship, the *Kangei Maru*, designed to operate for thirty years — a long-horizon bet on an industry whose actual customers are shrinking by the year.

## Where to actually eat it in Tokyo

For a food that almost no one cooks at home anymore, whale has a surprisingly stable restaurant footprint. Three Tokyo establishments are the usual reference points.

- **Kujira-ya (Shibuya)** — Open since 1953, one block off Dogenzaka. The room looks unchanged since the Showa era, which is part of the appeal. Set menus run **¥1,500 to ¥5,000**, with a lunch teishoku that includes whale cutlet, sashimi, and rice.
- **Geihinkan (Tsukiji)** — A more formal sit-down inside the old Tsukiji outer market area. Full courses land in the **¥4,000 to ¥6,000** range.
- **Whale Restaurant Taruichi (Shinjuku)** — In the Kabukicho side streets. Probably the most ambitious menu of the three, with seasonal preparations and a long sake list.

A typical full course runs **¥3,000 to ¥6,000** and walks through four or five preparations:

- **Sashimi** — Usually red tail meat (*onomi*), served raw with grated ginger and soy. Lean, dense, mildly metallic.
- **Saezuri / jerky** — Tongue and tail tendon, sliced thin and dried. Chewy, snack-like.
- **Yamato-ni** — Cubed whale stewed in soy, mirin, sugar, and ginger. The canned version was a postwar pantry staple.
- **Karaage** — Marinated, battered, deep-fried. The version most diners over sixty remember from school.

None of these restaurants are hard to book. None of them are full on a weekday.

## The "school nostalgia" demographic

The clearest pattern in current whale consumption is age. According to a 2024 Asahi Shimbun survey, only about **1% of Japanese adults eat whale meat once a month or more**. The majority of that 1% is over sixty. They are, almost by definition, the *kyushoku* generation — people for whom whale tastes like elementary school.

This is not a small detail. It is the entire commercial argument. The industry's most reliable customers were created by a government feeding program that ended four decades ago, and that customer base is, demographically, about to thin out fast. Younger Japanese have no particular reason to start eating a food that is more expensive than chicken, harder to find than beef, and politically awkward to be photographed with.

Surveys consistently show that most Japanese adults under 40 have either never eaten whale or ate it once and felt nothing in particular about it. Support for whaling in principle remains moderately high — it polls as a matter of national sovereignty — but support measured in actual purchases at the supermarket is negligible.

## The ethics, presented without a verdict

This is the part where outside coverage usually picks a side. We are going to try not to.

The factual situation is this. The common minke whale, which makes up the bulk of Japan's catch, is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List, with a global population estimated in the hundreds of thousands. Japan's quota of roughly 330 animals per year is, on the population numbers alone, not a conservation threat to the species.

The international objection is not primarily about minke population counts. It is about the precedent of commercial whaling itself, the difficulty of humanely killing a large cetacean at sea, and the concern that legal Japanese whaling weakens enforcement against unregulated hunts elsewhere. The Japanese government's counter-position is that whaling is a regulated food fishery comparable to tuna or bluefin, and that opposition to it is cultural rather than scientific.

It is worth separating two things that international media often blur. **The annual dolphin drive hunt at Taiji**, in Wakayama, is a different operation — different animals, different region, different legal framework, different ethical debate. The footage that travels internationally is almost always from Taiji. The whale meat sold at Kujira-ya in Shibuya is not.

## An industry that does not pencil out

The hardest fact for any neutral observer to ignore is the balance sheet. Kyodo Senpaku's commercial whaling has run at a structural loss for years. The Japanese government provides **subsidies of around ¥5 billion annually** to keep the fleet operating, the new factory ship financed, and the warehouses moving. Reports periodically surface of unsold frozen whale meat being auctioned off below cost, donated to school lunches in a handful of nostalgia-driven municipalities, or used as pet food.

In strict market terms, the consumer demand to support an active whaling industry is not there. What is there is a political commitment — across both ruling-party and opposition lines — to keep the option alive. Whether that commitment outlasts the *kyushoku* generation is the question nobody at the Fisheries Agency wants to answer on the record.

For now, the man at the Shibuya counter finishes his ¥1,500 yamato-ni, pays in coins, and walks back out onto Dogenzaka. The restaurant has been open since 1953. The ship he is, in some indirect sense, keeping afloat is brand new.

## TL;DR

Whale was the dominant cheap protein in postwar Japan from 1947 through the 1980s, served in school lunches and peaking at 233,000 tons a year in 1962. Consumption has since collapsed to about 3,000 tons. Japan left the IWC in 2019 and now hunts roughly 330 minke whales annually under a quota system that, on the species' population numbers, is not a conservation threat — though international opposition to commercial whaling continues. Demand is concentrated among Japanese over sixty who remember the school-lunch version, and the industry survives on government subsidies rather than retail sales.
