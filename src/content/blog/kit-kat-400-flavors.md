# Japan Has Released 400+ Kit Kat Flavors — Including Sake, Wasabi, and Hot-Baked Potato

In most countries, Kit Kat is a chocolate bar you grab at a gas station while paying for fuel. In Japan, it is a **seasonal gift, a regional souvenir, a good-luck charm for high-school exams**, and an entire wing of the confectionery industry.

Nestlé Japan releases roughly **30–50 limited-edition flavors every year**. The historical catalogue now sits at **more than 400** distinct varieties — counting from the first regional limited-edition (strawberry, 2000) and accelerating sharply once the formal seasonal program began around 2003. Some are gorgeous. Some are deranged. One of them tastes, accurately and on purpose, like wasabi.

Here is the full, slightly unhinged map of why Japan turned a British chocolate biscuit into a national obsession — and how you can taste it without booking a flight to Narita.

## 1. The Numbers, Briefly

- **~400+** flavors released since 2000, per Nestlé Japan's own count
- **~30–50** brand-new limited editions every single year
- **Japan** is the **#1 Kit Kat market in the world** by sales — overtaking the UK, where the bar was invented in 1935
- The original Japanese "weird" flavor was **strawberry**, launched in 2000 as a Hokkaido regional exclusive
- The flavor that broke the internet was **sake (日本酒)**, released in 2016, containing 0.8% actual sake powder
- A single dedicated shop, **Kit Kat Chocolatory** in Ginza, has sold premium Kit Kats designed by pastry chef Yasumasa Takagi since 2014, with bars going for ¥400–¥900 each

## 2. Why Japan Went Kit Kat Crazy: The "Kitto Katsu" Coincidence

The whole empire rests on a pun.

In Japanese, "Kit Kat" sounds almost exactly like **"kitto katsu"** (きっと勝つ) — which translates to **"you will surely win."** Nestlé didn't engineer this. They just got lucky in the early 2000s when high-school students started giving each other Kit Kats before university entrance exams as a good-luck token.

Nestlé Japan, to their credit, immediately noticed. By 2009 they were partnering with **Japan Post** to let parents mail Kit Kats as official "good luck" exam-season postcards. They started printing **encouragement messages** on the back of the wrapper — small spaces for a mother to write "頑張って" ("do your best") to a kid sitting the brutal *centre shiken* entrance test.

Once Kit Kat became a **gift object** rather than a candy, the rules changed. Gifts need **variety, seasonality, and regional pride**. Japan already had a giant cultural template for this — *omiyage* (お土産), the obligation to bring back a regional specialty for your coworkers when you travel. Kit Kat slotted into that ecosystem perfectly.

That's the unlock. Every weird flavor that follows is downstream of one accidental pun.

## 3. The Sake Bar That Changed Everything

The pivot point most food writers point to is the **2016 sake bar** that went viral overseas. It was the moment Nestlé Japan signaled that **nothing was off-limits**. If a chocolate bar could contain rice wine, it could contain anything. Within five years they had launched bars flavored after pickled plums, soy sauce, edamame, and — yes — wasabi.

Once the brand decided it was a **luxury seasonal collector's item** rather than a snack, the marketing team essentially became chefs.

## 4. Fifteen-Plus Weird Flavors That Actually Exist

A non-exhaustive tour of the catalogue, ranging from delicious to "why":

- **Sake (日本酒)** — White chocolate infused with 0.8% sake powder. Tastes faintly boozy, mostly sweet. The icon of the lineup.
- **Wasabi** — White chocolate with real wasabi powder from Shizuoka. The heat sneaks up on you about three seconds in.
- **Matcha (抹茶)** — The single best-selling regional flavor. Made with Uji matcha from Kyoto. Genuinely good.
- **Hojicha (ほうじ茶)** — Roasted green tea. Tastes like toasted hay, in a good way.
- **Purple Sweet Potato (紅芋)** — An Okinawa exclusive. Surprisingly creamy.
- **Raspberry** — One of the better fruit ones. Tart, sharp, properly pink.
- **Ginger Ale** — Yes. Fizzy candy plus white chocolate. Divisive.
- **Hot-Baked Apple Pie** — Designed to be put in the toaster oven for two minutes. Smells like a bakery.
- **Hot-Baked Pudding** — Same toaster trick. Caramelizes on top.
- **Hot-Baked Sweet Potato** — Tastes startlingly like a real *yaki-imo* street cart.
- **Strawberry Cheesecake** — A Yokohama regional. Tangy, dense, weirdly accurate.
- **Yuzu Kosho** — Citrus peel and chili paste. A spicy dessert experiment.
- **Sakura (Cherry Blossom)** — Spring seasonal. Tastes like a floral pickle.
- **Edamame Soybean** — Sounds wrong. Tastes like nutty white chocolate.
- **Soy Sauce** — A 2008 release. More caramel than salty. Better than it sounds.
- **Plum (Ume)** — Sour pickled-plum sweetness. Hard pass for most foreigners.
- **Hokkaido Melon with Mascarpone** — A Hokkaido exclusive. Tastes like the inside of a fancy parfait.
- **Shinshu Apple** — A Nagano regional. Crisp, clean, the easiest sell.
- **Cough Drop** — Yes. A real product. Marketed for winter. Tastes like Vicks.
- **Ruby Chocolate** — A 2018 launch using actual ruby cocoa beans. Naturally pink, naturally tart.

That's twenty without breaking a sweat. The full historical list runs four times longer.

## 6. Regional Exclusives: The Omiyage Trick

This is the smartest part of the whole strategy.

Most Kit Kat flavors are sold **only in the region they're themed after**. The purple sweet potato bar lives in Okinawa. The Shinshu apple lives in Nagano. The Yokohama strawberry cheesecake lives in Yokohama. If you want them, you have to go there — or know someone who did.

That makes every regional Kit Kat a legitimate *omiyage* — a souvenir that proves you actually traveled. Japanese tourists collect them obsessively. So do foreigners, which is why airport gift shops in **Narita, Haneda, and Kansai** stock the "best of" regional sets in handsome cardboard boxes that look more like wine packaging than candy.

The result: Kit Kat is now part of Japan's domestic tourism economy. There are people who plan day trips around obtaining a specific bar.

## 7. The Kit Kat Chocolatory: When Candy Becomes Patisserie

In 2014, Nestlé Japan opened **Kit Kat Chocolatory** inside Tokyo's Ikebukuro Seibu department store. The chef in charge is **Yasumasa Takagi**, a Michelin-starred pastry chef.

The bars sold there are not candy in any meaningful sense. They are **hand-decorated, individually wrapped, ¥400–¥900 confectionery objects** designed to be given as formal gifts. Flavors have included passionfruit, sublime bitter, sublime gold, butter, and a Champagne-flavored bar that briefly required age verification.

There are now nine Chocolatory locations across Japan. None outside it. This is on purpose.
