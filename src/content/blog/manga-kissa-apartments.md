# Tokyo's Manga Cafés Are Doubling as Apartments. By Choice.

A booth measures roughly 1.5 by 2 meters. Inside: a reclining chair, a monitor, a power outlet, and unlimited soft drinks from the hallway machine. The door, at the premium tier, locks. For somewhere between ¥1,500 and ¥2,500 you can stay until morning. For around ¥35,000 a month, on the right plan, you never have to leave.

This is the manga kissa (マンガ喫茶) — Japan's 24-hour internet and comic café. Most foreign visitors discover it by accident, somewhere around 1 a.m. after missing the last train. A non-trivial number of Japanese residents discover it as the cheapest roof in Tokyo and then never quite move out.

## What a Manga Kissa Actually Is

The format is older than most people assume. It started in the 1980s as a place to read comics over coffee, then quietly absorbed PCs, then absorbed the internet, then absorbed showers, food menus, and overnight rates. By the late 2000s the manga kissa had become a strange composite — half library, half hotel, half office, all open 24 hours and lit like a dentist's waiting room at 3 a.m.

The big chains today are a small, recognizable cast. **Manboo** runs more than 200 locations and is the closest thing the category has to a McDonald's. **Gera Gera**, **DiCE**, and **Bagus** trade on slightly nicer interiors. **Mediacafe Popeye**, the largest premium operator, runs flagship branches with private rooms, female-only floors, and showers that don't require a coin. Pricing is broadly consistent: about **¥350 to ¥600 per hour**, **¥1,500 to ¥2,500 for an overnight "night pack,"** and **¥30,000 to ¥50,000 for a monthly pass** at chains that offer one. Cash still works everywhere. So does an IC card.

## Inside the Booth

You check in at a counter, hand over an ID, and pick a seat type. The cheapest is "open" — a chair in a partitioned row, no door. One tier up is a "private booth," fully walled but with a curtain. The premium tier is a **flat-floor room** with a reclining seat, a lockable door, and enough space to lie down. That last one is what people mean when they say they "lived in a manga café."

Amenities are standardized to a degree that feels almost regulatory. Free soft drinks. A wall of around 30,000 manga volumes. A PC with internet and the relevant subscription services. A food menu that runs from curry rice to soft-serve. **Showers cost about ¥200** and are available on a sign-up sheet. Some branches still allow smoking on designated floors, which remains one of the last indoor smoking refuges in Tokyo.

It is not luxurious. It is not meant to be. It is a chair, a door, and a flat rate — and in a city where a one-room apartment in the 23 wards starts at around ¥80,000 a month before deposit, key money, and a guarantor, those three things add up to a category of housing.

## The Net Café Refugees

The Japanese term is **ネットカフェ難民** — *net cafe nanmin*, "net café refugees." It entered the news cycle in the mid-2000s after journalists started noticing the same regulars in the same booths at the same chains, week after week, with their belongings stored in coin lockers down the street.

The numbers have been measured, carefully, only once at scale. A **2017 survey by the Tokyo Metropolitan Government** estimated that roughly **4,000 people in Tokyo were living in net cafés full-time** — not occasionally, not between apartments, but as their primary residence. About **75% were men**. Roughly **half were dispatch workers, day laborers, or part-timers** whose income was real but too irregular to clear the paperwork for a lease. Around **30%** had landed there after losing a job, a marriage, or both. The rest were a mix the survey couldn't cleanly categorize.

**Mediacafe Popeye in Akihabara** is the branch most often named in coverage — partly because it is large, partly because Akihabara has cheap late-night food, partly because journalists know where to find it. Staff at chains across Tokyo know the regulars by face and generally leave them alone.

The legal status is a grey zone rather than a crime. A manga kissa is not a residence. It cannot issue the *jūminhyō* address required for most full-time employment contracts, national health insurance enrollment, or voter registration. People who live in booths can technically vote and see a doctor, but the friction is high enough that many don't. The booth solves the problem of where to sleep tonight. It does not solve the problem of being legible to the state tomorrow.

The honest reading is that the manga kissa is doing work the rest of the system has declined to do. It is not housing policy. It is what fills in when housing policy doesn't reach.

## COVID-19 and the 2020 Rupture

In April 2020, under Japan's first state of emergency, Tokyo asked net cafés to close. They mostly did. An estimated **4,000-plus people** lost their address overnight.

The Tokyo Metropolitan Government opened emergency hotel and business-hotel shelters for displaced net café residents — a response that was, by the standards of Japanese welfare administration, fast. It also exposed something the 2017 survey had only implied: the people in the booths were not a fringe. They were a workforce. When the booths closed, the people inside still had jobs to commute to, in many cases, and now nowhere to shower before their shift.

The chains reopened. Most of the residents returned. The shelter program wound down. The category of housing reverted to being technically not housing.

## Manga Kissa vs. Capsule, Vs. Shelter, Vs. Park

It helps to place the manga kissa on a spectrum. A **capsule hotel** runs about ¥3,000 to ¥5,000 a night and is more comfortable but enforces a checkout time and a one-night cap on most stays; see our guides on the [capsule hotel in Tokyo](/blog/capsule-hotel-tokyo) and the [best capsule hotels in Tokyo](/blog/best-capsule-hotels-tokyo). A **nap café** is a daytime-only version of the same idea — we covered the [nap cafés of Tokyo](/blog/nap-cafes-tokyo) separately. None of these are residences. All of them are used as one by somebody.

Further down the spectrum sit the traditional homeless infrastructures Tokyo still maintains and largely tries not to discuss: the **doya** (cheap lodging houses) of **Sanya**, the **Yamaya** support organizations working in that neighborhood, and the **Ueno Park** encampments that periodically appear and get cleared. The manga kissa population overlaps with these worlds but is generally younger, more digitally connected, and more likely to be in active employment. It is, to a large extent, the version of homelessness that has a smartphone and a shift starting at 6 a.m.

Cultural visibility has lagged the reality. The 2006 anime **"Welcome to the N.H.K."** introduced the net café refugee to a mainstream audience and gave the term its first wave of recognition outside the news pages. NHK itself has run documentaries. The format also sits inside a broader cluster of Japanese social phenomena that get covered in isolation but rhyme with each other — the [1.5 million hikikomori](/blog/hikikomori-1-5-million), the [sōshoku-kei "herbivore men"](/blog/herbivore-men-soshoku), the [salaryman asleep on the sidewalk](/blog/salaryman-drunk-sidewalk). Each is a different exit ramp from the same expressway.

## Why Tourists Keep Discovering the Place

Most foreign visitors find the manga kissa through the back door: a missed last train, a fully booked hotel during cherry blossom season, or a guidebook footnote about "Japan's weirdest hotels." It is genuinely useful in this role. No reservation required. Walk-in until full. About ¥2,000 for the night. A shower in the morning. A door that locks, if you pay for it.

The current 2026 picture is roughly stable. Chain count is down slightly from the 2018 peak, in part because inbound tourism has soaked up cheaper hotel rooms and in part because some flagship branches converted to higher-margin private-room layouts during the pandemic. Manboo, Mediacafe Popeye, and Gera Gera remain the easy defaults for a tourist who wants to try one. The same branches remain the easy defaults for somebody who has been there for nine months.

## TL;DR

Japan's 24-hour manga cafés are cheap, private, and unselfconsciously useful — a hotel for tourists who missed the train, a workspace for freelancers, and, for around 4,000 people in Tokyo, a quiet substitute for an apartment they can't get a lease on. The chairs recline. The doors lock. The system around them mostly looks away.
