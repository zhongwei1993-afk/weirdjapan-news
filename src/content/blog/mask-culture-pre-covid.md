# Japanese Wore Surgical Masks Daily Long Before COVID

In March 2020, foreign journalists arrived in Tokyo expecting to document a city panicking into surgical masks. They found a city that had already been wearing them for a century. The mask was not a pandemic response in Japan. It was Tuesday.

The pictures the Western press kept running — commuters in white pleated masks on the Yamanote line, schoolchildren in matching hygiene gear, salarymen sneezing politely into fabric — were, in Japan, the visual equivalent of pictures of people carrying umbrellas in the rain. Unremarkable. Pre-existing. Older than most of the people in the photo.

## A Habit Older Than the Word "Pandemic"

The Japanese mask habit did not begin in 2020, or 2009 with H1N1, or even in the SARS era. It began in 1918, with the global Spanish flu outbreak that killed an estimated 450,000 people in Japan alone. The Naimusho — the Home Ministry — ran a national public health campaign with posters reading "マスクをかけぬ命知らず" ("Reckless is the one who wears no mask"). The campaign distributed cloth masks at train stations, schools and government offices. It was one of the first state-coordinated mask drives anywhere on earth.

What is unusual is not that Japan did this. Many countries did, briefly. What is unusual is that Japan never quite stopped.

When the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake leveled Tokyo and Yokohama, the resulting fires burned for three days and coated the survivors in ash. Dust masks — a slightly heavier industrial cousin of the surgical kind — became a daily necessity for months. Newspaper photos from the reconstruction show entire neighborhoods of masked residents picking through rubble. A second flu wave in 1934 then re-normalized the medical mask in particular, this time backed by a more developed domestic textile industry that could mass-produce gauze. By the time Japan entered the postwar period, the cultural reflex — discomfort, dust, illness, put on a mask — was already encoded.

## Then the Forestry Agency Made Everyone Allergic

The postwar chapter is where the story turns from public health into self-inflicted ecological comedy.

In the 1950s, the Forestry Agency (林野庁) launched a national reforestation program to rebuild a country that had spent the war years burning its own timber. The chosen tree was sugi, Japanese cedar — fast-growing, straight-trunked, ideal for construction. Millions of hectares were planted. By the time the trees matured in the 1970s and 80s, cheap imported lumber had collapsed the domestic timber market, and the cedar forests were left standing, unharvested, releasing pollen on an industrial scale.

The result is kafunsho — cedar hay fever — which now affects roughly 30% of the Japanese population each spring, according to Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare estimates. The sneeze season runs from early February through April, peaks during the cherry blossom photo-ops nobody can enjoy through watering eyes, and converts a third of the country into mandatory mask-wearers for roughly ten weeks a year. The masks predate kafunsho. Kafunsho simply guaranteed that the habit would never lapse.

## The Politeness Mask

By the 1990s, a second logic had layered onto the medical one. Wearing a mask while sick stopped being a personal protection measure and became an act of social courtesy: I have a cold, I am protecting you. Companies began to expect it. Showing up to the office with a runny nose and no mask is, even now, mildly rude — the equivalent of coughing into a colleague's lunch.

This is a small but important inversion. In most of the West, masks are framed as something the well wear to protect themselves from the sick. In Japan, masks are something the sick wear to protect everyone else. The verb is the same. The grammar of obligation is reversed.

It is also why pre-COVID Japan never had to argue about masks. There was nothing to debate. The mask was a piece of etiquette equipment, like the handkerchief used to dry one's hands in a bathroom that has not provided towels, or the small bow offered when accepting change.

## Datemasuku: The Mask as Outfit

Around 2012, journalists and pollsters began noticing a new category of wearer — young women masking in perfect health, often in winter, indoors, on dates. The Japanese press coined the term datemasuku (伊達マスク), literally "fashion mask" or "mask for show." Surveys by the cosmetics industry attributed the trend to a stack of reasons that all made sense individually: skipping makeup, hiding a blemish, staying warm, avoiding eye contact on the train, looking smaller-faced (a mask covers the jawline), and — most quietly — anonymity in a country with limited personal space.

For Gen Z women in particular, the mask functioned as a kind of partial veil. It allowed a person to be in public without being fully on display. Mental health researchers later began describing this as kao pantsu (顔パンツ) — "face underwear" — the idea that the lower face had become a private region one did not necessarily wish to expose to strangers. The trend was strong enough that brands began designing for it. Pitta Mask launched colored polyurethane masks marketed explicitly as accessories. Unicharm's Cho Kaiteki line emphasized cheek-slimming silhouettes. Aibo and other smaller labels chased the same market.

By 2019, a Japanese convenience store carried at least four mask brands, in sizes for adults, women specifically, and children, at around ¥30 to ¥80 per single mask. The mask aisle was usually next to the pocket tissues. Nobody thought this was strange because nobody had ever thought it was strange.

## Then COVID Arrived and Broke the Norm

This is the part that confuses outside observers. COVID was supposed to entrench Japanese mask culture forever. Instead, it appears to have ended it.

For three years between 2020 and 2023, masking in Japan was — for the first time — explicitly recommended by the government. When the recommendation was lifted in March 2023, something unexpected happened. The percentage of people masking in public, which had been a steady cultural baseline of roughly 30–40% during cold and pollen seasons for decades, began to drop. By late 2024, multiple surveys put daily mask use among under-30s at historic lows. Office workers stopped. Students stopped. Even kafunsho sufferers were trying nasal sprays first.

The most plausible explanation, offered in editorials in Asahi Shimbun and elsewhere, is that COVID accidentally re-coded the mask. For a century, the mask had meant "I am being considerate." For three years, it meant "the government has told me to." Once the second meaning attached, the first one quietly drained out. A piece of personal etiquette became a piece of policy, and policies, unlike etiquette, can be revoked.

The contrast with the Western mask debate is almost too neat to be real. In the United States and parts of Europe, the mask became politicized — a symbol of compliance or resistance, depending on the wearer. In Japan, it became boring. And boring, in a country that quietly elevated mask-wearing to a hundred-year national habit, may turn out to be more lethal to the practice than any backlash.

The masks have not vanished. They are still at every konbini, still ¥30, still sold in flesh-pink for women and white for men and tiny pleated ones for kindergarteners. The pollen still falls. The cedar trees the Forestry Agency planted in 1955 are still standing, still untouched, still producing yellow clouds visible from satellites every March. The infrastructure is intact. The reflex is what shifted.

## TL;DR

Japan's mask culture started with the 1918 Spanish flu, was reinforced by earthquake dust in 1923 and another flu wave in 1934, and then was permanently locked in by the Forestry Agency planting cedar forests in the 1950s that now give a third of the population spring hay fever. Add postwar politeness norms (sick people mask to protect others) and a 2012 fashion trend (datemasuku) and you get a country that wore surgical masks daily for a century without thinking about it. COVID, by turning the mask from etiquette into policy, paradoxically broke the habit.
