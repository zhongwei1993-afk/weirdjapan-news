# Why Matcha is in Literally Everything in Japan, Including KFC

Walk into any Japanese convenience store on a Tuesday morning and you can buy: a matcha Kit Kat, a matcha onigiri, a matcha-flavored Cup Noodle, a matcha latte in a can, a matcha eclair, a matcha mochi, a matcha beer, and — if the season is right — a matcha-flavored Pepsi. None of this is marketed as novelty. It is simply the shelf.

This is the part foreigners tend to miss. Matcha in Japan isn't a trend. It is a 12th-century imported Buddhist ritual that quietly absorbed the entire industrial food system, then bounced back out as a global wellness export. Somewhere along the way, it ended up dusted on a Cup Noodle.

## From Tencha Leaves to a Granite Mill

Before anything else, the actual product. Matcha is finely powdered green tea, made from a specific cultivar called **tencha** — leaves grown under bamboo or synthetic shade cloth for roughly three weeks before harvest. Starving the plant of sunlight forces it to overproduce chlorophyll and amino acids, which is why proper matcha is electric green instead of the army-fatigues color of cheap powder.

Tencha is hand-picked (for the good stuff) in a short window in **May**, steamed, de-veined, de-stemmed, and dried into rough flakes. Those flakes are then milled — slowly, in **traditional granite stone mills** — at a rate of about 30 to 40 grams per hour. That is not a typo. A single stone mill produces enough matcha for roughly twenty bowls of tea per hour, which is why ceremonial matcha costs what it costs. Try to grind it faster and the friction heat scorches the leaf, turning it bitter and yellow.

Ceremonial-grade matcha runs around **¥10,000 per kilogram** at the producer level. Culinary grade — the stuff in your latte powder — sits closer to **¥1,500 per kilogram**. Both are technically matcha. Only one of them was ever meant to be drunk straight.

## A Buddhist Monk's Souvenir

Powdered tea arrived in Japan in the **12th century**, brought back from Song-dynasty China by the Buddhist monk **Eisai**, founder of the Rinzai school of Zen. He pitched it, accurately, as a stimulant that kept monks awake through long meditation sessions. He also wrote one of the first Japanese books on tea, *Kissa Yojoki* ("Drinking Tea for Health"), which is essentially a 12th-century wellness blog.

For the next four hundred years, matcha was a monastic and aristocratic substance. By the 16th century, the tea master **Sen no Rikyu** had codified it into **chanoyu** — the formal tea ceremony — with its prescribed utensils, bowl rotations, seasonal sweets, and four-hour runtime. (If you've never sat through one, we wrote about that experience separately at [/blog/4-hour-tea-ceremony](/blog/4-hour-tea-ceremony).)

For roughly eight centuries, this was the entire matcha universe: monks, samurai, very patient guests, and a small caste of farmers in Kyoto Prefecture. Then 1996 happened.

## The Häagen-Dazs Pivot

In 1996, **Häagen-Dazs Japan** launched Green Tea ice cream. It was meant as a domestic curiosity — a regional flavor for the local market, the way American Häagen-Dazs sells dulce de leche. It became the best-selling Häagen-Dazs flavor in Japan within two years, then began quietly shipping internationally. It is now a global flagship product, sold in over thirty countries, and is single-handedly responsible for an entire generation of Westerners knowing what matcha tastes like.

Three years later, in **1999**, Starbucks Japan launched the **Matcha Frappuccino**. It crossed the Pacific to U.S. menus in **2006**. By the early 2010s — somewhere around the same moment turmeric lattes were having their cultural minute — matcha tipped from "Japanese specialty" to global wellness staple. Instagram, predictably, did the rest.

What followed was the matcha-everything era, which has not stopped: matcha Kit Kats (one of the [400-odd regional flavors](/blog/kit-kat-400-flavors) Nestle Japan now produces), matcha mochi, matcha eclairs, matcha cheesecake, **Coedo Matcha Lager**, Suntory matcha cocktails, matcha ramen at specialty shops in Asakusa, and — yes — a limited-edition matcha Cup Noodle that Nissin released as a regional Kyoto product and then quietly kept making because people kept buying it. Starbucks Japan rotates a matcha-something into nearly every seasonal menu, the way it rotates [sakura drinks in spring](/blog/starbucks-japan-sakura).

## Uji, Nishio, Sayama: Where It Actually Comes From

Roughly **70% of Japan's matcha** is produced in three regions: **Uji** (Kyoto Prefecture), **Nishio** (Aichi Prefecture), and **Sayama** (Saitama Prefecture). Uji has the longest pedigree and the highest ceiling — it is where Sen no Rikyu sourced his tea, and where the top ceremonial grades still come from. Nishio produces more volume and dominates the culinary and industrial market. Sayama is smaller, colder, and known for a fuller-bodied roast.

The brand hierarchy, if you walk into a Kyoto tea shop and want to look like you've done your homework:

- **Marukyu Koyamaen** — Uji. The benchmark for ceremonial grade. Eye-watering at the top end.
- **Ippodo** — Uji, founded 1717. The Tiffany & Co. of Japanese tea, with a beautiful flagship in Kyoto and a Marunouchi outpost in Tokyo.
- **Yamamasa Koyamaen** — Uji, often confused with Marukyu by tourists. Different family.
- **Tsujiri** — Uji, founded 1860. Aggressive global expansion; the one you've probably seen in Singapore or London.
- **Nakamura Tokichi** — Uji, founded 1854. Famous for matcha desserts; the Yokohama NEWoMan branch has a queue most weekends.

If you want to drink the real thing in Japan, those last three names are where to start.

## Ceremonial vs. Culinary, and Why Your Latte Is Lying to You

Here is the part the wellness industry would prefer you not dwell on. **Roughly 90% of the matcha Westerners consume is culinary grade** — the cheaper, more bitter, slightly browner powder designed to be cut with milk, sugar, ice cream, or wheat flour. Ceremonial grade is meant to be whisked with hot water and drunk plain. Putting ceremonial matcha in a Frappuccino is, technically, a waste; using culinary matcha in a tea ceremony is, technically, an insult.

Most cafés outside Japan use culinary grade and call it "matcha." This is not fraud — it is just the supply chain. But it does mean that the average Western "matcha latte" bears roughly the same relationship to chanoyu matcha as a Hershey bar does to single-origin Valrhona.

The health-claim industrial complex compounds the confusion. Matcha is genuinely interesting on paper: about **3% catechins by weight** (mostly EGCG, the antioxidant compound everyone cites), a useful dose of **L-theanine** (the amino acid responsible for the famous calm-focus feeling), and — per one widely circulated 2003 study — roughly **137x the antioxidant content of standard brewed green tea** by gram. All of this is true. All of it is also measured at **ceremonial-grade dosage** (roughly 2g of pure matcha per bowl), not at the homeopathic quantity dusted into a 24-ounce Frappuccino with 50g of sugar. Drinking a matcha latte for the EGCG is a bit like eating a Caesar salad for the anchovies.

## The 2025 Uji Shortage

In late 2024 and through **2025**, Uji producers began rationing. Global demand — driven by TikTok, the matcha-latte boom, and a post-pandemic spike in Japanese tourism — outpaced supply. Tencha acreage cannot be expanded quickly; the shade cultivation, hand-picking, and stone milling do not scale on TikTok's timeline. Ippodo introduced per-customer limits at its Kyoto flagship. Marukyu Koyamaen suspended new wholesale accounts. Wholesale prices for ceremonial grade rose roughly **50%** year-over-year.

The shortage is, in a sense, the entire matcha story compressed into eighteen months: an eight-hundred-year-old craft product colliding with global consumer demand it was never structured for. The producers are not displeased — prices are good — but they are also not in a rush to industrialize. Granite mills, as it turns out, still grind at 30 grams an hour.

## Where to Actually Drink the Good Stuff

If you find yourself in Japan and want to taste matcha as something other than a flavoring agent:

- **Ippodo Tokyo Marunouchi** — a quiet tasting room next to Tokyo Station. They will whisk a bowl for you and explain it without condescension.
- **Nakamura Tokichi, NEWoMan Yokohama** — for the dessert side. The matcha jelly parfait is the gateway drug.
- **Tsujiri Asakusa** — touristy, but the soft-serve is genuinely excellent.

For deeper context, the [4-hour tea ceremony](/blog/4-hour-tea-ceremony) is the canonical experience. For the opposite end of the spectrum, the [depachika food halls](/blog/depachika-food-halls) in any major department store will sell you fourteen matcha desserts in a single basement.

## TL;DR

Matcha is a 12th-century Zen-monk import that spent 800 years as a ceremonial drink, then in 1996 became a Häagen-Dazs flavor, then in 1999 a Starbucks Frappuccino, then — by 2025 — a global wellness commodity in genuine short supply. About 90% of what the West calls "matcha" is culinary grade; the ceremonial grade is the one worth flying for. The shortage is real, prices are up 50%, and Cup Noodle still makes a matcha edition. None of this is considered weird in Japan.
