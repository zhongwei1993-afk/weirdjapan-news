# The Pounding of Mochi is Japan's Most Dangerous Cooking Method

Two people, one giant wooden mallet, one stone bowl. They alternate strikes — one swing per second, sometimes faster. Between each strike, a second person reaches bare-handed into the mortar to flip and fold the steaming dough. If the timing breaks, the mallet hits the hand. If the dough cools, it has to be reheated. If everyone does it right, you end up with a fresh batch of mochi for New Year's morning. And, statistically, somewhere in Tokyo, a few people will die from eating it within the week.

This is the central paradox of mochitsuki: it is one of Japan's most beloved annual rituals and also one of its most quietly lethal foods. Both things are true at the same time, and neither has any visible effect on the other.

## The Ritual: Mochitsuki, Step by Step

Mochitsuki — literally "mochi-pounding" — is the traditional method for turning hard, glutinous rice into the soft, elastic block known as mochi. The process is unchanged in any meaningful way from how it was done centuries ago.

First, **mochigome**, a short-grain glutinous rice, is washed and soaked overnight. The next morning it is steamed in wooden trays — never boiled — until each grain is translucent and barely holding its shape. The hot rice is then dumped into an **usu**, a heavy stone or wood mortar that can weigh more than 100 kilograms. From there it is worked with a **kine**, a long wooden mallet shaped roughly like an oversized croquet hammer.

The first phase is grinding. One person leans on the kine and presses the rice into paste, twisting at the wrist. Only when the grains have broken down does the pounding begin. From there it is alternating rhythm: one person strikes, the other folds the dough with wet hands between strikes. A standard 1.5-kilogram batch takes about thirty minutes of continuous work and produces a slab of dough that is glossy, warm, and so cohesive it pulls back from your fingers like a living thing.

That cohesion is the entire point. It is also the problem.

## A New Year Tradition With a Body Count

Mochi is eaten year-round in Japan, but the major mochitsuki events happen in late December, timed to **oshogatsu** — the New Year holiday. Families, temples, and neighborhood associations gather in courtyards to pound enough mochi for the coming weeks. The fresh dough is shaped into small rounds, dusted with starch, and eaten in soup (*ozoni*), grilled with soy sauce, or wrapped around sweet bean paste.

It is also, every January, the most lethal food in the country.

The **Tokyo Fire Department** publishes annual figures on mochi-related choking incidents in the capital alone. In a typical year it logs roughly **10 to 15 deaths and around 150 emergency hospitalizations** between late December and early January. Nationwide figures are higher. The victims are overwhelmingly elderly — by the department's own count, the vast majority are aged 70 or older, with the steepest risk curve climbing past 80.

The numbers do not move much from year to year. They have been broadly stable for decades. New Year's mochi deaths are, in the public-health sense of the word, predictable.

## Why Mochi, Specifically, Is So Hard to Survive

Choking deaths from other foods happen, of course. In the United States, hot dogs and whole grapes are the most-cited offenders, particularly for young children — together responsible for dozens of pediatric deaths a year and a long list of preventive warnings from pediatricians. The shape of a hot dog is famously close to the diameter of a child's airway.

Mochi is a different problem. The danger is not shape but **behavior**. Warm, fresh mochi is extraordinarily elastic and adhesive. When a piece lodges in the throat, it does not sit there as a discrete object the way a grape might. It deforms, fills the airway, and bonds to the mucosal walls. The Heimlich maneuver, which works by using lung air to expel a foreign object, is significantly less effective against a substance that has effectively glued itself in place. Emergency responders in Japan often describe needing to physically extract pieces with forceps or a suction device — and by the time they arrive, the window is frequently closed.

This is also why the risk is so concentrated in the elderly. Reduced saliva production, slower swallowing reflexes, weaker cough strength, and (often) ill-fitting dentures combine to make a mouthful of mochi a meaningfully harder problem at 82 than at 32.

## The Public-Health Response

Japan's response to all of this is not to stop eating mochi. It is to issue, every December, what may be the most consistent annual food-safety campaign in the world.

The Tokyo Fire Department, NHK, regional governments, and consumer-affairs offices all publish the same advisory in the last week of December. The standard message, repeated almost verbatim each year:

- **Cut mochi into small pieces** before serving — pieces no larger than a 1 cm cube for elderly diners.
- **Chew thoroughly.** Do not talk while eating.
- **Drink water or tea first** to moisten the throat.
- **Do not leave elderly relatives eating mochi alone.**
- **Know how to respond** to a choking incident before the meal begins.

The campaigns are blunt by Japanese standards. Posters show the number of expected deaths. Television news segments demonstrate the back-slap technique. It is roughly comparable, in tone and ubiquity, to FDNY's annual fire-safety push around Thanksgiving deep-frying — a recurring, candid public reminder that a beloved seasonal ritual has a body count attached.

A small market has grown up around the problem. Vacuum-style oral aspirators — handheld devices that fit over the mouth and pull obstructions out with negative pressure — are now sold widely in Japan for use in elder-care homes. The American-made **LifeVac**, the best-known of these, has been sold in the Japanese market since 2018 and is now stocked in many nursing facilities and a growing number of private homes. Hospitals serving older patients increasingly use a **gel-form mochi**, manufactured to taste and look like the real thing but to break apart cleanly in the mouth.

## The Mochi Universe Around the Risk

It is worth remembering, when reading the choking statistics, how thoroughly mochi is woven into the year. It is not one food. It is a calendar.

- **Kagami mochi** — two stacked discs of hardened mochi topped with a **daidai** bitter orange — is placed in homes as a New Year altar decoration, then eaten in mid-January.
- **Kashiwa mochi**, wrapped in an oak leaf, appears around Children's Day on **May 5**.
- **Sakura mochi**, dyed pink and wrapped in a pickled cherry leaf, marks the early-April hanami season.

Each region has its own variants — flatter, rounder, filled, unfilled, grilled, boiled. The cultural footprint is enormous and almost entirely benign. The deaths come overwhelmingly from one specific use case: fresh, warm, hand-pounded mochi eaten in soup on the morning of January 1.

Most of that mochi, today, is not actually pounded by hand. Tiger and Zojirushi sell countertop **mochi machines** — the Tiger SMG-A360 is a standard model around ¥30,000 — that steam and knead a batch in under an hour. Convenience stores stock individually wrapped rounds year-round. Hand-pounded mochitsuki has shifted, for most families, from a household chore to a community event: a thing you do once a year at the local shrine while someone photographs it for Instagram.

The ritual survives in other forms too. The kabuki play **"Sakai no Saru"** features a comic mochi-pounding scene in which a monkey character tries to operate the kine; it has been performed for over a century and is one of the standard reference points for the cultural weight of the practice.

## Why the Tradition Continues Anyway

A reasonable outside observer might ask why a country with a rapidly aging population continues to center its biggest holiday on a food it knows will kill some of its grandparents.

The honest answer is that Japan has made a collective decision — never formalized, never debated — that the cultural value of mochi outweighs the cost. The deaths are treated as a known risk to be managed, not a reason to abandon the food. The public-health apparatus issues its warnings; families cut the pieces smaller; care homes buy the aspirators; the gel mochi sits on hospital trays. And on January 1, almost everyone still eats it.

There is something quietly characteristic about this. The tradition is not preserved in spite of the risk, exactly. It is preserved alongside the risk, with both acknowledged in the same breath. The Tokyo Fire Department's annual press release does not ask anyone to stop. It asks them to chew.

## TL;DR

Mochitsuki is a centuries-old ritual: steamed glutinous rice pounded in a stone usu with a wooden kine for about thirty minutes per batch, traditionally for New Year. The resulting dough is so elastic and adhesive that, every January, the Tokyo Fire Department records roughly 10–15 choking deaths and 150 hospitalizations in the capital alone, almost all in people over 70. Japan responds with annual public-warning campaigns, household aspirators like LifeVac (sold there since 2018), gel-form hospital mochi, and countertop mochi machines — but the tradition itself continues, intact, every year.
