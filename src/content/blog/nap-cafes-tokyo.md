# Tokyo's Nap Cafés Sell 90 Minutes of Sleep for ¥1,000

It is 1:14 p.m. on a Tuesday in Shimbashi. A man in a grey suit pays ¥800 at a counter, removes his shoes, walks behind a curtain, lies down on a clean single bed, sets a quiet alarm on his phone, and closes his eyes. Forty minutes later he is back at his desk. No one at his office knows where he went, and no one asks. This is not a scandal. It is lunch.

Tokyo has been quietly running a parallel economy of paid sleep for almost two decades. The product is simple — a bed, a pillow, a curtain, silence — and the customer is anyone whose day has thirty spare minutes and nowhere private to spend them. To a foreign visitor the whole thing looks like a wellness trend. In Japan it is closer to plumbing.

## The Ohirune Café, Briefly Explained

The category Japanese media calls *ohirune café* (お昼寝カフェ — literally "afternoon-nap café") refers to any small commercial premises that sells a flat-rate sleeping session, typically thirty to ninety minutes, inside a private or semi-private bed. The format is not glamorous. There is no spa music, no aromatherapy upsell, no influencer corner. There is a bed, a clean sheet, a lavender pillow if you are lucky, and a staff member who will quietly knock when your time is up.

The pioneer is generally credited as Napia, which opened in Shimbashi in 2009 and became the first business in central Tokyo to advertise nothing but napping. Napia's original menu was almost confrontational in its simplicity: ten minutes for ¥160, thirty for ¥500, an hour for ¥1,000. Customers were instructed to remove shoes, lie down, and leave. It was so functional it bordered on austere, which is probably why it worked.

Today the going rate across the city sits between ¥500 and ¥1,500 for a thirty-to-ninety-minute session, depending on whether you are paying for a curtained partition, a private booth, or a full single room. There is no concept of brunch pricing. The product is sleep.

## A Short Tour of the Competitors

Napia is not alone. The format has quietly multiplied across the city, each operator nudging the formula in a slightly different direction.

- **Nesta Café (Shibuya)** styles itself as a "relaxation café" with reclining beds, blackout partitions, and a menu that explicitly separates "power nap" (thirty minutes) from "deep nap" (ninety). Drinks are free. Talking is not.
- **Bed and Book Tokyo** in Ikebukuro grafts the nap onto a small lending library. You select a paperback, climb into a bunk-style sleeper lined with shelves, and read until you don't. Sessions start around ¥600 an hour.
- **Naphours**, the daytime program run by the 9h ("nine hours") capsule hotel chain, sells one-hour use of a sleek white capsule pod for around ¥1,500. The pods are the same ones overnight guests use; only the lighting and the contract change.

For travellers who already understand the [capsule hotel](/blog/capsule-hotel-tokyo) format, Naphours is the easiest mental bridge: it is a [proper capsule](/blog/best-capsule-hotels-tokyo), rented by the hour, in the middle of the working day.

A second, older tier of napping infrastructure runs in parallel. The big manga kissa chains — **Manboo** and **Gera Gera Bo** chief among them — rent private booths from roughly ¥400 to ¥800 an hour, originally for reading comics, increasingly for sleeping. Many include a flat mat, a reclining chair, a power outlet, free soft drinks, and a shower for an extra few hundred yen. The line between "internet café" and "nap café" has eroded to the point that some Tokyo residents now [live in manga kissa booths](/blog/manga-kissa-apartments) for months at a time. Sleeping in one for forty minutes barely registers.

## Inemuri: Why Nobody Is Embarrassed

None of this would scale in a culture that treated daytime sleep as a moral failing. Japan does not. The relevant concept is *inemuri* (居眠り), usually translated as "being present while asleep" — the socially accepted practice of nodding off in trains, in waiting rooms, at desks, and, famously, in meetings. The widely held interpretation is that visible exhaustion signals visible effort. Falling asleep at your desk does not mean you are lazy. It means you worked late enough to need the nap.

We have covered the workplace version of this elsewhere — the [meeting-room inemuri](/blog/inemuri-meeting-naps) where a senior manager dozes through a presentation and nobody mentions it — and the more extreme public version, in which a salaryman ends his evening [asleep on the sidewalk](/blog/salaryman-drunk-sidewalk) outside a konbini. The nap café is simply the commercial, indoor, sober variant of the same cultural permission. If sleeping in public is fine, sleeping in a paid bed is hardly controversial.

This is also why the [silence on Tokyo trains](/blog/silent-trains-tokyo) is not an etiquette rule pinned to a wall. Half the carriage is asleep. The quiet is for them.

## The Business Model: Salarymen, Then Everyone Else

Napia's original customer was extremely specific: the Shimbashi salaryman with a ninety-minute lunch break and nowhere to go. Tokyo office workers do not typically have private offices, sofas, or the cultural cover to nap at their desks for an entire hour. They do have ¥1,000 and a willingness to walk three blocks.

That core customer still pays the rent. But the audience has widened in two directions. First, women — particularly retail and hospitality workers on split shifts — who never had access to the male-coded "smoke outside, snooze in the meeting" workaround. Second, and more recent, the international remote worker. Naphours and the 9h pods in particular have leaned into English signage, contactless booking, and Wi-Fi strong enough to take a Zoom call before lying down. The pitch writes itself: a quiet, climate-controlled, single-occupancy room in central Tokyo for the price of two flat whites.

The convenience-store chains briefly tried to muscle in. In 2019, both **FamilyMart** and **Lawson** ran small pilots installing micro-nap pods inside or beside selected branches — twenty-minute sessions, vending-machine payment, no staff. The pilots were widely reported overseas as the future of urban rest. They were quietly shelved within about a year. The official explanation was hygiene cycling and staff overhead. The unofficial one, repeated by people who used them, was that a konbini under fluorescent light at the volume of a konbini is not actually a place anyone can sleep.

## The Sleep Deficit Driving All of This

The market exists because the country is exhausted. Japan's own Cabinet Office surveys put the national average sleep duration at roughly **7 hours 22 minutes** per night — the second-shortest figure in the OECD, behind only South Korea — and consistently find that around **25 percent** of adults report chronic insufficient sleep. The numbers are worse for working-age women, who shoulder a disproportionate share of unpaid domestic work on top of full days at the office.

Public-health researchers have been raising the alarm for years. Work from the **RIKEN** institute and affiliated sleep labs has repeatedly pointed at the twenty-minute nap as a high-return, low-cost intervention: long enough to lift alertness and short-term memory, short enough to avoid sleep inertia, and compatible with an actual workday. Several large Japanese employers — including Mitsubishi Estate and a handful of tech firms — now formally permit, and in some cases encourage, mid-afternoon naps at the desk or in dedicated rooms. The nap café is, in effect, the outsourced version of that policy for everyone whose employer has not caught up.

## How a Foreign Visitor Actually Books One

The biggest barrier is not money. It is reading a Japanese-only reservation page.

- **Naphours** is the easiest entry point. Walk-in is possible at the Akasaka and Shinjuku 9h locations; one hour runs about ¥1,500, payment by card or IC.
- **Nesta Café** in Shibuya takes walk-ins outside lunch peak (roughly 11:30 to 14:00). Cash is faster than apps.
- **Bed and Book Tokyo** can be booked through a normal web form; basic English is fine.
- **Manboo** and **Gera Gera Bo** require a one-time membership card (free, ID needed); thereafter you tap in, pick a booth class, and pay on the way out.

Etiquette is short and almost entirely physical. Shoes off at the threshold, not at the bed. Phone to silent, alarm set to a polite volume, set *before* you lie down. Do not eat in the bed. Do not video-call from the bed. When the staff knocks, leave promptly — the next session is usually already booked, and the bed needs ten minutes to be reset. Tipping is not expected and will confuse the cashier.

A small practical note: most operators will swap the pillowcase and top sheet between customers but not the duvet itself. If this matters to you, choose a pod-format venue (Naphours, 9h) where the unit is wiped down end-to-end.

## The West Tried This and Mostly Gave Up

The obvious comparison is **MetroNaps**, the New York start-up that introduced the futuristic "EnergyPod" recliner to American corporate lobbies in the mid-2000s. Google, HuffPost, and a long tail of Silicon Valley campuses installed them. Most were treated as office décor. A handful of airport branches survived. The standalone retail concept — pay-by-the-minute napping for the general public — never quite took, partly because there was no cultural baseline that made daytime sleep acceptable in front of strangers, and partly because the unit economics of a single ¥1,500 bed only work in a city that is already willing to fill it from noon to seven.

Tokyo has both. The cultural permission was there before the product, and the city's relentless walk-up density means a clean bed near a station will sell out on a weekday. That, more than any wellness narrative, is why the nap café is a Tokyo phenomenon rather than a global one.

## TL;DR

- Tokyo's *ohirune* cafés sell thirty- to ninety-minute naps in private beds for **¥500–¥1,500**.
- The pioneer, **Napia**, opened in Shimbashi in **2009**; competitors now include **Nesta Café**, **Bed and Book Tokyo**, and the **Naphours** daytime program at 9h capsule hotels (~¥1,500/hour).
- Older infrastructure — manga kissa chains like **Manboo** and **Gera Gera Bo** — has quietly absorbed the same demand at ~¥800/hour.
- The cultural baseline is *inemuri*: public sleep is read as effort, not laziness.
- Japan averages **7h 22min** of sleep — second-shortest in the OECD — and roughly a quarter of adults report chronic deficit; **RIKEN** research backs the twenty-minute nap.
- FamilyMart and Lawson tried konbini nap pods in **2019**. They are mostly gone.
- Western analogues like **MetroNaps** never matched the format, because the cultural permission was never there.
