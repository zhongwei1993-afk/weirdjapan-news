# Inside Tokyo's Nintendo Store — A Pilgrimage Site for Gamers

For roughly four decades, Nintendo was one of the most globally famous Japanese companies that did not bother to operate a single branded retail store in its home country. There was a Nintendo NY in Rockefeller Center, opened in 2005. There were Pokémon Centers everywhere. There were entire Disney and Universal theme parks devoted to characters Nintendo had created or licensed adjacent to. But if you flew into Tokyo and asked where the Nintendo store was, the honest answer was: there isn't one.

That changed in November 2019, when Nintendo TOKYO opened on the 6th floor of the newly rebuilt Shibuya PARCO. It was the first Nintendo-branded retail store in Japan. Tokyo Disneyland had been operating since 1983. Universal Studios Japan had been open in Osaka since 2001. Nintendo, the company whose mascot is more recognizable than most national flags, took until 2019 to put a permanent shop in its own country.

It is now the single most reliably crowded floor in Shibuya PARCO, and the anchor of what is quietly becoming Tokyo's most concentrated gaming-retail address.

## A 538-square-metre cathedral on the 6th floor

The store occupies roughly 538 square metres of the PARCO's 6th floor and was designed by Tokyo studio Plus 81. The design brief, by all appearances, was to take a generation of Nintendo's most marketable IP and convert it into walk-through architecture. They did not under-deliver.

The entrance is framed by a green Mario warp pipe, scaled up enough that adults can stand inside it for photos and routinely do. A two-metre Bowser statue glowers from one corner, mostly used as a sight gag by people half its height. A life-size Link sword display anchors the Zelda section. There is a Splatoon ink-wall photo corner where the splat patterns are repainted often enough that they always look freshly hostile.

None of this is rotated out. These are permanent installations, which is itself unusual — Japanese flagship retail tends to favour seasonal swap-outs and pop-up theming. Nintendo TOKYO instead operates more like a small museum that happens to sell things, with the fixed installations forming a circuit you can walk in under ten minutes if you're not stopping for photos. Nobody is not stopping for photos.

The product floor wraps around the installations. Switch consoles in every current colourway sit near the back, flanked by every first-party title currently in print. The merchandise wall is the actual point of the visit.

## The merchandise economy

The original sin of Nintendo's late entry into Japanese retail is that it created enormous pent-up demand for physical, branded objects. The store leans into this hard.

Standard pricing, as of the current run:

- **T-shirts**: ¥3,500–¥6,000, with Splatoon and Animal Crossing graphics rotating most often
- **Plush toys**: ¥4,000–¥15,000, with the higher end occupied by oversized Kirbys and the limited Yoshi line
- **Gachapon**: ¥500–¥1,500 per turn, with the machines parked near the till as a deliberate impulse trap
- **Stationery, mugs, tote bags, pin sets**: priced as you would expect, designed better than you would expect

The category that actually moves the needle is the **Tokyo-exclusive line** — controllers, Joy-Con shells, and bundle items branded specifically to the store and not sold through Nintendo's general retail or online channels. These are the items that justify the airfare for a certain kind of visitor and, more importantly, the items that show up on Mercari within hours of release at two to three times retail.

This brings us to the part of the store that has quietly shaped its operational policy: the resale arbitrage problem. When Nintendo TOKYO runs a sale — most notably the recurring Mario Kart limited Switch bundle drops — the queue forms before sunrise, and a non-trivial percentage of buyers are visibly there to resell. The store has responded by enforcing one-per-customer limits on flagged SKUs, requiring ID checks on some bundles, and on busy days routing entry through a timed-ticket system distributed at the PARCO entrance from opening. Weekend queues without a ticket regularly run 30 to 60 minutes. Weekday afternoons are walk-in.

It is, in short, a normal store that has been forced by its own scarcity to behave like a venue.

## The PARCO 6F gaming floor

Nintendo TOKYO is not alone on the 6th floor, and this is the part most visitor guides under-explain.

Immediately adjacent — a separate store, with its own entrance and staff, but sharing the same elevator bank — is **Pokémon Center Shibuya**. The two are operationally distinct (Pokémon Centers are run by The Pokémon Company, which is jointly owned by Nintendo, Game Freak and Creatures), but the practical effect for a visitor is that you can walk from a Bowser statue to a Mewtwo statue without changing floors.

A short walk further along the same floor is **Capcom Store Tokyo**, the flagship for Monster Hunter, Resident Evil and the rest of Capcom's catalogue. Smaller than Nintendo TOKYO, denser per square metre, and noticeably more popular with the over-30 demographic.

The result is that PARCO 6F has, more or less by accident, become the most concentrated dedicated game-publisher retail floor in Japan. You can spend four hours here without leaving the elevator radius. Many people do.

## Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto: the three Nintendo stores

After the 2019 Tokyo opening, Nintendo expanded slowly:

- **Nintendo TOKYO** — Shibuya PARCO 6F, opened November 2019. The original. Busiest. Largest exclusive range.
- **Nintendo OSAKA** — Daimaru Umeda, opened 2022. Roughly comparable in size, with a partially overlapping but Osaka-flavoured exclusive line.
- **Nintendo KYOTO** — Takashimaya Kyoto, opened 2023. The smallest of the three, and the closest to Nintendo's actual head office, which sits about two kilometres south.

Three stores in four years, all in major department stores, all on upper floors, all designed with permanent installation pieces. The Kyoto store is the most thematically restrained of the three — fewer warp pipes, more wood — which appears to be a deliberate concession to the city it sits in.

For visitors trying to choose: Tokyo has the deepest exclusive range and the worst queues, Osaka is the fastest in and out, Kyoto is the most photogenic and the least crowded on a weekday.

## Planning a visit, and what to pair it with

Nintendo TOKYO is open 11:00 to 21:00, in line with the rest of Shibuya PARCO. There are no photo restrictions inside the store, which is itself a small policy choice worth noting in a country where retail photography is often quietly discouraged. English-speaking staff are present on most shifts, and the till accepts most international cards alongside the usual IC and QR payment options.

If you are visiting on a weekend, arrive at opening or expect the timed-ticket flow. If you are visiting on a weekday before 3pm, you will likely walk straight in. Either way, budget at least an hour inside, and another for the rest of the 6th floor.

The natural pairing for a longer trip is **Super Nintendo World at Universal Studios Japan in Osaka**, which is a different proposition entirely — built by Universal Parks under licence rather than by Nintendo itself, theme-park scale rather than retail scale, and priced accordingly. Treat it as the experiential counterpart to the Shibuya store's commercial one. Doing both in a single trip is the closest a Nintendo fan currently gets to a complete circuit in Japan.

Zooming out: Nintendo TOKYO is one node in a larger and still-accelerating shift toward publisher-operated flagship retail in Japan. Capcom, Square Enix, Bandai Namco and The Pokémon Company are all now running their own physical stores in major cities, in some cases on the same floor as each other. For thirty years the model was that game publishers sold software through electronics chains. The new model is that publishers sell experiences through their own walls, and the software is almost incidental. Shibuya PARCO 6F is what that model looks like at full volume.

## TL;DR

Nintendo TOKYO opened in November 2019 as Nintendo's first branded retail store in Japan, on the 6th floor of Shibuya PARCO. It is 538 square metres of permanent Mario, Zelda and Splatoon installations wrapped around a merchandise floor with Tokyo-exclusive items priced from ¥500 gachapon to ¥15,000 plush. Queues run 30–60 minutes on weekends; weekdays are walk-in. Pokémon Center Shibuya and Capcom Store Tokyo share the same floor. Osaka opened in 2022, Kyoto in 2023. Pair with Super Nintendo World at USJ Osaka for the full circuit.
