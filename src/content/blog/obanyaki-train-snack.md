# Obanyaki — The ¥150 Snack Sold From Tokyo Train Station Stalls

There is a smell that hits you when you exit the wrong ticket gate at Tokyo Station — somewhere between pancake batter and caramelized sugar — and if you follow it, you will end up in front of a small stall selling a hot, hand-sized cake stuffed with sweet bean paste. The cake costs less than a coffee. The man making it has been making it since before you were born. Nobody is filming it. This is obanyaki, one of the most quietly ubiquitous snacks in the country.

## From a bridge in Kanda

Obanyaki (大判焼き) literally means "large-imprint baked" — a reference to the *ōban*, the oversized oval gold coins minted in the Edo period. The cake is shaped like one. That is essentially the entire poetry of the name.

The trouble is that nobody can agree what to call it. The same product is sold as:

- **Imagawa-yaki (今川焼き)** — the original name, from the Imagawa bridge in the Kanda district of Edo, where a stall is said to have started selling them around 1772, during the reign of the shogun's chief advisor Tanuma Okitsugu.
- **Kaiten-yaki (回転焼き)** — "rotating bake," favored in western Japan.
- **Gyoza-yaki**, **taiko-yaki** (drum-bake), **futaba-yaki**, **kogane-yaki** — and a few dozen other regional names depending on which prefecture's grandmother you ask.

It is the same cake. The naming variation is a small map of how Japanese regionalism works: an identical object travels three hundred kilometers and acquires a new word, a new origin story, and a faintly defensive local pride. Ask an Osakan if their kaiten-yaki is the same as Tokyo's imagawa-yaki and watch the face they make.

For the rest of this article we will call it obanyaki, mostly because it is the name least likely to start an argument in the comments.

## The griddle, the dollop, the lid

The mechanics are almost insultingly simple. The standard tool is a cast-iron griddle pocked with rows of cup-shaped molds, usually 8 centimeters across and 3 centimeters deep, heated from below by gas. The vendor brushes each mold with a thin film of oil, ladles in a pale, slightly sweet batter — flour, egg, sugar, sometimes a whisper of mirin — and then drops a generous spoonful of *anko* (sweet azuki bean paste) into the center of half the molds. A second pour of batter goes over the top of a separate row of empty molds. When the bottoms have set, the vendor uses a metal pick to lift the bean-paste cakes and flip them onto the plain ones, sealing the two halves into a single puck. The whole griddle is then closed with a hinged top plate, and the cakes finish cooking on both sides.

The result, when handed to you in a thin paper sleeve, is about the weight of a baseball and roughly the temperature of the sun. A first bite usually involves at least one small, regrettable noise. (For a related lesson in respecting Japanese street food temperatures, see our piece on [how takoyaki burns your mouth](/blog/takoyaki-mouth-burn).)

Prices in 2026 sit between ¥120 and ¥250 each, depending on neighborhood and filling. Classic *tsubu-an* (chunky bean) and *koshi-an* (smooth bean) are the baseline. From there the fillings sprawl:

- **Matcha custard** — the most photogenic.
- **Sweet potato (satsumaimo)** — autumnal, mealy, oddly nostalgic.
- **Custard cream** — the gateway flavor for children and tourists.
- **Savory variants** — cheese, curry, pizza-flavored, and at one Akihabara stall, a mentaiko-mayo version that has no business being as good as it is.

A purist will tell you that obanyaki is anko or nothing. A purist will also be ignored by the line of office workers behind them ordering the cheese one.

## Where to find them

Train stations are the natural habitat. The reasons are partly logistical — small footprint, high foot traffic, impulse purchase — and partly emotional, because a hot bean cake on a cold platform is one of the genuinely good things about commuting in Japan.

A short list of reliable stalls in Tokyo:

- **Mr. Imagawa**, inside Tokyo Station's Gransta area, doing a brisk trade in classic anko and a rotating seasonal flavor.
- **Kashiwaya**, just outside JR Akihabara's Electric Town exit, where the line is shortest around 3 p.m. and longest after 6.
- **Lumine 1F kiosks at Shinjuku**, where two or three competing stalls sit within fifty meters of each other, a small daily referendum on whose batter is better.
- **Sanshodo** and **Asakusa Imagawa-yaki**, two of the city's larger chains, with roughly ten locations each scattered across the wards. Asakusa Imagawa-yaki keeps a stubbornly traditional menu; Sanshodo experiments more.

Outside the city, the stalls reappear at every *matsuri* — the summer and autumn festivals where shrine grounds fill with rows of yatai stands selling yakisoba, kakigori, candied apples, and inevitably, obanyaki. The festival version is often slightly larger, slightly cheaper, and sold by someone who only does this six weekends a year.

## The economics of a ¥200 cake

The math is what gets you.

A festival obanyaki sells for about ¥200. The ingredient cost — flour, sugar, egg, a tablespoon of azuki paste — comes in around ¥40 per cake. A single vendor working a busy festival evening can move 500 cakes in a few hours. That is roughly ¥80,000 of gross margin from one griddle, one folding table, and a propane tank. Multiply that across a three-day festival and you understand why the same families show up at the same shrines every year, and why nobody is in any rush to franchise this out.

The convenience stores noticed. In 2022, **FamilyMart** launched a chilled, microwaveable obanyaki in its dessert case, an event that the food press covered with the slightly embarrassed enthusiasm it usually reserves for new Kit Kat flavors. **Lawson** countered with a matcha version a few months later. Neither product is as good as a fresh one off a cast-iron griddle, and both sell extremely well anyway, because the price of convenience is always paid in texture. (The same logic applies to [supermarket anpan versus a real bakery's](/blog/anpan-bread-bean-paste).)

Home cooks have a third option: a ceramic *obanyaki pan* for a single gas burner. **Iwatani** sells one for around ¥3,000 that makes seven at a time, bought mostly by grandmothers and YouTube viewers. The pans work. The cakes are never quite right — a kitchen burner cannot match a commercial griddle's heat mass, and the result always tastes faintly like a pancake pretending to be something else.

## How to spot a good one

After enough of these, you develop a small private rubric.

- **Visible azuki ratio.** A vendor cuts one open as a display sample. Look at it. You want bean paste filling at least 60% of the interior cross-section, with whole beans still recognizable. Anything less and you are eating a sweet pancake with a smear.
- **Crust thickness.** The shell should be thin enough that you can see faint browning patterns through it, not a thick cakey wall. A thick crust means too much batter, usually a sign the vendor is stretching ingredients.
- **Heat.** It should be uncomfortable to hold for the first thirty seconds. If it is room-temperature when handed to you, it has been sitting. Walk away.
- **The flip line.** Look for a faint seam around the equator where the two halves were joined. A clean, almost invisible seam means a confident vendor. A messy one means a trainee.

It helps to know what obanyaki is *not*. It is not [taiyaki](/blog/melon-pan-no-melon), the fish-shaped cousin invented in 1909 in Azabu by a vendor trying to differentiate his stall from the imagawa-yaki crowd. It is not [dorayaki](/blog/mochi-pounding-dangerous), the disc-shaped Edo-era pancake sandwich beloved by Doraemon. It is not a [depachika dessert-counter item](/blog/depachika-food-halls), although you will sometimes find a premium version masquerading as one. It is its own thing — older, plainer, cheaper, and in many ways the most honest of the family.

That honesty is the appeal. No story being sold, no chef with a tasting menu — just a man, a griddle, a bowl of beans, and a line of people who want a hot snack on the way home. Japan has been doing this since the year George Washington was forty, and shows no sign of stopping.

## TL;DR

- **What it is**: A round, palm-sized, griddle-baked cake filled with sweet azuki bean paste. Same product as imagawa-yaki, kaiten-yaki, gyoza-yaki, taiko-yaki — naming depends on region.
- **Origin**: Edo-period Kanda, near the Imagawa bridge, around 1772.
- **Size and price**: 8cm wide, 3cm thick, ¥120–¥250 each.
- **Where**: Train station stalls (Tokyo Station's Mr. Imagawa, Akihabara's Kashiwaya, Shinjuku Lumine), chain shops like Sanshodo and Asakusa Imagawa-yaki, and every shrine festival in the country.
- **Modern variations**: Matcha, custard, sweet potato, cheese, curry, pizza. Konbini versions exist; fresh ones are better.
- **How to spot a good one**: Visible whole beans, thin crust, painfully hot to the touch.
