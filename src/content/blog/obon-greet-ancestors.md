# The Festival Where Japanese Greet Their Dead Ancestors

For three or four days in the middle of August, the entire country quietly assumes that the dead have come home. Offices close. Bullet trains sell out months in advance. Grandmothers leave eggplants on the family altar. Nobody calls it a holiday on the calendar, because everybody already knows.

This is Obon (お盆), and it is arguably the most important annual event in Japan that no foreigner has heard of.

## The Sutra Behind the Festival

The official origin story is Buddhist. Obon is a contracted reading of *Urabon-e* (盂蘭盆会), the Japanese transliteration of the Sanskrit *Avalambana* — better known in English as the **Ullambana Sutra**. In the text, the disciple Maudgalyayana discovers that his late mother has been reborn into the realm of hungry ghosts, and the Buddha instructs him to make offerings to the monastic community on the fifteenth day of the seventh month. The mother is released. The festival is born.

That basic shape — offerings made on behalf of the dead — arrives in Japan sometime in the seventh century and gradually fuses with much older ancestor-worship customs already in place. By the Heian period, the imperial court has adapted the memorial service into something with music and movement, which is the lineage modern Bon-odori folk dance traces itself back to.

So Obon is, technically, a Buddhist memorial rite. In practice, it has been a national family-and-ancestors event for so long that most participants would struggle to name the sutra. The same way most people celebrating Christmas could not summarize the Gospel of Luke.

## When It Actually Happens (And Why Tokyo Is Different)

Most of Japan observes Obon from **August 13 to 16**. This is the dateline that drives Shinkansen pricing, school holidays, and roughly every news segment about traffic jams between mid-summer and Bon-odori season.

Tokyo and Yokohama, however, observe it a month earlier — **July 13 to 16**. This is not regional rebellion. It is a leftover from the Meiji-era calendar reform of 1873, when Japan switched from the lunar to the Gregorian calendar. The lunar seventh month roughly corresponds to August, so most of the country simply slid the festival to August and called it good. The big eastern cities, more eager to align with the new Western calendar, kept the original numerical date in July.

The result is a country that holds the same festival twice, in two different months, depending on which train line you take to your grandmother's house.

## The Ritual Flow: Welcome Fires, Cucumber Horses, Farewell Fires

The mechanics of Obon are surprisingly consistent across the country. The week before the festival, families do **ohaka-mairi** — visiting and cleaning the family grave. Weeds are pulled, the stone is scrubbed, fresh flowers and water are left. (The same grave visits happen at the spring and autumn equinoxes, a season called *higan*, which is the lower-stakes cousin to Obon.)

On the evening of August 13, families light **mukaebi** — small welcome fires, traditionally at the gravesite or the front entrance of the house. The fires guide the ancestor spirits back home. Inside, the family **butsudan** (the household Buddhist altar) is cleaned and dressed with offerings: seasonal fruit, vegetables, sake, sometimes the deceased's favorite snack.

Then there are the animals. On the altar you will see a cucumber with four toothpick legs, and an eggplant with four toothpick legs. These are **shoryouma** (精霊馬), spirit horses. The cucumber is the horse — fast, to bring the ancestors home quickly. The eggplant is the cow — slow, so they can take their time leaving, weighed down with the offerings. It is one of the more genuinely charming details in Japanese folk religion, and it is taken completely seriously by people who would otherwise describe themselves as non-religious.

On the night of August 16, the family lights **okuribi** — farewell fires — to send the spirits back. The most spectacular version of this is in Kyoto: the **Gozan no Okuribi**, often called Daimonji. Five enormous bonfires are lit on the mountainsides surrounding the city, each shaped into a kanji or symbol (the largest being 大, *dai*, "great"). They burn for about half an hour. Roughly **100,000 people** gather along riverbanks and rooftops to watch. Then the fires go out and the dead, presumably, go back.

## Bon-Odori, Awa Odori, and 32 Consecutive Nights of Dancing

While the spirits are visiting, you entertain them. This is the cultural job of **bon-odori**, the community folk dance held in temple courtyards, school grounds, and shopping street intersections across the country.

Most neighborhood bon-odori are extremely accessible. A wooden tower (*yagura*) goes up in the middle of a plaza, taiko drummers climb to the top, recorded music plays through tinny speakers, and concentric rings of dancers shuffle around the tower doing the same six or eight gestures over and over. The steps are taught, slowly, by whoever showed up. Entry is **¥0 to ¥500**, sometimes with a token for a free drink. Foreigners are not just tolerated, they are usually waved into the circle within about ninety seconds.

The two big regional spectacles are:

- **Awa Odori** in Tokushima — roughly **1.3 million attendees** over four nights in mid-August. Coordinated dance troupes called *ren* move through the city in formation.
- **Gujo Odori** in Gifu — held over **32 consecutive nights**, with four of those nights running until dawn. It is the marathon of Japanese folk dancing.

Neither requires a ticket to participate. You show up, you copy the person in front of you, you dance.

## The Unofficial National Holiday

Obon is not on the official list of public holidays. It is, in practice, the largest annual shutdown in the Japanese calendar after New Year. Most companies close for **three to five days**. Factories go dark. Family doctors put up handwritten signs. Convenience stores stay open, because that is the law of nature, but staffed by part-timers covering for the regulars who are on a train to Akita.

That train is the other half of the story. Every year, without fail, every Japanese news outlet runs the same story: the Shinkansen is full. Reserved seats vanish the morning bookings open, one month in advance. Unreserved cars hit 180% capacity. The *U-turn rush* — people returning to the cities after visiting the countryside — produces traffic jams measured in dozens of kilometers. The footage is identical every year and somehow still leads the evening news.

If New Year is the family holiday people talk about, Obon is the family holiday people actually take.

## Muenbotoke, Declining Villages, and the Foreigner Question

A quieter Buddhist concept also hovers over Obon: **muenbotoke** (無縁仏), literally "Buddhas with no connection." These are the spirits of the dead with no living family to make offerings — extinct lineages, the long-forgotten, those who died alone. Temples and many households set aside a separate offering for them, a small communal gesture for the ghosts nobody else is feeding.

The category is becoming uncomfortably contemporary. Rural participation in Obon ritual has been declining for two decades. Cabinet Office surveys released in 2024 showed that household observance of butsudan rites — daily offerings, Obon preparation — has dropped sharply among Japanese under 40, particularly in depopulating rural prefectures where the family grave is now an eight-hour drive from the nearest descendant. The festival continues. The household side of it is thinning.

For foreigners, the entry point remains the public-facing layer: the community bon-odori, the temple visits, the Daimonji viewing. None of it requires Buddhism, Japanese fluency, or a family in Japan. The unspoken etiquette is mostly: be quiet near graves, do not photograph people praying at altars, and if someone hands you a fan and points at the dance circle, get in the circle.

It is often compared, lazily, to Mexico's Día de los Muertos. The instinct is right — both are festivals of welcoming the dead home — but the textures are opposite. Day of the Dead is colorful, public, anchored in marigolds and sugar skulls and visible celebration. Obon is mostly held indoors, on a small altar, with a cucumber. The dancing happens, but the central act is private.

In 2026, with religious affiliation in Japan at a generational low and the country's official holidays as busy as ever, Obon remains what it has been for a long time: the **second New Year**, observed without being announced, the festival the country closes down for without quite admitting that it has.

## TL;DR

Obon is Japan's Buddhist festival for welcoming ancestor spirits home — cleaning the family grave, lighting welcome fires on August 13, leaving offerings on the household altar (including a cucumber-horse and eggplant-cow for the spirits to ride), dancing in community circles, and lighting farewell fires on August 16. Most of Japan observes it August 13–16; Tokyo and Yokohama do it in July. It is not a public holiday, but the country shuts down for it anyway, the bullet trains sell out months in advance, and the Daimonji fires in Kyoto draw 100,000 viewers. Quietly, it is the most important family event of the Japanese year.
