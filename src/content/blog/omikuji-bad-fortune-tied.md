# Japanese Shrines Sell Paper Fortunes. Bad Ones Are Tied to Trees.

Walk into any shrine in Japan on a Sunday afternoon and you will eventually see them: small white paper strips, folded and knotted by the hundreds onto a length of rope or the lower branches of a pine tree. From a distance, the trees look frosted. Up close, they look like a meteorological event made of stationery.

Each of those papers is somebody's bad fortune, deliberately left behind.

This is omikuji (おみくじ) — the sacred lottery — and it has been running, more or less continuously, since around the 10th century.

## A Heian-Era Side Hustle for Monks

The most widely repeated origin story credits the monks of Mount Hiei, the temple complex above Kyoto that produced roughly half of Japan's medieval Buddhist establishment. During the Heian period, the monk Ganzan Daishi is said to have systematized a method of drawing lots to deliver oracular guidance to Heian-era nobility, who needed to know whether their day's auspicious activity was "marry the second cousin" or "do not leave the house."

The technology was paper, ink and a numbered box. The product was certainty, or at least something that looked like it.

Over the next thousand years, the practice migrated from the imperial court to the general public, from temples to shrines, and from solemn divination to a coin-operated experience available at essentially every place of worship in the country. The kanji 御神籤 literally parse as "sacred-deity-lottery," which is also a fair description of the user experience.

## How an Omikuji Actually Works

Most shrines today run the same basic mechanism. You drop a coin — usually ¥100, occasionally ¥200 to ¥500 at larger sites — into a wooden offering box. You pick up a hexagonal metal canister, slightly heavier than it looks, and shake it until a thin stick slides out of a small hole at the top. The stick has a number on it.

You read the number to a shrine attendant, or at smaller shrines you locate the corresponding drawer yourself, and you receive a folded slip of paper. The paper is the fortune.

The hierarchy printed on the slip varies slightly by shrine, but the standard ladder runs:

- **Daikichi (大吉)** — great blessing
- **Kichi (吉)** — blessing
- **Sho-kichi (小吉)** — small blessing
- **Sue-kichi (末吉)** — future blessing
- **Kyo (凶)** — curse
- **Dai-kyo (大凶)** — great curse

Some shrines insert intermediate ranks; a few use a twelve-step scale. Below the headline verdict, the paper offers granular predictions across categories such as travel, romance, business, lost items, illness and the safety of awaited persons. The Japanese on the slip is often classical and abbreviated, the kanji frequently obscure even to native readers. Bring a translation app. Nobody will judge you for pointing your phone at it.

## The Pine Tree Loophole

Here is the part that quietly defines the entire practice.

If your fortune is good, you keep it. Fold it, slide it into your wallet, take it home. The blessing is portable and you are now carrying it around.

If your fortune is bad, you do not have to keep it. You tie it — physically, with your hands, in a small tight knot — to a designated pine tree or a length of rope strung between posts somewhere on the shrine grounds. The site is called the **Musubi-no-ki (結びの木)**, the "tying tree." The verb 結ぶ, *musubu*, means both "to tie" and "to bind a connection," which is the entire pun.

The folk-theological logic is that the misfortune stays bound to the tree, and you walk away unencumbered. Pine — *matsu* (松) — also puns with the verb "to wait" (待つ), so an additional reading is that bad luck has to wait there indefinitely while you leave. Japanese ritual frequently runs on homophones. It is best not to overthink it.

The visual result is a tree or rope thickly fringed with thousands of small white papers, refreshed by shrine staff on a regular schedule. Photographs do not really convey the texture. The papers move in the wind, slightly, all at once.

## Sensoji and the Famously Brutal Odds

Most shrines tilt their omikuji distributions toward the upper end of the ladder. The unofficial national average for negative draws sits around 10 percent.

Sensoji, in Tokyo's Asakusa district, runs at roughly 30 percent. One in three omikuji drawn at Japan's most-visited Buddhist temple is some form of curse.

The temple's official position is that the distribution faithfully preserves the original Edo-period ratios and was simply never softened. The practical effect, for visitors, is that the Musubi-no-ki at Sensoji is one of the most densely occupied rope structures in the city. It is also, not coincidentally, one of the most photographed.

Sensoji's bell-shaped Kannon-prayer omikuji — the canister itself is cast in the shape of a temple bell — is the iconic image of the practice. Most travel guides include a photograph. Few mention the odds.

## A Short Tour of Notable Omikuji

Once you start paying attention, you discover that omikuji are a competitive design category.

- **Hie Jinja (Tokyo)** sells a small wooden monkey-shaped charm omikuji for around ¥800. The monkey is the shrine's messenger animal. The fortune is folded inside. People keep the monkey regardless of the verdict.
- **Heian Jingu (Kyoto)** is widely considered to produce the most beautifully calligraphed fortune slips in the country. The paper itself feels different. Collectors exist.
- **Yushima Tenjin (Tokyo)** is dedicated to Sugawara no Michizane, patron deity of scholarship. Its omikuji are heavily weighted toward academic outcomes, and in the weeks before university entrance exams the shrine fills with high schoolers drawing them in nervous batches.
- **Meiji Jingu (Tokyo)**, in a quiet concession to its international visitors, offers English-language omikuji. So do a handful of Kyoto temples. The translations are competent and the bad fortunes are equally bad in both languages.
- Various **Pokemon Centers and anime-collaboration shrines** now produce themed omikuji featuring Pikachu, character art and franchise-specific blessings. The mechanics are identical. The aesthetics are not.

## Cultural Reading, and How Not to Embarrass Yourself

A 2019 NHK survey found that roughly 75 percent of Japanese respondents had drawn an omikuji within the past year. This makes it one of the most widely practiced rituals in the country, comfortably ahead of formal shrine visits outside of New Year's.

What is interesting is that very few participants describe themselves as religious. The omikuji occupies a category that English does not really have a word for: ritualized hope, performed earnestly, without requiring metaphysical commitment. You draw the slip. You read it. If it is bad, you tie it to the tree, and the tying is the part that matters. The act discharges the anxiety. The theology is optional.

The closest cousin ritual is the **ema (絵馬)**, the small wooden plaques on which visitors write wishes and hang in racks near the main hall. Same shrine, same logic, different stationery.

For foreign visitors, the etiquette is genuinely simple and worth getting right:

1. Drop a ¥100 coin (or whatever the posted price is) into the wooden offering box. Do not negotiate.
2. Shake the canister gently until one numbered stick emerges. Do not force it.
3. Tell the staff member your number, or find the corresponding drawer.
4. Accept the folded paper with both hands if you can manage it.
5. Read it. Translate if needed. Do not tear it up, even theatrically, even as a joke.
6. If it is positive, take it with you. If it is negative, walk to the Musubi-no-ki and tie it on, using one hand if you can — folklore says one-handed tying takes more effort, which makes the binding stronger.

That is the entire procedure. A thousand years of refinement, a coin, a stick, a slip of paper, and a tree full of other people's bad days quietly absorbing your own.

## TL;DR

Omikuji are paper fortunes sold at Japanese shrines and temples for ¥100 to ¥500. Good ones go home in your wallet. Bad ones get tied to a designated pine tree so the misfortune stays behind. Sensoji in Asakusa famously hands out a curse to roughly one visitor in three. Roughly three-quarters of Japanese adults drew one in the past year.
