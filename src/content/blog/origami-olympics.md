# Japan Has Origami Olympics. The World Champion is 9 Years Old.

A nine-year-old in Saitama can fold a 1,400-step dragon from a single uncut square. He has a coach. He has a sponsor. He has, somewhere on his bedroom shelf, a small trophy with a paper crane on top. He is not unusual. He is the third-best in his prefecture.

This is one of those things Japan does quietly — without fanfare, without irony, and without any apparent awareness that the rest of the world finds it remarkable.

## The Modern Competitive Scene

Origami stopped being a children's craft sometime in the 1950s and nobody told the foreign press. Today there is a recognizable competitive circuit: regional folding meets, juried exhibitions, technical-design awards, and an unspoken hierarchy of who is fast, who is precise, and who is inventing the next generation of crease patterns. Competitive origami rewards two things — original design and clean execution — which is roughly the same rubric used for figure skating and architecture.

The current rules of the game were set, more or less single-handedly, by **Akira Yoshizawa (1911–2005)**. He is the father of modern origami in the same way Cézanne is the father of modern painting: everyone after him is, in some sense, working in his shadow. Yoshizawa published roughly 50,000 original designs over his lifetime, invented the wet-folding technique that makes paper curve like sculpted clay, and co-developed the Yoshizawa-Randlett diagramming system — the standardized arrows and dashes that allow a folder in Lyon to read a folding sequence from a folder in Sendai. Before Yoshizawa, origami instructions were essentially folk knowledge passed hand to hand. After Yoshizawa, origami was a literature.

He died in 2005, on his 94th birthday, with paper on the table.

## The Conventions Nobody Mentions

There are two major conventions that anchor the global calendar, and most travelers in Japan have walked past both without noticing.

The **Nippon Origami Association** has organized Japan's annual Origami Convention in Tokyo every year since 1981. It now draws roughly 3,000 attendees — folders, teachers, paper merchants, and a non-trivial number of mathematicians. Sessions run from morning until late evening. There is no opening ceremony. People simply arrive, unfold their kits, and begin.

The American counterpart, **OrigamiUSA's annual convention** in New York — often referenced inside the community by its older nickname, BARF (Birds of a Feather) — pulls a different crowd: engineers, hobbyists, and a steady stream of Japanese masters who fly in as guests of honor. The two events are not officially linked, but the diagrams traded between them are how the global state of the art moves forward.

If you attend either, the first thing you notice is the silence. Three thousand people folding paper produces a sound roughly equivalent to light rain.

## The 2020 Olympics and 1,000 Cranes Per Medalist

The detail that international viewers missed during the 2020 Tokyo Olympics — held in 2021, but officially the 2020 Games — was that every single medalist received, in addition to their medal, a string of **1,000 folded paper cranes**. The set, known as a *senbazuru*, was commissioned through the **Hiroshima Senbazuru Initiative**, which coordinates the folding labor of volunteers across the prefecture.

This was not a souvenir. The senbazuru is a vow object. The tradition holds that anyone who folds a thousand cranes is granted a wish, and the modern association of that wish with peace, healing, and survival traces directly to **Sadako Sasaki**, a Hiroshima girl exposed to the atomic bomb at age two who developed leukemia at twelve and began folding cranes from her hospital bed. She died in 1955. The cranes she completed before her death are now displayed in Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park and in several museums abroad.

Giving 1,000 cranes to an Olympic gold medalist is not a cute gesture. It is one country handing another country's athlete a small, folded paper monument to its own grief, with no accompanying explanation. Most medalists put them in their luggage and went home.

## Origami as Mathematics, Engineering, and Surgery

Somewhere around the late 1990s, the people who took origami most seriously stopped being artists and started being scientists. The field now has its own academic society — the **Japan Origami Academic Society**, which publishes peer-reviewed research — and a small but influential community of researchers abroad.

**Erik Demaine** at MIT runs the Computational Origami group, which proves theorems about what folds are physically possible and which crease patterns produce which shapes. His work is genuinely fundamental: questions like "can any polygon be folded from a single sheet without cutting?" turn out to have real answers, and those answers turn out to matter.

**Robert J. Lang**, an American physicist who left a career in laser optics to fold paper full time, wrote **TreeMaker**, a CAD program that takes a stick-figure description of an insect or animal and generates the crease pattern required to fold it. TreeMaker is the closest thing origami has to a compiler.

The applications are the part that sounds invented. Researchers at Northeastern University have used origami principles to design **heart stents** that fold flat for insertion through a catheter and expand inside an artery. **NASA** has used origami-derived folding patterns for **solar arrays** on satellites — large flat structures that need to ship inside a small fairing and unfold reliably in orbit. The patent literature is full of airbags, lens systems, and architectural facades that all trace back, in their geometry, to a Japanese craft tradition.

In 2008, the documentary **Between the Folds**, directed by Vanessa Gould, became the first sustained attempt to put this entire ecosystem on film. It is still the best one-hour introduction to the field in any language.

## The Living Superstars and the One Who Died Too Soon

The contemporary masters are not anonymous. They have followings, signature pieces, and the kind of YouTube traffic normally reserved for guitar players.

**Satoshi Kamiya**, born in Nagoya, is best known for **Ryujin 3.5**, a Japanese dragon folded from a single uncut square, with individually folded scales running the length of its body. The model takes experienced folders dozens of hours to complete. Kamiya designed it in his twenties.

**Robert J. Lang**, in addition to writing TreeMaker, designs insects and animals of an anatomical precision that biologists use as reference. **Eric Joisel**, a French sculptor who died in 2010, brought a wet-folded, almost Rodin-like figurative tradition to the form; his masks and gnomes are still the gold standard for expressive origami and are held in private collections worldwide.

A short list, but a complete one. Origami is small enough as a global field that almost everyone serious knows almost everyone else.

## Where to See It, Where to Fold It, Where to Buy the Paper

Tokyo has two institutions worth a deliberate visit. The **Nippon Origami Hall** is housed inside the **Origami Kaikan** in Yushima, a working paper studio with a small museum upstairs; drop-in folding workshops run for around ¥1,000 and require no Japanese. The **Origami Museum at Narita Airport** is a quieter affair — a glass-cased collection inside Terminal 1 that most travelers see only by accident on a long layover.

For paper, the same Yushima Origami Kaikan stocks the full range of washi suitable for serious folding. **Itoya** in Ginza, the century-old stationery department store, carries a curated selection on its upper floors and will gift-wrap anything you buy.

## The 2017 Hong Kong Incident

In 2017, an international origami exhibition in Hong Kong became briefly contentious when several Japanese masters and several mainland Chinese artists disagreed, publicly and in print, over the origins and ownership of the form. The Chinese position emphasized older paper-folding traditions on the mainland; the Japanese position emphasized that origami as a codified, named, diagrammed practice is unambiguously Japanese in its modern form. The exhibition went ahead. The catalog was reprinted twice. No one apologized.

It remains the only diplomatic incident in living memory caused by folded paper.

## What's Happening in 2026

The current frontier is **3D scanning of completed folds**, paired with machine-learning systems that attempt to derive the underlying crease pattern automatically. A handful of labs in Tokyo and Cambridge are pursuing this; the goal is essentially reverse-engineering origami so that an AI can be shown a finished sculpture and asked to produce the diagram. The masters are skeptical. The grant funding, characteristically, is not.

Whether any of this matters depends entirely on whether you think a nine-year-old in Saitama folding a 1,400-step dragon is a hobby, a sport, an art form, or a branch of applied mathematics. The most accurate answer, in Japan, is that it is all four and that no one finds this confusing.

## TL;DR

Competitive origami is a real circuit with real stars, real money, and a real research literature. Akira Yoshizawa invented the modern vocabulary. The Nippon Origami Association runs the largest annual convention. Olympic medalists in Tokyo 2020 each received 1,000 folded cranes. MIT, NASA, and a Northeastern cardiology lab all use the math. You can fold paper in Yushima for ¥1,000 on a Tuesday afternoon.
