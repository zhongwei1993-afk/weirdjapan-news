# Japan's Hospitals Use Robots to Replace Bedridden Office Workers

A 1.2-metre robot rolls up to your table in a Nihonbashi cafe, sets down a ¥1,800 hand-drip coffee, tilts its head, and asks how your morning is going. The voice is human. The operator is in a hospital bed in Oita, two hours south of Tokyo, breathing through a ventilator, controlling the robot with eye movements. You are not supposed to forget this. The whole point of the place is that you don't.

This is the Dawn Avatar Robot Cafe, and it is — without much argument — the most quietly radical piece of social engineering currently happening in Japanese tech.

## The Robot Itself

OriHime (オリヒメ) is a small white desktop robot, about 23 centimetres tall, with a smooth featureless face, a camera where the forehead should be, a built-in speaker, and two articulated arms that can wave, gesture, raise in a victory pose, or cover the face in embarrassment. It looks, intentionally, like a stylised Noh mask. It is not trying to be cute in the Pepper sense. It is trying to be a vessel.

The company behind it is Ory Lab (オリィ研究所), founded in 2010 by Kentaro Yoshifuji, a Tokyo engineer who spent his own childhood housebound by chronic illness and decided, around age fifteen, that loneliness was a hardware problem. He has been working on the same idea ever since: a body you can borrow when your own one will not cooperate.

OriHime has no autonomy. There is no AI deciding what to say. Every arm wave, every "irasshaimase" comes from a real person logged in remotely through a laptop, a tablet, or — in the most advanced cases — an eye-tracking interface called OriHime Eye, which lets ALS patients type and speak by looking at letters on a screen. The package runs about ¥220,000 and is now partially covered by Japanese public insurance.

## OriHime-D and the Cafe That Hires Them

The desktop OriHime is the consumer model. The unit at the cafe is OriHime-D — the "D" stands for Dawn — a 1.2-metre wheeled version with a tray, longer arms, and enough torque to deliver a drink without sloshing it. It debuted in 2018 inside a pop-up called DAWN ver.β, organised by Ory Lab and the Nippon Foundation as a three-week experiment in Akasaka. The pop-up ran again. And again. In June 2021 it opened a permanent location on the ground floor of the Nihonbashi Mitsui Tower, ten minutes' walk from Tokyo Station, and rebranded simply as Dawn Avatar Robot Cafe.

There are roughly fifty registered "pilots" on the roster now, with more in training. They include patients with ALS, spinal cord injuries, muscular dystrophy, post-stroke paralysis, and — increasingly — people with severe mental health conditions or [hikikomori](/blog/hikikomori-1-5-million) histories who have not left their apartments in years. They log shifts from hospital wards, care homes, and bedrooms in Hokkaido, Okinawa, and everywhere in between. Pay is around ¥1,100 per hour, processed through Ory Lab as standard employment, which means for many of them it is the first taxable income they have ever earned.

The unofficial face of the project is Masayuki Doi, an ALS patient who became OriHime's first paid employee and still works shifts when his condition allows. There is footage of him operating the robot from his bed, controlling the arms with eye movements, joking with customers. They do not always know they are talking to a man who cannot speak with his own voice. They figure it out eventually. Nobody seems to mind.

## What It's Actually Like to Visit

You reserve online through dawn2021.orylab.com — most slots go a week or two in advance, particularly the "Diorama" seats, small enclosed booths where the OriHime-D rolls directly to your table for an extended conversation. The cafe takes walk-ins for the Tele-Barista counter if space allows, but reservations are safer.

The menu reads like a normal third-wave cafe — pour-over coffee, matcha lattes, a small lunch set with curry or pasta, mostly in the ¥1,200 to ¥2,000 range. The ¥1,800 signature "OriHime conversation set" is the one most people order. It includes a drink and roughly fifteen minutes of dedicated chat time with a pilot at your table. You order at the counter from another OriHime, which takes your card. A larger OriHime-D wheels over carrying the cup. The arms move. The voice comes through a speaker mounted near the face. You talk. They talk back.

It is, by the explicit design of the place, not efficient. A human server would be faster. That is the point. You are not paying for coffee. You are paying for the proof of concept that someone who has been told their working life is over still has one.

## Corporate Buy-In, Quietly

The Nippon Foundation has funded the cafe and the broader avatar program from the start. ANA has been running pilot deployments using OriHime as a remote guide at Haneda Airport since 2019, with operators answering passenger questions from home. Lawson trialled an OriHime-D at a Tokyo convenience store in 2020 as a stocking-assistance experiment. Several regional hospitals have run smaller projects. None of these have been splashy. Ory Lab does not really do splashy.

That restraint is part of why the company has survived where flashier robotics ventures have not. There is no claim that OriHime is going to replace anyone. The opposite, in fact: it exists because someone has already been replaced — by illness, by a broken spinal cord, by a society that mostly does not know what to do with people who cannot leave their homes — and the robot is how they un-replace themselves.

## The Henn-na Hotel Comparison Nobody Makes

It is worth contrasting this with the [Henn-na Hotel](/blog/robot-hotel-henna), the Sasebo property that famously fired half its 243 robots in 2019 after years of guests complaining that the autonomous concierges could not actually do the job. Or [Pepper, the SoftBank humanoid](/blog/pepper-robot-stores) deployed in storefronts and even, briefly, as a [Buddhist priest at funerals](/blog/ai-buddhist-priest-pepper), which has been quietly withdrawn from most retail floors after customers found it more annoying than helpful.

The common thread in those failures is autonomy. The machines were sold on the premise that they could replace human judgement and warmth with algorithms, and they could not. OriHime makes the exact opposite bet. It is a deliberately thin shell wrapped around a real person. The intelligence, the timing, the small social calibration that decides whether to ask about your day or leave you alone — all of that is human. The robot just gives the human a body that can be in two places.

This is, in robotics terms, called human-in-the-loop. In Japanese disability advocacy circles it is called something closer to "presence-as-a-service." There is no good English word for it yet.

## The Quiet Philosophical Case

The cafe sits inside a larger Japanese conversation about loneliness and work that has been running for at least two decades. The country has 1.5 million [hikikomori](/blog/hikikomori-1-5-million), an aging population that is shedding workers faster than it can recruit them, a culture in which identity is heavily tied to occupation, and a parallel economy of paid-companionship services — from [rent-a-friend agencies](/blog/rent-a-friend) to the broader "[soshoku-kei](/blog/herbivore-men-soshoku)" disengagement from traditional social roles — that suggests Japanese people have been quietly working on the loneliness problem from many angles at once.

OriHime is the disability-and-work corner of that map. It treats the inability to physically show up as a logistics problem rather than a moral one. The bedridden ALS patient in Oita is not being given charity. He is being given a shift, a paycheck, a name tag, and customers who remember him. The robot is the truck that delivers the labour.

Ory Lab's own framing for this, repeated in interviews, is that nobody should have to choose between being alive and being useful. It is the kind of line that would sound saccharine almost anywhere else. In a Nihonbashi cafe, watching a 1.2-metre robot deliver a coffee on behalf of a man who has not stood up in eleven years, it lands differently.

## How to Actually Go

- **Where**: Dawn Avatar Robot Cafe, 1F Nihonbashi Mitsui Tower, 2-1-1 Nihonbashi-Muromachi, Chuo-ku, Tokyo. Five minutes from Mitsukoshimae Station, ten from Tokyo Station.
- **Reservations**: dawn2021.orylab.com — book the "Diorama" or "Tele-Barista" seat at least a week ahead. The site has an English toggle.
- **Cost**: ¥1,800 for the signature conversation set; lunch sets run ¥1,200–¥2,000.
- **Hours**: Generally 11:00–18:00, closed Mondays. Verify before going; pilot schedules shift.
- **Etiquette**: Talk to the robot the way you would talk to a person. The pilot can hear and see you. They will remember the conversation.

## TL;DR

Ory Lab's OriHime is a remote-controlled robot operated by bedridden workers — ALS patients, hikikomori, paralysed adults — who clock in from home and serve coffee at a permanent Tokyo cafe for ¥1,100 an hour. The robots are deliberately not autonomous. The point is the human inside. Where Pepper and Henn-na Hotel tried to remove people from service work, OriHime puts back the ones who had been removed by illness. It is the most humane piece of robotics happening in Japan right now, and almost nobody outside the country has heard of it.
