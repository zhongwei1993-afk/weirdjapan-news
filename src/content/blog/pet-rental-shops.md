# Tokyo's Pet Rental Shops Let You Borrow a Dog for ¥3,000

On a Saturday afternoon in Shibuya, a salaryman in his thirties walks into a small shop above a convenience store, presents his driver's license, signs a one-page liability waiver, listens to a five-minute briefing on a shiba-inu named Koharu — she dislikes other small dogs, prefers the left side of the leash, will not drink from public fountains — and walks out the door holding a leash attached to someone else's dog. Sixty minutes later, he brings Koharu back, pays ¥3,000, and goes home alone to a 22-square-meter apartment where pets are forbidden by his lease. This is **rental-pets** (レンタルペット), and depending on who you ask, it is either a humane workaround for one of Japan's quieter urban tragedies or a slow-motion welfare scandal that the industry has been managing rather than solving for twenty years.

## The Business Model

The premise is unromantic. A shop maintains a small roster of animals — usually dogs, but cats, rabbits, owls, parrots, and at the high-strangeness end of the market, otters — and rents them out by the hour or day. Dogs dominate the trade because they are the only animal most customers can plausibly walk through a park without drawing security. Hourly rates in Tokyo run ¥2,000 to ¥4,000 for a small or medium dog, with discounts for half-day and full-day blocks. Cats are cheaper and almost always rented for in-shop cuddling rather than transport. Owls and otters carry surcharges and tighter restrictions, mostly because they cannot be walked and mostly because they should not, on a more fundamental level, be rented.

The customer is not buying an animal. They are buying a ninety-minute approximation of ownership.

## The Chains

A handful of operators define the category. **WANCO**, founded in 2008, runs branches in Shibuya, Roppongi, and Akihabara, and is the closest thing the industry has to a flagship — clean storefronts, English-language waivers, shiba-inu walks priced at ¥2,000 to ¥4,000 per hour, and a quiet preference for repeat customers over tourists. **Hatchikun Dog Rental** operates a smaller footprint in the western wards and tends to stock larger breeds suited for longer Yoyogi loops. **Animal Land Hideki**, the outlier, leans exotic — owls, parrots, and at various points small primates — and has been the subject of the most sustained welfare complaints in the sector. There used to be more of these shops. In 2015 the Tokyo metropolitan area supported roughly forty dog-rental operators. As of 2026, the figure is closer to fifteen, and the contraction is not over.

The shops that survived are the ones that professionalized: vet-on-call contracts, ID-and-waiver intake, fixed walking routes through Yoyogi and Ueno, and a deliberate ceiling on how many sessions any individual animal works per week. The shops that closed mostly did not.

## Who Rents, and Why

The customer base sorts into four uneven categories. The largest is **pet-less apartment dwellers** — Tokyo's standard residential lease still includes a no-pets clause by default, and an estimated majority of central-ward apartments enforce it. People who want a dog and cannot legally have one make up the bulk of weekend bookings. The second is **prospective adopters test-driving a breed** before committing to fifteen years of vet bills and walks in the rain; shiba-inu, famously stubborn, account for a disproportionate share of these sessions. The third is **tourists**, drawn by novelty and by the same instinct that fills [hedgehog cafés in Roppongi](/blog/hedgehog-cafe-roppongi) and [owl cafés across Tokyo](/blog/owl-cafe-tokyo). The fourth, and the one the industry likes to talk about, is **therapeutic outings** — care homes that book a calm older dog for a resident who used to own one, or families bringing a rented animal to an elderly relative who can no longer keep one of their own.

The therapeutic bookings are real. They are also a small fraction of the volume.

## How It Actually Works

The mechanics are deliberately bureaucratic, because the bureaucracy is what keeps the operator's license intact. A customer arrives, presents government ID, and signs a liability waiver covering bites, escape, and medical costs. A staff member runs through the dog's preferences — pace, sensitivities, food, water, other dogs — and hands over a fitted harness, a leash, water bottle, waste bags, and a laminated map marking the approved walking route, typically a 60-minute loop through **Yoyogi Park** or **Ueno Park**. Some shops fit dogs with GPS collars. Most require the customer's phone number stays reachable for the duration. At the end of the session, the dog is checked, watered, brushed, logged, and rotated off-shift. A good shop will not send the same dog out twice in a day. A less good shop will.

## The Welfare Reckoning

The animal welfare critique has been building since the mid-2010s and has, slowly, started to win. The **Japan SPCA** and the country's RSPCA-equivalent organizations have spent the last decade lobbying against the rental model on a single consistent argument: repeated handover to strangers is measurably stressful for dogs, and the stress compounds across a working life. The 2013 amendments to the **Pet Shop Animal Welfare Act** added a disclosure requirement — rental shops must now keep and produce records of each animal's rental history, including session counts and any incidents — and in practice this has been the single most effective brake on the industry. Shops that could not produce clean ledgers were quietly denied license renewals.

The most-cited closure is **PetiPet Tokyo**, which shut down in 2021 after a sustained welfare-group campaign documented animals being rented out six and seven times a day. The shop's owner disputed the figures. The license lapsed anyway. Several smaller operators have followed.

None of this means the surviving shops are above criticism. It means the floor has moved.

## The Adjacent Formats

The lower-impact cousin of the rental shop is the **animal café**, where animals stay put and customers come to them — rabbit cafés such as **Usa-pyon Asakusa**, [owl cafés](/blog/owl-cafe-tokyo), [hedgehog cafés in Roppongi](/blog/hedgehog-cafe-roppongi), and the more genuinely benign end of the spectrum, [Japan's cat islands](/blog/cat-islands-japan) and the unmanaged street-cat colonies of [Yanaka's cat village](/blog/yanaka-cat-village). The welfare math is not automatically better — owl cafés, in particular, have their own well-documented problems — but the absence of leash-handover-stranger-leash-return at least removes one layer of stress.

The other adjacent format is the **doggy daycare for actual owners**, like **HiP Daycare Tokyo**, which boards and exercises dogs whose owners work long hours. This is the model the welfare lobby would prefer the industry move toward, and the slow contraction of rental shops suggests it is, gradually, happening.

A foreign analogue worth naming: the UK's **BorrowMyDoggy** is sometimes cited as the British version of rental-pets, but it isn't. It pairs owners with vetted neighbors for walks and socialization, money does not change hands at the animal level, and no commercial inventory of dogs exists. The structural difference matters.

## The Regulatory Grey Zone

On paper, every rental operator must hold a **first-class animal-handling-business license** issued by the prefecture, with separate endorsements for boarding, training, and display. In practice, enforcement is patchy. Tokyo's metropolitan animal welfare office conducts spot inspections, but the inspector-to-shop ratio is low, and the penalties for first violations are administrative rather than criminal. The serious enforcement happens at license-renewal time, every five years, which is why the industry's contraction has come in slow waves rather than headlines.

Japan's pet ownership rate sits at roughly **17 percent of households**, per the Japan Pet Food Association's 2023 survey — well below the United States' ~70 percent — and the gap is almost entirely explained by the default no-pet clause in urban rental leases. Rental-pets exist because ownership is structurally suppressed. Fix the leases and the industry largely disappears on its own.

## What Foreign Tourists Should Actually Do

Skip it. The kindest version of this article would tell you which shop to visit; the honest version says the welfare case against rental-pets is strong enough that a one-time tourist booking is hard to defend, especially when the alternatives are good. **Yoyogi Park** runs off-leash hours where Tokyo dog owners gather on weekend mornings and most are happy to let a polite visitor say hello to their animal. Animal cafés with published welfare credentials — vet contracts, rest-day rotations, posted handling rules — are a defensible middle ground. And if the underlying impulse is loneliness in a strange city, [Japan's rent-a-friend services](/blog/rent-a-friend) are at least transacting with a consenting party, and [themed restaurants like Vampire Cafe Ginza](/blog/vampire-cafe-ginza) will absorb an evening without anyone needing to be leashed.

## TL;DR

Tokyo's pet-rental shops let you borrow a dog, cat, rabbit, owl, or occasionally an otter for ¥2,000–¥4,000 an hour. The industry exists because most Tokyo apartments ban pets and Japan's household pet ownership rate is roughly a quarter of America's. Chains like WANCO, Hatchikun, and Animal Land Hideki still operate, but the sector has shrunk from ~40 shops in 2015 to ~15 in 2026 under sustained welfare-group pressure and tighter post-2013 disclosure rules. PetiPet Tokyo closed in 2021 after a welfare backlash. Tourists should give it a pass — Yoyogi off-leash hours and credentialed animal cafés are better choices.
