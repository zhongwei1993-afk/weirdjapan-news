# The Multimillion-Yen Craft of Japanese Plastic Food

The bowl of ramen in the window has been sitting there since 2009. The broth is the right shade of brown, the chashu has a faint sheen of fat, and the noodles are suspended mid-lift on a pair of chopsticks that defy gravity. None of it is food. All of it cost more than the meal it represents — often by a factor of ten.

This is *sampuru* (サンプル, from the English "sample"), Japan's parallel food industry. It feeds no one. It generates somewhere between ten and twenty billion yen a year. And almost every piece is still made by hand.

## One Man, One Omurice, 1932

The conventional origin story credits Iwasaki Takizo, a craftsman from Gujo Hachiman who moved to Osaka in the early 1930s. In 1932, working with paraffin wax dripped into cold water, he produced a replica of an omurice — the ketchup-glazed rice-and-egg dish his wife had served him — and set it on the family table.

She reportedly mistook it for the real thing. He reportedly took this as market validation.

Within a year, Iwasaki had founded what would become Iwasaki Be-I, a company that still dominates the industry nine decades later. Department stores in Osaka began ordering wax versions of the dishes served in their upper-floor restaurants. The logic was practical: most Japanese diners in the 1930s had never seen Western food, and a written menu of "hayashi rice" or "korokke" meant nothing. A waxen replica explained the entire concept in one glance.

The whole industry exists because of a translation problem nobody ever fully solved.

## The Town That Makes Most of It

Iwasaki was born in Gujo Hachiman, a small castle town in Gifu Prefecture wedged between mountains and the clear Yoshida River. He went back. Today, the town of roughly 13,000 people produces an estimated **80 percent of Japan's plastic food** — a level of regional concentration usually reserved for things like Swiss watches or Burgundy wine.

Walk through Gujo Hachiman and the evidence is mostly hidden: small workshops behind shopfronts, a handful of factories on the outskirts, the occasional craftsman cycling to work with a tray of unfinished tempura. The town leans into it gently. Several shops on the main street sell finished sampuru as souvenirs, and a few run drop-in workshops where visitors can dip their own wax lettuce leaves or pour their own tempura shrimp. The shrimp is harder than it looks. Most people's prawns come out flat.

This is the upstream end of the supply chain. Almost every plastic croquette in every Tokyo basement food court traces back here.

## How a Bowl of Ramen Gets Made

For the first fifty years, sampuru was wax — poured, dipped, painted, and prone to melting in summer windows. The industry transitioned to PVC (polyvinyl chloride) in the 1970s and '80s, which holds color, survives sunlight, and doesn't wilt by August.

The process is closer to sculpture than manufacturing. A craftsman takes a real dish — actual ramen, actual tonkatsu — and builds a silicone mold around it. PVC is poured in, baked in an oven, and pulled out as a blank. From there, everything is done by hand: airbrushing the broth gradient, painting individual grill marks onto a steak, gluing each sesame seed onto a bun. A single ramen bowl involves dozens of separate components assembled in sequence. The noodles are extruded one strand at a time and arranged with tweezers.

A senior craftsman might spend a full day on one bowl. Apprenticeships run five to ten years before anyone is allowed to paint a final piece. The painters are the prima donnas of the industry, and they know it.

## What It Costs

The pricing reflects the labor. A single replica dish — one bowl of curry, one plate of spaghetti — generally runs **¥3,000 to ¥30,000**, depending on complexity and the number of toppings. A full-size replica cake, the kind that sits in a patisserie window, starts around **¥20,000** and climbs sharply if the icing involves piped detail. A complete window display for a mid-sized restaurant — ten to fifteen dishes, custom-matched to the actual menu — easily lands in the ¥300,000 to ¥500,000 range.

Restaurants treat this as a capital expense, amortized over a decade. A good sampuru set outlasts the chef.

## Kappabashi: The Retail Floor

If Gujo Hachiman is the factory, Kappabashi-dougu Street in Tokyo's Taito ward is the showroom. The 800-meter stretch between Ueno and Asakusa sells everything a restaurant needs to exist — knives, ceramics, signage, uniforms — and a handful of long-running shops specialize in food replicas.

**Maizuru** has been at it since 1932, the same year Iwasaki started, and its window is a rotating catalogue of whatever the company can do at the moment: dripping ice cream cones, sashimi platters, a perfect bowl of cold soba with the dipping sauce mid-pour. **Ganso Shokuhin Sample-ya** runs a parallel operation focused increasingly on the tourist trade, selling sushi keychains and bacon-and-egg phone stands alongside the trade orders. Both will custom-make a single piece for a walk-in customer. Neither will rush it.

Kappabashi is also where most foreign buyers first encounter the industry. The shops have learned to ship internationally and to price in dollars without flinching.

## The Cultural Job It Was Doing

Before smartphones, before Instagram, before the Tabelog star rating, the window display was the menu. A diner could stand on the sidewalk, point at the wax tempura set, and walk in knowing exactly what would arrive at the table. The replica functioned as a contract: this is what you are ordering, this is what you will be served, this is the portion size. Disputes were rare because both parties had agreed in advance, in three dimensions.

It also solved Japan's particular politeness problem. Walking into a restaurant, scanning a menu you can't read, and walking back out is socially awkward. Pointing at a window from the street commits to nothing.

The phone camera was supposed to make all of this redundant. It hasn't. The windows are still full, partly because old restaurants don't refurbish lightly, and partly because a glowing screen still doesn't beat a life-size plastic gyoza for stopping foot traffic.

## The Souvenir Era

The current generation of sampuru makers has noticed the export market. Workshops in Gujo Hachiman now run weekend classes — typically ¥1,500 to ¥3,000 for an hour of dipping wax tempura — that fill up months in advance with domestic tourists and a steady trickle of foreign visitors. The wax is back, just for the workshops; it's easier for beginners than PVC.

Downstream, the keychain market has quietly become a meaningful revenue stream. A miniature plastic sushi piece sells for ¥500 to ¥1,500 at Kappabashi and at airport gift shops. The same craftsmen who paint ¥40,000 ramen bowls also paint ¥800 keychain ramen bowls, sometimes from the same batch of PVC. The economics work because the molds are already made.

It is, in a quiet way, one of the more successful pivots in Japanese craft: a 1930s solution to a translation problem turned into a 2020s souvenir industry, with the original supply chain still intact and the original company still in business.

---

**TL;DR:** Plastic food displays began in 1932 when Iwasaki Takizo made a wax omurice for his wife in Osaka. Roughly 80% of the industry is now concentrated in his hometown of Gujo Hachiman in Gifu. Each piece is hand-cast in PVC and hand-painted; single dishes run ¥3,000–¥30,000, full cakes ¥20,000+, and custom restaurant sets reach ¥500,000. Tokyo's Kappabashi Street is the retail hub. The whole industry exists because pre-war Japanese diners couldn't read Western menus, and it never stopped being useful.
