# Japan Has 100+ Regional Ramen Styles. Here Are 10 of the Strangest.

Ramen is technically Chinese. That is the first sentence Japanese ramen historians will say, slightly embarrassed, before spending the next hour explaining why it is now, definitively, a Japanese food. The dish arrived through Yokohama's Chinatown in the 1880s as a cheap wheat-noodle soup served by Cantonese cooks to dock workers, was largely ignored for sixty years, and then — in the rubble of the postwar 1940s, when American wheat aid flooded a starving country — became national infrastructure. Today the Japan Ramen Industry Association (JRIA) recognizes roughly thirty distinct regional styles, and the country sustains an estimated 25,000 ramen shops, of which around 5,000 are in Tokyo alone.

## From Yokohama Chinatown to Postwar Survival Food

The origin story is unglamorous. Late-Meiji Yokohama had a Chinese quarter, the Chinese quarter had noodle stalls, and Japanese sailors started calling the dish *shina soba* — "Chinese noodles." It stayed a niche curiosity until Japan lost a war, lost its rice supply, and gained a flood of American wheat. Black-market noodle stands set up outside bombed-out train stations in 1946 and 1947, sold a bowl of hot soup for the price of a cigarette, and accidentally invented an industry. By 1950 the dish had a new, less colonial name — *ramen* — and a permanent place in the working lunch.

The cultural acceleration came in 1958, when a Taiwanese-Japanese inventor named Momofuku Ando flash-fried a brick of pre-cooked noodles in his garden shed in Osaka and called it Chicken Ramen. Thirteen years later, in 1971, his company Nissin released Cup Noodle, packaging the same idea in styrofoam so astronauts and salarymen could eat it standing up. Global consumption now runs at roughly 100 billion servings of instant ramen per year. The garden shed is a museum.

## The Big Eight You Are Expected to Know

A serious eater is supposed to be able to identify eight regional styles by sight, smell, and noodle thickness. The list is not officially codified, but every food magazine in Japan publishes a version of it, and they mostly agree.

**Sapporo (Hokkaido)** is the miso bowl. Heavy, opaque, the color of wet cardboard, finished with a curl of butter and a pile of sweet corn. The style was invented at a tiny shop called Sumire in 1955 specifically to survive a Hokkaido winter, and it still tastes that way.

**Hakata (Fukuoka)** is the tonkotsu bowl — pork bones boiled at a rolling violence for twelve to eighteen hours until the broth turns the color of skim milk and the kitchen smells like a slaughterhouse. The noodles are pencil-thin and cooked to *barikata*, almost raw. Ichiran, founded as a Hakata shop in the 1940s, now operates more than 200 locations globally and has weaponized the formula into single-seat "flavor concentration booths."

**Kitakata (Fukushima)** is the contrarian: a clear soy-shoyu broth and a flat, wavy noodle that drinks the soup. The town has been making it since the 1920s and now has more ramen shops per capita than any other municipality in Japan.

**Wakayama** is technically called *chuka-soba*, a light tonkotsu-shoyu hybrid served with a side of *saba-zushi* — pressed mackerel sushi you eat between bites of noodles. Nobody outside Wakayama orders the fish. Everyone inside Wakayama insists you must.

**Onomichi (Hiroshima)** uses a tare built around chunks of rendered pork-back fat that float on the surface like tiny lily pads. The style dates to 1947 and is best eaten while staring at the Inland Sea.

**Hakodate (Hokkaido)** is the elegant one — a clear *shio* broth, often built on kombu and seafood, the noodles thin and straight. It is what people order when they want to remember that ramen can be quiet.

**Kumamoto** is tonkotsu, but with a slick of black-garlic oil — *mayu* — burned in a wok until it tastes like a campfire. The bowl looks ominous and tastes like comfort.

**Yokohama Ie-kei** ("house style") splits the difference: pork broth, soy tare, thick noodles, a sheet of nori, and a slab of pork-back chashu. Yoshimuraya invented it in 1974, the suffix *-ya* ("house") became a chain marker, and now every prefecture has at least one Ie-kei spinoff.

## Tokyo Is Not a Style, Tokyo Is a Marketplace

Tokyo does not have a single ramen identity, which is the Tokyo identity. The city contains Asakusa-style old-school shoyu shops that have not changed their recipes since 1953, and it contains Rokurinsha in Tokyo Station, which arguably invented modern *tsukemen* — dipping noodles served cold next to a tiny cup of concentrated broth — when it opened in 2005 and triggered a fifteen-year national craze. (The original Rokurinsha relocated to the station's "Ramen Street" in 2009 and has had a queue ever since.) A typical bowl across the city runs ¥800 to ¥1,500. The chains layer on top: Ippudo, Hakata Tenjin, Tsujita (the Shinjuku flagship is the foreign-tourist default), Tsukemen Yasubei, plus the now-global Ichiran. Underneath the chains is a long tail of one-counter shops where the owner has been making the same broth since 1986 and would prefer you did not photograph it.

## The Cultural Elevation Nobody Saw Coming

For most of its history ramen was working-class food. Then, in 2015, a Tokyo shoyu shop called Nakiryu in Otsuka was awarded a Michelin star, and the genre quietly relocated to a different shelf. Dozens of bowls now appear in Michelin's Bib Gourmand list each year. Ramen schools have proliferated in parallel — Ginza Hakkei runs a certified instructor course where you can become, on paper, a *ramen-sho* — and the country supports a steady industry of cookbooks, manga, broth-clarity TikTok, and at least one annual ranking war between food magazines.

There are also museums, because of course there are. The Shin-Yokohama Ramen Museum has run since 1994 in a faithfully reconstructed 1958 Tokyo street, with rotating regional shops in the basement and a ¥500 admission. Momofuku Ando's Cup Noodle Museums in Osaka-Ikeda and Yokohama let visitors design their own cup. Neither establishment finds this strange.

## Seasonal, Regional, and Slightly Unhinged Variations

The big eight are the textbook. The actual map is messier. Asahikawa in northern Hokkaido does a *reimen*-adjacent cold ramen built for August. Morioka's *jajamen* is barely soup. Summer brings kimchi versions, tomato-and-cheese fusions, and the seasonal lemon ramen that food magazines re-discover every June as if for the first time. Toyama black ramen is genuinely black — heavy on soy and pepper, originally fueled by laborers who needed the salt. There is a curry ramen belt in central Hokkaido. There is a stamina ramen, raw garlic optional, that is illegal to eat before a meeting. Most of these styles are run by exactly one or two shops and would collapse if either owner retired.

At home, the ¥800 Marutai stick-noodle kits from Kyushu have quietly become a national pantry item, and supermarket fresh-noodle ramen sales have grown every year since 2019. Convenience-store chilled ramen is now good enough that food critics review it without irony.

## How to Eat It (Loudly)

A few practical notes. Slurping is required, not optional — pulling air across the noodles cools them and aerates the broth, which is what every restaurant guide will tell you, but the deeper reason is that silence is read as disappointment. Eat fast: a bowl of ramen is engineered to be finished in seven to ten minutes, before the noodles dissolve into the soup. Drink the broth or don't — both are fine, though Hakata regulars routinely order *kaedama*, a second helping of noodles dropped into the leftover soup. Pay at the vending machine on the way in if there is one. Do not tip. Twenty-four-hour shops exist in every major city, and a 3 a.m. bowl after the last train is its own genre of memory.

## TL;DR

Ramen is a Chinese noodle soup that Japan imported in the 1880s, ignored for sixty years, adopted as postwar survival food, industrialized through Nissin in 1958, fragmented into ~30 JRIA-recognized regional styles, monetized into a Michelin-grade national obsession, and now serves an estimated 100 billion instant bowls a year worldwide. The big eight — Sapporo, Hakata, Kitakata, Wakayama, Onomichi, Hakodate, Kumamoto, Yokohama Ie-kei — are your entry exam. Tokyo is the graduate seminar. Slurp loudly.
