# Inside Japan's Robot Hotel — Where the Receptionist is a Velociraptor

In July 2015, a hotel opened on the grounds of a Dutch-themed amusement park in western Kyushu and was promptly entered into the Guinness Book of World Records as the first hotel staffed by robots. Four years later, its owner publicly admitted that half the robots had been making more work for the humans, and they were quietly being decommissioned. Both of these things are true, and somehow neither of them killed the brand.

This is the story of the Henn-na Hotel — 変なホテル, literally "Strange Hotel" — and what happens when a country famous for hospitality decides hospitality is a software problem.

## The hotel that put a velociraptor at the front desk

The original Henn-na Hotel opened on 17 July 2015 inside **Huis Ten Bosch**, the full-scale replica Dutch village that occupies a corner of Sasebo, Nagasaki Prefecture. Huis Ten Bosch is already one of the stranger places in Japan — a 152-hectare theme park built around canals, windmills, and a 1:1 reproduction of a Dutch royal palace — so a robot hotel arriving in the parking lot did not feel especially out of register.

The hotel was the project of **Hideo Sawada**, chairman of the travel conglomerate H.I.S., operating through the subsidiary that would become H.I.S. Hotel Holdings. Sawada's pitch was unsentimental and almost entirely about cost: Japan's hospitality industry was running out of people, robots did not call in sick, and a 72-room hotel could in theory be run with fewer than ten human staff. Opening rates started around **¥9,000 per night**, low enough to undercut nearby business hotels and high enough to suggest you were paying for something more interesting than a futon.

What you were paying for, at check-in, was a pair of animatronic **velociraptors** in bellhop bow ties. The dinosaurs lived behind the front desk and walked guests through a touchscreen check-in process. Beside them, a humanoid receptionist robot bowed in Japanese, English, Chinese, and Korean. There was also, for reasons no one ever quite explained, a separate fish tank.

## The robot staff, in full

At its 2015–2018 peak, the property listed **243 robots** on the payroll. The headline names:

- **The velociraptor receptionists**, marketed as "dinosaur staff" and almost universally the first thing guests photographed.
- **Churi**, an egg-shaped in-room concierge with the silhouette of a tulip bulb and the job description of an Amazon Alexa. Churi sat on the nightstand, controlled the lights, answered questions about the weather, and listened — always listened — for wake-up requests.
- **Cocoro**, a stubby wheeled porter that ferried luggage from the lobby to the room along a fixed track in the corridor.
- A cloakroom robot arm that stored bags behind a glass partition, like a small industrial museum exhibit.
- A facial-recognition system that replaced room keys with your own face, which guests found either delightful or mildly threatening depending on jet lag.

The branding was consistent: every robot had a name, a bow, and a slightly-too-cheerful default expression. The aesthetic was less Black Mirror, more children's science museum after closing.

## The 2019 firing

By early 2019, something had gone visibly wrong. Sawada — to his enormous credit — went on the record about it. In interviews with the *Wall Street Journal* and Japanese business press, he acknowledged that roughly **half of the 243 robots** had been decommissioned because they were generating more work for human staff than they eliminated.

The catalogue of failures has since become a small parable inside the Japanese hospitality industry:

- **Churi**, the in-room concierge, had a habit of waking guests in the middle of the night. Its voice-activation system was mistaking snoring for commands and answering questions no one had asked. Guests would jolt awake to a cheerful "I'm sorry, I didn't catch that — could you repeat?"
- The **multilingual check-in robots** struggled badly with regional accents. A guest from Osaka, a guest from Glasgow, and a guest from Guangzhou could all defeat the same machine in different ways, after which a human would be summoned to type the booking in by hand.
- The **velociraptors** could not photocopy passports — a legal requirement for foreign guests in Japan — so a human stood next to them for the entire check-in process anyway.
- The **luggage porter** got stuck on uneven flooring, refused to operate in snow, and was unable to deliver to rooms on certain upper floors because the elevator interface had not been updated.
- Maintenance staff reported that the robots required **constant, often nightly software patches**, and that older units were going out of warranty faster than they were depreciating on the balance sheet.

Sawada's quoted conclusion was, paraphrased: the robots had not made the hotel more efficient, only more famous. The decommissioning was reported in English-language press as "Japan's robot hotel fires half its robots," which is both accurate and slightly unfair — the hotel didn't fire them so much as politely move them into storage and stop replacing the ones that died.

## Expansion anyway

The strangest part of the Henn-na story is what happened next: the chain kept growing. By 2026, H.I.S. Hotel Holdings operates **around 17 Henn-na properties**, most of them business hotels in transit-friendly locations rather than theme-park novelties. The current map includes **Tokyo Akasaka, Tokyo Hamamatsucho, Osaka Shinsaibashi, Kyoto Hachijoguchi, Maihama** (next to Tokyo Disney Resort), and **Haneda Airport**, among others.

The newer properties are quieter. The velociraptors are mostly confined to the original Huis Ten Bosch flagship and a couple of branches that lean into the gimmick. The Tokyo and Osaka business hotels still have automated check-in kiosks and the facial-recognition keys, but the in-room Churi units are gone or optional, replaced by a tablet on the desk. A handful of "demo" robots stay on duty in the lobby for photographs, less staff than mascot.

Room rates have climbed in line with the rest of Japan's post-pandemic accommodation market. As of 2026, a night at a Henn-na property runs roughly **¥6,000 to ¥15,000**, depending on city, season, and how close you are to a Disney park.

## What guests actually say

The aggregated review trend, across Booking.com, Jalan, and Google Maps, is remarkably consistent across years. Reactions fall into three groups:

- **Charming.** First-time guests, especially with children, describe the experience as fun, photogenic, and good value. The velociraptors photograph well. The facial-recognition entry feels like a small piece of science fiction. Nobody minds that the lobby is empty of humans.
- **Uncanny.** Solo travellers tend to flag the silence. Hotels in Japan are normally staffed to a degree that borders on theatre — a person at the door, a person at the desk, a person at the lift. Replacing all of that with a tulip-shaped speaker and a dinosaur, in the dark, on a weekday in winter, is reliably described as "a bit much."
- **Laggy.** Across every branch, the most common complaint is the same: the technology is two or three product generations behind what guests use at home. Voice recognition that struggles with Australian accents. Touchscreens that need a second tap. A check-in flow that, in theory, takes ninety seconds and, in practice, takes seven minutes plus a phone call.

None of this has dented occupancy in a meaningful way, which is itself a small piece of evidence about why the hotel exists.

## The lesson, such as it is

Henn-na is sometimes held up as proof that automation in hospitality doesn't work. That isn't quite right. What it actually proves is more specific and more useful: automation has a **hidden labour cost** that nobody puts on the spreadsheet at the planning stage. Robots break. Robots need patching. Robots can't photocopy passports. Robots wake guests up at 3 a.m. because someone snored in the key of "Churi, lights on."

The hotel's quiet pivot — from robot-staffed novelty to mostly-normal business hotel with a robot mascot in the lobby — is, in its own dry way, one of the most honest things a Japanese chain has done about technology in the last decade. The Guinness record still stands. The velociraptors are still bowing. Half the staff has been replaced by humans. The brand survived all of it.

That, more than the dinosaurs, is the strange part.

## TL;DR

- **Henn-na Hotel** (変なホテル, "Strange Hotel") opened July 2015 inside Huis Ten Bosch in Sasebo, Nagasaki, with **243 robots** on staff and a Guinness World Record for being the first hotel run by them.
- Operated by **H.I.S. Hotel Holdings** under chairman **Hideo Sawada**. Original room rate around ¥9,000/night.
- Star robots: **velociraptor receptionists**, the egg-shaped in-room concierge **Churi**, and the **Cocoro** luggage porter.
- In 2019, Sawada admitted **roughly half the 243 robots** were creating more work for humans than they eliminated and quietly decommissioned them. Churi was waking guests up by mistaking snoring for voice commands.
- The chain still expanded to **~17 properties**, including Tokyo Akasaka, Hamamatsucho, Osaka Shinsaibashi, Kyoto Hachijoguchi, Maihama, and Haneda.
- 2026 status: still open, mostly human-run, with a small demo robot crew. Rooms now **¥6,000–¥15,000**.
- The real lesson is about the hidden labour cost of automation, not about robots being bad at being polite.
