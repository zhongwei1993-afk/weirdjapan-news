# Japan's 'Crying Clubs' Where Men Pay ¥7,000 to Sob in Public

On a Tuesday evening in a beige-carpeted Shibuya community room, twenty-three adults — most of them men in office shirts, most of them between 35 and 55 — are watching a short film about a dog waiting at a train station for an owner who is never coming back. By minute six, the room sounds like a wind tunnel of sniffles. By minute nine, someone in the second row is openly sobbing. At the front of the room, a man in a cardigan walks quietly between the chairs, distributing premium tissues with the brisk competence of a flight attendant during turbulence.

This is **rui-katsu** (涙活), literally "tear-seeking." It is a paid, scheduled, two-hour activity. It costs between ¥3,000 and ¥7,000. People book it weeks in advance.

## What Rui-Katsu Actually Is

Rui-katsu sessions are organized group crying events. The format, refined over more than a decade, is almost industrial in its consistency: attendees sit in a darkened room, a facilitator screens a curated playlist of sad short films, plays selected music, and sometimes passes around photographs or letters chosen for their emotional charge. The goal is not catharsis as a side effect. The goal is catharsis as the entire deliverable.

The term was coined around 2013 by **Hidefumi Yoshida**, a former high-school counselor who now runs the Tokyo Cry Clinic and bills himself, without visible irony, as Japan's leading "tear teacher." His original observation was simple: he noticed that students who cried in his office tended to leave looking lighter. He decided to scale the phenomenon. Roughly thirteen years later, he runs sessions in Tokyo, advises corporate HR departments, and has trained a small national guild of certified specialists.

A standard session lasts two hours. The price covers the venue, the curated content, the tissues (always good ones), and a brief facilitated debrief at the end. Nobody is forced to cry. Nobody is forced to talk. The social contract is unusual: you are paying for the right to weep in front of strangers who have agreed, in advance, not to find it strange.

## The Science Behind the Sob

Yoshida's pitch is not purely emotional. He leans heavily on a body of research from **Toho University** and other Japanese institutions suggesting that emotional crying triggers a measurable shift from sympathetic to parasympathetic nervous-system activity — the same physiological gear-change that happens during deep sleep. Cortisol drops. Heart rate slows. Self-reported mood improves for several days afterward.

Yoshida likes to claim, in interviews, that a two-hour cathartic cry can deliver roughly **a week's worth of stress relief**. This number is generous, scientifically speaking, and he is the first to admit it is more of a marketing heuristic than a peer-reviewed finding. But the underlying mechanism is reasonably well supported: emotional tears differ chemically from basal and reflex tears, and the act of weeping does appear to do something genuinely regulatory to the human stress system.

It is worth briefly noting the **tear taxonomy** here, because Yoshida insists on it. Humans produce three kinds of tears. *Basal tears* lubricate the eye. *Reflex tears* respond to onions, dust, and wind. *Emotional tears* — the ones rui-katsu is interested in — contain higher concentrations of stress hormones and protein-based painkillers, which is the strongest available argument that crying is, on a chemical level, a flushing mechanism.

## The Namida-Sommelier

Around Yoshida's clinic has grown a small, slightly absurd professional ecosystem. The most photogenic part is the **namida-sommelier** — literally "tear sommelier" — a certified specialist trained to assess a client's emotional state and prescribe the appropriate crying stimulus. A breakup calls for a different short film than a fatigued mid-career engineer. An overworked nurse needs a different playlist than a recently divorced accountant.

There are roughly thirty certified namida-sommeliers across Japan. They are trained, accredited, and listed on Yoshida's site. The job exists. People have business cards.

This is the part where foreign coverage usually pivots to mockery. The Japanese press, by contrast, mostly covers them the way it would cover any other niche specialist — with respect, a clear price list, and a photograph of the practitioner looking competent in a clean shirt.

## Corporate Adoption

Rui-katsu has quietly moved into corporate wellness. **Recruit Holdings**, **CyberAgent**, and **Nikkei** have all run trial sessions for staff, framed as stress-management workshops rather than group therapy — a distinction that matters in a culture where formally seeking therapy still carries weight. HR departments book Yoshida or one of his trainees, an in-house conference room is darkened, and middle managers cry for two hours on the clock.

The corporate logic is straightforward. Japan loses an estimated **¥7 trillion** per year in lost productivity to mental-health-related absence and presenteeism, by Health Ministry estimates. Against that backdrop, ¥200,000 for a workshop that produces visibly lighter employees on Wednesday morning is not a hard sell to a CFO.

## Why Japan, Specifically

You cannot write honestly about rui-katsu without writing about the cultural backdrop, which is heavier than the sessions themselves.

Japan has a stoic-male norm that runs deep. **Gaman** — the cultural value of quiet endurance — is taught in childhood and reinforced in offices. Crying in public, especially for men, has historically been read as a failure of composure. At the same time, Japan records around **21,000 suicides per year**, per Health Ministry data, with the highest rates concentrated in men aged 20 to 50 — the same demographic that fills most rui-katsu sessions. **Karoshi** (death from overwork) remains a recognized cause of death with its own Health Ministry prevention bureau. The Labor Ministry's suicide-prevention guidelines have, since the late 2010s, explicitly encouraged employers to consider "emotional release" workshops.

In that context, a structured, socially sanctioned, ticketed event where it is officially acceptable to weep is less of a curiosity and more of a piece of soft infrastructure. It exists because the alternative — not crying at all — has a body count.

Worth noting: Japan has roughly **8 psychologists per 100,000 people**, compared with around 28 per 100,000 in the United States, per WHO figures. Therapy, where it exists, is often expensive, lightly insured, and culturally awkward. A two-hour group crying session for ¥3,000, no diagnosis required, no chart opened, slots neatly into that gap. (Anyone in Japan in acute distress can reach **TELL Lifeline**, an English-language support service, at 03-5774-0992.)

## Venues, Triggers, and Spin-Offs

Sessions run most often in **Shibuya, Yokohama, and Osaka community centers**, with occasional premium editions staged at hot-spring resorts — onsen weekends marketed as "cry-and-soak" retreats, which sound like satire and are not. The standard programming menu includes three reliable categories of trigger: **animal-reunion videos** (dogs and owners after long separations, almost always), **war-survivor interviews** (a specifically Japanese sub-genre with deep emotional reach for older attendees), and **Studio Ghibli scenes** — Grave of the Fireflies being the nuclear option, deployed only on advanced groups.

The format has spawned variants. **Women-only sessions** address the (correct) observation that mixed groups skew the dynamic. **Crying dating events** pair singles after a shared cry, on the theory that emotional vulnerability accelerates intimacy more efficiently than small talk. **Corporate retreats** combine rui-katsu with team-building exercises, which is exactly as awkward as it sounds and, by most reports, exactly as effective.

## Does It Actually Work

The honest answer is: probably, for a while.

Short-term, the evidence is reasonably strong. Attendees report feeling lighter for several days. Sleep improves. Self-reported anxiety drops. The acute physiology — the parasympathetic shift, the cortisol dip — is real.

Long-term, the evidence is thinner. There is no serious claim that rui-katsu treats clinical depression or replaces therapy. Yoshida himself, to his credit, says this in nearly every interview: rui-katsu is maintenance, not medicine. The skeptics' position — that this is a structured feel-good ritual rather than a clinical intervention — is basically correct, and basically beside the point. The same critique applies to massage, yoga, and going for a walk.

## How to Actually Attend One

Booking is mostly done through Yoshida's official site or directly through community centers running affiliated sessions. English-language support is limited; a Japanese-speaking friend or a translation app will close the gap. Walk-ins are occasionally accepted in Tokyo but not relied upon. Bring cash. Wear something you do not mind crying onto. Eat lightly beforehand — the parasympathetic crash after a good session is real, and a heavy meal in the stomach turns the post-cry calm into a nap.

## TL;DR

Rui-katsu is a paid, two-hour group crying session, invented around 2013 by former school counselor Hidefumi Yoshida, now a small national industry with certified "tear sommeliers" and corporate clients. It exists because Japan has a stoic-male culture, limited access to therapy, and a measurable mental-health crisis among working-age men. The science is real if modest. The cultural function is significant. The tissues are excellent.
