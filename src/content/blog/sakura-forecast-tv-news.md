# Japan Has a National Cherry Blossom Forecast on the News

At 6:53pm on a Tuesday in late March, NHK pauses the regular weather segment, brings up a map of the Japanese archipelago shaded in pink gradients, and tells roughly 30 million viewers exactly which prefectures' cherry trees will open their first petals in the next 72 hours. The forecaster speaks in the same measured tone used for typhoon advisories. Nobody in the studio acts like this is unusual, because in Japan it isn't. It is the **sakura-zensen** — the cherry blossom front — and it has been an item on the national news, in one form or another, since 1955.

## The sakura-zensen, briefly

"Zensen" is the same word Japanese meteorologists use for cold fronts and warm fronts. Applied to cherry blossoms, it describes the line that creeps northward across Japan from late January to early May as the **somei-yoshino** cultivar (Japan's reference variety, roughly 80% of cultivated cherry trees) reaches blooming temperature in each region.

The front is not a metaphor. It is mapped, forecast, revised, and broadcast like any other weather system. Six private and public organizations issue competing predictions. Travel agencies build packages around them. Sake brewers schedule deliveries. Office workers reserve tarp space in city parks two weeks in advance, sometimes using a junior employee as a placeholder for eight straight hours. The forecast is infrastructure.

## From government bureau to private competition

The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) ran the official national forecast from 1955 through 2010. For 55 years, government meteorologists walked to designated trees, counted open buds, and issued bulletins. In 2010, citing budget reasons, JMA stopped producing its consolidated nationwide forecast and handed the public-facing job to the private sector.

Today the dominant players are:

- **Weathernews Inc.**, whose "Sakuranavi" service crowdsources observations from roughly 10,000 citizen reporters and sells a premium app subscription for ¥980 per year.
- **Japan Weather Association (JWA)**, whose "Sakura Project" leans on historical degree-day modeling and partners with newspapers and broadcasters.
- A handful of regional outlets and tourism boards that issue their own numbers, often for marketing reasons.

JMA still tracks blossom dates at its specimen trees as a climate indicator. It just no longer publishes the splashy map. That has been outsourced to companies whose business model depends on getting it right.

## About 1,000 specimen trees

The forecast rests on a network of roughly **1,000 designated specimen trees** (hyōhonboku) scattered across shrines, public parks, and meteorological offices. Each tree has been observed continuously for decades, sometimes more than a century. The tree, not the city, is the data point.

A few of the more famous reference trees:

- **Yasukuni Shrine, Tokyo.** A single somei-yoshino in the shrine grounds is the official reference tree for Tokyo's bloom declaration. When that one tree opens five or six flowers, the entire capital is declared "in bloom."
- **Kyoto Imperial Palace.** The reference tree for Kyoto. Used by JMA since the postwar period.
- **Osaka Castle Park, Sapporo's Maruyama Park, Fukuoka's Maizuru Park.** Each region has its anointed tree, and the forecasters check them by hand.

This is the part most foreigners struggle with: the call is made by humans standing in front of a specific plant, holding a clipboard. There is no satellite shortcut.

## Three stages, every tree

Once a tree is being watched, it passes through three named stages:

- **Kaika (開花)** — first bloom. Officially declared when 5–6 flowers on the reference tree have opened. This is the headline date.
- **Mankai (満開)** — peak bloom. About 80% of buds open. Usually arrives 5 to 8 days after kaika in Tokyo, faster in warmer regions, slower in the north.
- **Hana-fubuki (花吹雪)** — petal storm. The window, often less than a week, when petals detach in wind and fall like horizontal snow. Photogenic. Brief. Mourned.

The whole cycle from kaika to bare branches lasts about two weeks in a normal year. The forecast exists because that window is genuinely short, and because the country has decided to take it personally.

## The 600-degree rule

The standard forecasting model is almost embarrassingly simple. Beginning February 1, meteorologists sum the daily mean temperatures of each region. When the cumulative total approaches **600 degrees Celsius**, the local somei-yoshino is expected to open. (A separate "400-degree rule" using daily maximums produces similar results.) Modern services layer in regression models, soil temperature, and previous-season bud development, but the 600-degree rule remains the backbone.

The rule has held since the 1950s. It is also the rule that climate change is steadily breaking.

## A 1,200-year temperature record, made of cherry trees

The most disquieting blossom data does not come from JMA. It comes from **Yasuyuki Aono's laboratory at Osaka Prefecture University**, which has reconstructed Kyoto's peak-bloom dates back to the **9th century** by combing through imperial diaries, monks' journals, and noble households' poetry collections. The series is one of the longest continuous phenological records on Earth, and climatologists use it as a proxy for spring temperatures across a millennium.

What the record shows: Kyoto's peak bloom hovered around April 17 for most of recorded history, drifted slightly earlier during medieval warm spells, and has accelerated sharply since 1900. The 2021 peak — March 26 — was the earliest in the entire 1,200-year series. The 2026 average across JMA's specimen trees is running roughly **five days earlier than the 1980s baseline**, and the trend is monotonic.

The cherry forecast is, accidentally, one of Japan's most legible climate dashboards.

## The economy of pink

Hanami — formal blossom-viewing — generates an estimated **¥600 billion** in domestic tourism per year, per JTB. That figure covers everything from convenience-store party platters to inbound hotel bookings to the seasonal Starbucks cup. (For the corporate side of that supply chain, see [Starbucks Japan's sakura industrial complex](/blog/starbucks-japan-sakura/).)

Where the money goes, roughly:

- **Ueno Park, Tokyo.** Free. Crowded by 9am. The default.
- **Yasukuni Shrine.** Free, contested historically, but home to the city's reference tree.
- **Inokashira Park, Kichijoji.** Boat rental available. Couples mandatory.
- **Chidorigafuchi, near the Imperial Palace.** Rowboats ¥500 for 30 minutes. The single most-photographed sakura location in Japan, partly because the moat reflects the petals on the water.

## Yozakura, NHK, and the apps

After dark, many of the major sites switch on illumination for **yozakura** — night cherry blossoms — turning the trees pale blue, warm white, or, in less restrained venues, magenta. Meguro River's 800 lanterns are the famous case. Maruyama Park in Kyoto runs spotlights on a single weeping cherry that has been the city's centerpiece since the Meiji era.

For the broadcast layer:

- **NHK** runs daily blossom updates in the evening news, usually around 6:53pm during peak season.
- **Weathernews** streams a 24-hour sakura channel on YouTube every spring.
- **Sakuranavi** (¥980/year) and the free **tenki.jp** app from JWA both push notifications when your watchlist tree changes stage.
- **Japan-guide.com** maintains the standard English-language tracker, which is what most foreign visitors and concierges actually use.

## The grandfather trees

Beyond the somei-yoshino network sit a handful of individual celebrity trees, technically different species, all photographed obsessively:

- **Jindai-zakura**, Yamanashi Prefecture. An Edohigan estimated at **1,800 to 2,000 years old**, traditionally said to have been planted by the legendary Prince Yamato Takeru. Conservatively dated to at least 1,200 years.
- **Miharu Takizakura**, Fukushima. A weeping cherry roughly **1,000 years old**, named "waterfall cherry" for the way its branches spill toward the ground.
- **Usuzumi-zakura**, Gifu. A 1,500-year-old tree whose petals fade from pink to ink-gray as they age.

These trees have their own forecast cycles, their own pilgrims, and in Miharu's case, their own bus shuttle. They sit in roughly the same cultural category as [a 300-year-old bonsai](/blog/bonsai-300-years-old/) — living objects that have outlasted shogunates and continue to attract crowds.

## When the forecast is wrong

The forecast is usually accurate to within two or three days. When it misses, the consequences are loud.

In **2018**, an early-season warm spell caused Weathernews to issue an aggressive Tokyo forecast that was later walked back by nearly a week. Hotels in central Tokyo reported a wave of cancellations and re-bookings; tour operators rescheduled bus fleets; one wedding venue near Meguro River had to refund deposits. The post-mortem ran on prime-time news, with the forecasting company's executives bowing on camera.

The 2026 season has its own complication: **shorter bloom windows**. Warmer autumns mean buds enter dormancy less deeply, and warmer springs accelerate opening, but the petal-drop window does not lengthen to match. Several northern cities recorded kaika-to-hana-fubuki spans of under nine days this April, compressing the entire hanami economy into a sprint.

The forecast, in other words, is becoming both more important and harder to get right. Which is roughly the position the rest of climate forecasting finds itself in too.
