# Why It's Legally OK to Be Passed Out Drunk on Tokyo Sidewalks

It is 1:14 a.m. on a Saturday in Shibuya. A man in a charcoal suit is curled around a planter outside a convenience store. His briefcase is open beside him. His phone is face-up on the pavement. Nobody is robbing him. Nobody is shaking him awake. Two office workers step over his legs without breaking conversation. A police officer fifteen meters away glances over, decides he is breathing, and goes back to directing taxi traffic. By 6 a.m. he will be gone, replaced by a different man in a different suit outside a different konbini, and the cycle will begin again on Sunday night.

This is one of the few public behaviors in Tokyo that looks lawless and is, in fact, deeply regulated by custom.

## The Friday Night Geography of Passed-Out Salarymen

The phenomenon concentrates in three districts: Shibuya, Shinjuku, and Roppongi. Each has its own flavor. Shibuya runs younger — mid-twenties consultants, ad agency staff, the IT crowd around Sakuragaoka. Shinjuku skews older and more institutional: bank middle managers and government workers who walked over from the Kabukicho izakaya belt. Roppongi gets the international finance set, often passed out next to someone speaking Cantonese into a phone.

The hours are predictable. Almost nothing happens before 11 p.m. The peak window is 12:30 to 2:00 a.m., immediately after the last train pushes the drinking crowd back onto the street with nowhere to go. By 3 a.m. the truly stranded have either found a capsule hotel, a manga café, or a piece of sidewalk dry enough to commit to.

The Tokyo Metropolitan Police logged roughly **3,000 sidewalk inebriation incidents in 2023** — a figure that only counts cases where officers were called, paramedics responded, or someone actually had to be moved. The real number is much higher. Most sleeping salarymen are simply allowed to sleep.

## The Math of a Nomikai Evening

To understand the sidewalks, you have to understand the calendar that leads to them. Drinking with colleagues is not optional in most Japanese white-collar workplaces; it is the second shift. The Cabinet Office's workplace culture survey estimates that **roughly 80% of Tokyo salarymen attend a nomikai at least once a month**, with bounenkai (year-end parties) and shinnenkai (new-year parties) functioning as near-mandatory rituals each December and January.

A canonical Friday looks like this:

- **18:00 — Ichijikai (first round).** An izakaya near the office. Around ¥3,000 per person for the nomihoudai (all-you-can-drink) course. Speeches. Toasts. The boss orders the first round of beers and nobody is allowed to start before he lifts his glass.
- **21:00 — Nijikai (second round).** Smaller group, walks to a second bar. Around ¥4,000. The conversation gets honest. People say things they will pretend not to remember on Monday.
- **23:00 — Sankai (third round) or karaoke.** The committed core. Around ¥5,000. Whisky highballs. Someone sings *My Way*. Someone else cries.

By midnight, the math has done its work. Three venues, six to ten drinks, roughly ¥12,000 spent. The last train leaves between 12:00 and 1:30 a.m. depending on the line. Miss it and the choices are a ¥4,000 capsule hotel, a ¥10,000 taxi home to the suburbs, or the sidewalk.

A surprising number of professionals, presented with that menu, choose the sidewalk.

## The Senior-Junior Drinking Trap

The reason nobody just leaves at 8 p.m. is structural. Japanese workplace drinking runs on a strict senpai-kohai (senior-junior) hierarchy. The junior must accept any drink poured by a senior. The junior must pour the senior's drinks, holding the bottle with two hands, never letting the glass go empty. Refusing — especially early in your career — reads as a rejection of the team itself.

This is not folklore. Surveys by labor consultancies routinely find that younger workers cite mandatory drinking events as a top-three source of workplace stress, and HR complaints about *aru-hara* (alcohol harassment) have been a recognized category for over a decade. The unspoken rule is that you drink what is poured for you, and you stay until the senior person decides to release the group. If the senior person is enjoying himself, you are enjoying yourself, regardless of what your liver thinks.

A Tokyo Metropolitan University study in 2017 found that **23% of Tokyo workers report binge drinking at least weekly**, a rate that aligns almost exactly with the share who attend frequent nomikai. The sidewalk is not an accident. It is the predictable downstream output of the org chart.

## Why Nobody Bothers You

Here is the part that disorients visitors. A man unconscious on a sidewalk in most major world cities is a problem — for him, for passersby, for police. In Tokyo it is a category that the legal and social system has quietly accommodated.

Public intoxication is not, by itself, a crime in Japan. Police generally do not arrest sidewalk sleepers unless they are violent, blocking a doorway, or vomiting in a way that requires intervention. The cultural read is that the man is exhausted, not dangerous. He worked late, drank what his boss poured, missed his train, and is now doing the most sensible thing available: shutting down until the system reboots at sunrise.

There is also a quiet civic safety net. Passing strangers do check pulses. Convenience store clerks do call ambulances if breathing looks wrong. Taxi drivers will sometimes wake a sleeper to ask if he needs a ride. The low street-crime rate — Tokyo logged fewer robberies in all of 2023 than some single U.S. precincts log in a month — means wallets and phones almost always survive the night. The sidewalk sleeper is not a victim-in-waiting. He is a temporarily horizontal commuter.

Adjacent to all this is the rise of *tatsumeshi* — stand-up bars where harried workers grab a quick beer and a small plate for around ¥800 before catching the train. Tatsumeshi is the engineered alternative for people who want the social ritual without the four-hour collapse. It is growing. It is not yet winning.

## The Bill Nobody Talks About

The cultural tolerance has a price, and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare has been quietly publishing it for years. Alcohol-related health and social costs in Japan run to roughly **¥4 trillion annually**. The country records on the order of **37,000 alcohol-attributable deaths per year**, including liver disease, alcohol-related cancers, and traffic and workplace accidents. Workplace productivity losses linked to drinking — absenteeism, presenteeism, errors — are estimated at around **¥1 trillion a year** on their own.

These are not small numbers. They sit awkwardly next to the cheerful Friday-night iconography of the salaryman-on-pavement. The man around the planter in Shibuya is funny, until you look at the actuarial tables he is part of.

Mental health professionals have been pushing back for years. If you or someone you know is struggling with alcohol or work stress in Japan, the English-language **TELL Lifeline at 03-5774-0992** takes calls in English and is free to use. It is one of the few low-friction options for foreign residents who do not want to navigate the Japanese-language medical system at 2 a.m.

## The Generation That Is Quietly Opting Out

The most interesting recent shift is generational. Surveys by Japanese drinks-industry groups and the Cabinet Office both find that roughly **40% of Tokyo workers aged 20 to 30 now identify as "soft-drinkers" or non-drinkers**, compared to about 15% in the year 2000. The category *gekoshu* (people who genuinely cannot or do not drink) has gone from quietly shameful to mildly fashionable. Suntory and Asahi now run entire product lines aimed at the zero-alcohol office party.

The pandemic accelerated all of this. Between 2020 and 2022, mandatory nomikai effectively vanished. Some companies discovered that team cohesion did not, in fact, depend on a 1 a.m. karaoke session. A meaningful minority of firms — particularly in tech and foreign-affiliated finance — have since formally canceled compulsory drinking events, framing the change as part of broader karoshi-prevention and work-life-balance reforms.

The sidewalks have not emptied. But the demographic on them is aging. The man around the planter is increasingly 52, not 27.

## A Tourist's Field Guide to the Sleeping Salaryman

If you are visiting Tokyo and you encounter one of these scenes, the etiquette is simple and worth respecting:

- **Do not film him.** This is the single clearest line. Photographing or filming an unconscious stranger for social media is read, correctly, as cruelty. Locals will notice. Some will say something.
- **Do not shake him awake for a laugh.** He is having a bad night that is already documented enough by his own body.
- **Do alert someone if he seems unresponsive.** If his breathing looks wrong, if he is face-down, if there is vomit near his airway, flag a convenience store clerk or call 119 for an ambulance. The system works, but it relies on someone noticing.
- **Step around, not over.** It is a small gesture and the city runs on small gestures.

By morning he will be vertical again, smoking a cigarette outside a Doutor, drinking a hot can of Boss coffee, and pretending — convincingly, to himself most of all — that none of it happened. On Monday he will bow to the same boss who poured the drinks. The cycle is the point. The sidewalk is just where the cycle briefly touches the ground.

## TL;DR

Tokyo's passed-out salarymen are the visible end of a long pipeline: mandatory work drinking, rigid senior-junior etiquette, a last-train deadline, a low crime rate, and a police culture that treats sidewalk sleep as harmless rather than criminal. The ¥4 trillion annual health bill is the part nobody photographs. The under-30 generation is quietly walking away from the whole ritual, and the pandemic gave them cover to do it.
