# Tokyo's Public Bathhouses Are Vanishing. Here's How to Use One Properly.

You pay about ¥500, walk past a noren curtain into a tiled room full of strangers, and within ninety seconds you have made every possible etiquette mistake a foreigner can make. This is the **sento** (銭湯) — literally "coin bath" — and despite the name, it is one of the most precisely regulated rooms in Japan.

There were more than 18,000 of them at the 1968 peak. The Sento Cooperative's 2024 figures put the national count at around 3,000. The decline is steady, and it is mostly about old water heaters, old owners, and young people with showers at home. The remaining bathhouses are quietly excellent, and they will let you in for less than the price of a Tokyo coffee. They will also, politely, throw you out if you do the wrong thing in the tub.

## What a Sento Actually Is

A sento is a neighborhood public bathhouse. It is not a spa. It is not a tourist experience. It is, in the most literal sense, **municipal plumbing for people whose apartments are too small for a proper tub** — which historically meant most of Tokyo. You pay at a counter (a "bandai" if it's the old-school perch, a vending machine if it's been renovated), strip in a locker room, and bathe in a room with a row of low standing showers along the wall and one or more communal tubs against the back.

There are three institutions worth distinguishing here, because tourists conflate them constantly:

- **Sento** — neighborhood public bath, regulated price (Tokyo: ¥550 in 2024), tap water heated by gas or fuel oil, no frills.
- **Onsen** — natural hot spring, mineral water, usually rural, prices vary, often attached to inns. See our [Hakone black onsen eggs](/blog/hakone-black-onsen-eggs) piece for the geothermal cousin.
- **Super-sento** — large bathing complex, multiple pools, sauna, restaurant, rest lounge, ¥1,500 and up. Spa LaQua at Korakuen runs ¥3,000 on weekends. Manyo Club in Yokohama is similar. The famous Oedo Onsen Monogatari in Odaiba closed in 2021 and is not coming back.

The sento is the smallest, the cheapest, and the one with the rules.

## The Five Rules, in the Order You Will Break Them

Most of what follows is not optional. Some of it is enforced by the owner. The rest is enforced by the silence of ten naked retirees watching you.

1. **Wash thoroughly at the standing shower before entering the tub.** Sit on the small plastic stool, use the handheld shower head, soap up, rinse off completely. Twice if you have been sweating. The tub is for soaking in clean water, not cleaning yourself.
2. **No soap or shampoo in the tub.** Ever. Not a little. Not on a washcloth. Not by accident.
3. **Hair tied up out of the water.** Long hair goes up in a clip or a hair tie. Loose hair in the bath is the foreign equivalent of putting your shoes on the tatami.
4. **No swimsuits.** Full nudity is the rule. Sentos are gender-segregated — 女 means women, 男 means men, and if you cannot read the kanji, the noren curtains are usually color-coded (red/pink for women, blue/indigo for men). The signage matters. Walk through the wrong one and you become a story.
5. **Tattoos: assume no, then ask.** For decades, visible tattoos were a near-universal ban — a legacy of yakuza association. Since the mid-2010s, roughly **30% of sento now permit tattoos**, particularly in tourist-heavy wards. Tattoo-friendly addresses worth knowing: **Saito-yu** in Koto, **Daikoku-yu** in Maenoyu, and **Kosugi-yu** in Koenji. When in doubt, point at your arm at the front desk and wait for a nod or a head shake.

The sixth unwritten rule is the **iwato** — the small folded towel you carry. It stays out of the water. Most bathers fold it neatly and balance it on top of their head while soaking. This looks ridiculous in photos and is, nevertheless, the correct move.

## The Bathhouses Worth Visiting

A working sento is not a museum, and the regulars will notice if you treat it like one. That said, a handful of Tokyo bathhouses have become destinations in their own right, and they tend to be the ones most prepared for visitors.

- **Kosugi-yu** (Koenji, 1933) — The unofficial flagship of tourist-friendly sento. English signage, an explainer pamphlet, and a famous **kaki-buro** (electric bath) for the brave. Tattoo-friendly.
- **Daikoku-yu** (Inarichō) — Home to a celebrated Mt. Fuji mural by **Morio Nakajima**, one of the last working bathhouse mural-painters in Japan.
- **Takarayu** (Sumiyoshi, 1937) — Retro tile, koi pond in the garden, the kind of place where the owner remembers your face on the second visit.
- **Maenoyu** (Yanaka) — Traditional crane mural, old-Tokyo neighborhood, walking distance from the cemetery and the cat-heavy back streets.
- **Sakurayu** (Negishi) — Provides an English-language rules card at the counter, which is more than most.

The Mt. Fuji mural deserves its own paragraph. There are now **fewer than five working peyukashi** — the specialist painters who do bathhouse murals. Morio Nakajima and Mizuki Tanaka are the two most active. A new Fuji costs around **¥300,000** and is painted on-site, over a couple of days, while the bathhouse stays open. The tradition started in 1912 at a sento in Kanda and has been quietly dying ever since. Most owners who lose a mural to a renovation never commission a replacement.

## The Equipment, the Temperatures, the Extras

A standard sento layout, decoded:

- **Lukewarm pool** — about **38°C**. Where you start.
- **Hot pool** — about **42°C**. Where the regulars sit motionless for fifteen minutes while you turn pink.
- **Cold dip** — **14–16°C**. Thirty seconds maximum, against all instinct.
- **Electric bath (denkiburo)** — a low-current tub that makes your muscles twitch. Optional and frankly unnerving.
- **Sauna** — usually a **¥200 add-on**, often with its own wristband.
- **Jet bath, herbal bath, rotating "bath of the day"** — varies by venue.

The standard rotation is: shower, lukewarm pool, hot pool, cold dip, repeat once, then sit in the changing room with a small bottle of cold milk from the vending machine. The milk is non-negotiable, in the same way a [kotatsu](/blog/kotatsu-heated-table) is non-negotiable in February.

## Why It Matters That They're Disappearing

The sento is **nichijo** — daily, ordinary, neighborhood-scale social infrastructure. It is the place an 80-year-old widow goes on Tuesdays to see the same six people she has seen on Tuesdays for forty years. It is where two salarymen who do not know each other end up discussing the Giants game while half-submerged. It is one of the last spaces in urban Japan where strangers share a room with no phones, no transactions, and no clothes.

It is also expensive to run. **Heating fuel costs spiked through 2022–2024**, the regulated entry price barely moved, and the typical owner is now well past 70 with no successor. Each year, a few dozen close quietly. A renovated apartment block appears where the chimney used to be.

The counter-effort is the **Tokyo Sento Association's "sento ambassador" program**, which runs guided visits, stamp rallies, English-language maps, and the occasional pop-up event aimed at younger Tokyoites and tourists. It is genuinely working in a small way. Kosugi-yu is busier than it was a decade ago. So is Daikoku-yu. The math is still bad, but it is slightly less bad.

If you visit Tokyo and you are at all curious, this is a category of thing that may not exist in twenty years. It costs ¥550. It takes an hour. It is, in our considered editorial opinion, the single best non-touristy thing a tourist can do in this city.

## TL;DR

A sento is a ¥550 neighborhood bathhouse — wash at the standing shower first, no soap in the tub, hair up, no swimsuits, tattoos usually no but increasingly maybe. Tokyo had 18,000+ in 1968 and roughly 3,000 nationwide remain today. Visit **Kosugi-yu** (Koenji) for the easy on-ramp, **Daikoku-yu** (Inarichō) for the Mt. Fuji mural, **Sakurayu** (Negishi) for the English rules card. Bring coins, a small towel, and the willingness to be naked in front of strangers who do not care.
