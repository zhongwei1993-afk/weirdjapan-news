# Japanese Throw Beans at Family Members Once a Year to Banish Demons

Every February 3rd, a salaryman somewhere in Saitama puts on a red plastic horned mask, stands in his own genkan, and lets his seven-year-old daughter throw roasted soybeans at his face while screaming "Demons out, fortune in." Then everybody eats a sushi roll the size of a forearm in total silence, facing southwest. This is Setsubun, and almost nobody in Japan thinks any of it is strange.

## What Setsubun Actually Is

Setsubun (節分) literally means "seasonal division." In the old lunisolar calendar, the year was carved into 24 micro-seasons, and the four big hinge points — the eves of spring, summer, autumn and winter — were all technically setsubun. Only the spring one survived as a holiday, because Japan, like most agricultural societies, decided that the night before the new growing year was the night worth taking seriously.

The date is the eve of risshun (立春), the calendrical start of spring. Most years that lands on February 3rd. Occasionally, due to the same astronomical drift that gives us leap days, it slips to February 2nd or February 4th — 2021 was the first February 2nd Setsubun in 124 years, which delighted exactly the kind of newspaper columnists who enjoy that sort of thing.

It is not a public holiday. Banks open, trains run, schoolchildren go to school. The ritual happens around it: in living rooms after dinner, in kindergarten classrooms in the afternoon, at neighborhood shrines in the evening. The cultural weight is enormous; the official footprint is zero.

## Mamemaki: The Bean-Throwing Mechanics

The central rite is mamemaki (豆まき), "bean scattering." The required equipment is a small wooden box (masu) of roasted soybeans, called fukumame, "fortune beans." The required personnel is one volunteer to play the oni — a horned demon — and at least one thrower. In the typical household this resolves into Dad in a plastic mask getting pelted by his children.

The chant is fixed: "Oni wa soto, fuku wa uchi" (鬼は外、福は内) — demons out, fortune in. You throw beans out the open front door first, then close it firmly, then throw beans inward to invite the good stuff. There are families that do every room. There are families that just do the entryway. Nobody is checking.

Then you eat the beans. The rule is one bean per year of your age, plus one extra for the year ahead. This is charming when you are six and arithmetic-grim when you are seventy-eight, at which point most participants quietly switch to drinking fuku-cha — hot tea with three lucky beans dropped in — and calling it done. The beans must be roasted, not raw: a raw soybean that sprouts in the garden where it landed is, by folk logic, a demon taking root.

## Ehomaki: The Sushi Roll That Conquered the Convenience Store

Somewhere in the last forty years, Setsubun acquired a second ritual that has nothing to do with beans and almost certainly began as an Osaka marketing scheme: ehomaki (恵方巻き), "lucky-direction roll."

You eat a single uncut maki-zushi roll, roughly 20 centimeters long, in complete silence, while facing that year's lucky compass direction. The direction is decided by the year's Chinese zodiac heavenly stem and rotates among only four bearings — roughly north-northwest, east-northeast, south-southeast and west-southwest. You are supposed to make a wish while chewing. You are not supposed to put the roll down. You are not supposed to talk. Watching a family of five do this simultaneously at a kotatsu is one of the more unnerving sights in Japanese domestic life.

The convenience store chains noticed. Seven-Eleven, Lawson and FamilyMart now move an estimated 30 million ehomaki between them every Setsubun, in flavors that have drifted far from tradition: roast beef, tiramisu, whole-lobster. The same chains also throw out, according to MAFF (Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries) survey data, around ¥600 million worth of unsold rolls each year. The waste became enough of a scandal that MAFF began publicly nudging chains toward pre-order-only models around 2019. Compliance has been, predictably, partial.

## The Big Shrine Events and Their Famous Throwers

Households do the small version. Shrines do the spectacle. The format is the same — beans flung at demons — but scaled up, with celebrities replacing dads.

The headliners:

- **Sensoji, Asakusa (Tokyo)** — Throws happen at 11:30am and 2:15pm on February 3rd, and the throwers include professional sumo wrestlers from the nearby stables. Watching a 160-kilogram rikishi underhand a paper packet of beans into a crowd of office workers in suits is exactly as Tokyo as it sounds.
- **Naritasan Shinshoji (Chiba)** — The biggest event in the Kanto region. Sumo grand champions, NHK taiga-drama actors, and whoever is currently famous enough to draw a TV crew take the platform. Tens of thousands attend.
- **Yushima Tenjin (Tokyo)** — The shrine of the god of scholarship, so the bean-throwers skew toward kabuki actors and academic dignitaries rather than athletes.
- **Yoshida Shrine (Kyoto)** — The classical version. Costumed oni are formally chased off the grounds in a ritual that looks closer to medieval theater than to a convenience store campaign.

The chosen throwers are toshi-otoko and toshi-onna — "year-men" and "year-women," people born under the current year's zodiac animal, who are considered conduits of seasonal luck. In practice this is a useful pretext for booking celebrities: any famous person whose birth year matches qualifies, and Japan has a deep bench.

## Masks, Sardines, and the Tohoku Peanut Exception

The oni masks fall into a sharp two-tier market. The konbini version is a flat ¥500 piece of vacuum-formed plastic with an elastic strap; it lives on the front-counter promotional display for about ten days in late January. The serious version is hand-carved cypress in the Noh tradition, runs ¥30,000 and up from a named craftsman, and is the kind of object that gets photographed for design magazines. Same demon, very different supply chain.

There is also a quieter household charm called hiiragi-iwashi or iwashi-no-atama: a grilled sardine head skewered on a sprig of holly and nailed to the doorframe. The theory is that oni hate the smell of burning sardine and the prick of holly leaves. The practice is that fewer Tokyo apartments do this every year, mostly because of the smell. Rural Kansai and parts of Nara still take it seriously.

Regionally, the beans themselves are negotiable. Most of Tohoku and Hokkaido throw roasted peanuts in the shell instead of soybeans. The official explanation is that peanuts are easier to spot and pick up in snow; the unofficial explanation is that nobody wants to mop the genkan twice. Miyazaki and parts of Kagoshima do the same. Once you have thrown peanuts at a demon, soybeans feel slightly inefficient.

## Where It Came From, and How to Join In

The ritual traces back to a Heian-era court ceremony called tsuina (追儺), imported from Tang China around the 8th century. The original involved a costumed official wielding a spear and a four-eyed bronze mask, ritually driving evil out of the imperial palace on the lunar new year's eve. Beans entered the picture later, in the Muromachi period — partly because of a Buddhist-tinged folk pun (mame, "bean," can also be read as "destroy the demon's eye"), and partly because beans were cheap and aerodynamic.

Modern Japan has steadily declawed the rite. The serious exorcism became a children's game; the demon became a parent volunteer; the spear became a paper packet from FamilyMart. Schools run oni-piñata versions where kids whack a paper demon for candy. Kindergarten teachers describe Setsubun as one of the most reliably popular days on the calendar, slotted just below Christmas and just above sports day.

Foreigners are welcome at almost every public event. Sensoji's 11:30am and 2:15pm throws are open to anyone who can hold a position in the crowd; bringing a tote bag is recommended, since the beans come in small paper packets and the experienced grandmothers in the front row will collect dozens. Naritasan, Yoshida and Yushima Tenjin are all free and unticketed. The only etiquette ask is that you actually shout the chant when the throwers do. Standing silently with a phone camera is technically allowed and culturally bleak.

## TL;DR

Setsubun is the eve of the old lunisolar spring, observed on or around February 3rd. Families throw roasted soybeans at a relative in a demon mask while shouting "demons out, fortune in," eat one bean per year of life, then silently consume a 20cm sushi roll facing a zodiac-determined compass bearing. Major shrines run public versions with sumo wrestlers and celebrities. The convenience stores sell 30 million sushi rolls and throw out ¥600 million worth. Nobody finds any of this unusual.
