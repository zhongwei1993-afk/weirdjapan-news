# Japanese Dress 3-, 5-, and 7-Year-Olds in Kimono Once a Year

On the morning of November 15, a three-year-old in a miniature kimono is wheeled into a Tokyo photo studio at 7:40 a.m., handed a paper bag printed with cranes, and instructed not to drag the bag on the floor because the candy inside is supposed to symbolize a long life. She does not understand this. Her parents have already spent ¥38,000. The day has barely started.

This is Shichi-Go-San (七五三, literally "seven-five-three"), and it is one of the few Japanese rituals that has survived industrialization, war, demographic collapse, and the rise of the smartphone with its core logic basically intact: at certain odd-numbered ages, children are dressed in formal clothing, taken to a shrine, photographed extensively, and given a stick of candy roughly the length of their forearm.

## The Numbers Behind the Name

Shichi-Go-San is celebrated on November 15. Girls are honored at ages 3 and 7. Boys are honored at ages 3 and 5. The asymmetry is not a typo — five is a boys' age, seven is a girls' age, three is shared, and the whole structure has been frozen in this configuration for several centuries.

There is also an age-counting problem. Traditionally, Japan used *kazoedoshi* (数え年), the East Asian system in which a baby is born at age one and adds a year on every January 1. So a child born in December turned two within weeks. Modern families almost universally use *man'nen* (満年), the Western system that counts from zero on each birthday. Some traditionalist grandparents still insist on the older count, which means a family may quietly negotiate whether their child is being celebrated at three or four. Photo studios will accommodate either. Nobody enforces a rule.

November 15 itself has no fixed religious origin. The most-cited theory is that it was an auspicious date on the old lunar calendar, chosen by the Tokugawa court in the late 17th century. In practice, almost no one celebrates on November 15 anymore. Families book the nearest weekend, often in October to avoid crowds, and the candy industry quietly extends its season from mid-September to early December.

## A Court Ritual That Escaped

The original ceremonies were three separate Heian-era court rites, performed for aristocratic children at specific ages and unconnected to one another for several hundred years.

- **Kamioki (髪置き)**, "leaving the hair," was performed around age three. Both boys and girls had their heads shaved in infancy on the theory that this produced thicker adult hair, and kamioki was the day they were permitted to start growing it out.
- **Hakamagi (袴着)**, "wearing the hakama," was performed around age five. A boy stood on a Go board, faced a lucky direction, and was dressed in his first pair of formal pleated trousers.
- **Obi-toki (帯解き)**, "untying the cord," was performed around age seven. Girls stopped wearing the soft string that held their kimono closed in early childhood and graduated to a proper *obi* sash — effectively their first piece of adult clothing.

The conventional story is that the three rites were bundled together and assigned to November 15 by the fifth Tokugawa shogun, Tsunayoshi, in 1681 to mark the kamioki of his sickly son Tokumatsu. Whether the date was really his idea or whether the court simply standardized something already drifting that way, the bundling stuck. Over the next two centuries, Shichi-Go-San migrated out of the aristocracy, through the samurai class, and into merchant households. By the late Meiji period it was a broadly middle-class custom; by the postwar economic miracle it was universal.

## What Actually Happens on the Day

The contemporary ritual is short and surprisingly informal. A family arrives at a Shinto shrine in formal dress, queues at a reception desk, pays a fee (typically ¥5,000–¥10,000), and waits to be called into the main hall along with five or ten other families. A priest waves a *haraegushi* wand, reads the children's names aloud, and recites a *norito* blessing for their continued health. The whole thing takes about twenty minutes. The family is handed a paper bag containing an amulet, sometimes a small toy, and the candy.

On November 15 itself, the major shrines absorb the load. Meiji Jingu in Tokyo, Yasaka in Kyoto, and Atsuta in Nagoya each record several hundred families in a single day, with overflow tents, dedicated photo zones, and staff in white robes directing strollers. Smaller neighborhood shrines run the same ritual at a fraction of the scale and at half the cost. Either way, the families spend more time in the parking lot than in the hall.

## Chitose-Ame and the Economics of Cute

The candy is *chitose-ame* (千歳飴), "thousand-year candy." It is a single stick of hard sugar, traditionally red and white, sold in a long paper bag printed with cranes, turtles, pine, bamboo, and plum — the standard iconography of longevity. The stick is supposed to be roughly one meter long, and the convention is that it should never be broken: the unbroken length is the wish for an unbroken life.

A single stick in a shrine-issued bag is included in the blessing fee. A boxed set from a department store runs ¥500 to ¥1,500 depending on packaging. Fukusaya in Nagasaki, Toraya in Tokyo, and the major confectioners all release seasonal versions. There is also an entire sub-market of chitose-ame-flavored Pocky, Kit Kats, and convenience-store puddings that appear from mid-October and vanish before Christmas.

The bigger expense, by an order of magnitude, is the kimono. A rental set for a seven-year-old girl — kimono, obi, undergarments, zori sandals, hair ornaments, and a small handbag — runs ¥10,000 to ¥50,000 depending on fabric and brand. Most families bundle the rental with a photo studio package, which is where Studio Alice and Studio Mario have built an empire.

Studio Alice operates more than 400 outlets nationwide, mostly inside shopping malls. Studio Mario, owned by Kitamura Camera, runs roughly 250. A standard Shichi-Go-San package — rental, hair, makeup, two to three outfit changes, and a printed album — sits around ¥30,000 to ¥80,000. Booking opens in May. By August the November weekends are gone. By October the studios are running 7 a.m. shifts.

## The Slow Contraction

Shichi-Go-San is contracting in absolute numbers, for the simple reason that Japan is running out of three-year-olds. The Cabinet Office's annual birth-rate statistics tell the story: in 1973, Japan recorded just over 2 million births. In 2023 it recorded 727,277, the lowest since records began in 1899. The 2024 figure dropped further, below 700,000. Every cohort of children eligible for the ceremony is smaller than the last, and the major shrines have begun publishing year-on-year decline figures alongside their participation totals.

The industry has responded by spreading the season, lowering the entry price, and quietly broadening what counts as "celebration." Western-style options have entered the package — tuxedos and ball gowns for the studio portion, with the kimono reserved for the shrine visit. Some families skip the shrine entirely and treat it as a photo-only event. Department stores sell coordinated parent-child outfits in non-traditional colors. The ritual is being unbundled.

A small but growing share of participants are foreign families — long-term residents, international couples, and tourists who book Shichi-Go-San experiences through agencies that advertise in English, Chinese, and Korean. The studios have multilingual staff and ready-made packages. The shrines do not check passports. A three-year-old in a kimono in front of Meiji Jingu is treated the same whether her parents are from Setagaya or Singapore.

## TL;DR

- **What:** Shichi-Go-San (七五三), a coming-of-age ceremony for children at ages 3, 5, and 7.
- **When:** Officially November 15; in practice, any weekend in October or November.
- **Who:** Ages 3 (boys and girls), 5 (boys only), 7 (girls only).
- **Origins:** Three separate Heian court rites — kamioki, hakamagi, obi-toki — bundled together in the Tokugawa period and spread to commoners by the Meiji era.
- **Cost:** ¥30,000–¥80,000 for the full studio-and-shrine package; a single stick of *chitose-ame* candy is included with the blessing.
- **Trend:** Participation is shrinking with the birth rate, but foreign families and Western-style variants are quietly filling the gap.
