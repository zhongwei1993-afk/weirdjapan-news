# Japan's Bullet Trains Are Cleaned in 7 Minutes by 22 People

A Tokaido Shinkansen pulls into Tokyo Station. Within ninety seconds every passenger is off the platform. Twenty-two people in pastel uniforms line up at the doors of sixteen cars, bow toward the train, and step inside. Seven minutes later they step back out, bow again, and the train departs on the second it was scheduled to depart — now carrying a fresh load of passengers who boarded during the five-minute window the cleaners left behind. Foreign tourists with phones held overhead have been filming this routine continuously since around 2012. The crew has long since stopped noticing.

## TESSEI, the Company Nobody Was Supposed to Notice

The company doing the work is **Tetsudo Seibi Kabushikigaisha**, almost universally shortened to **TESSEI** (テッセイ). It was founded in 1952 as a wholly owned subsidiary of what is now JR Central and JR East, with the unglamorous mandate of cleaning trains at Tokyo Station. For most of its history it was exactly the kind of operation nobody outside the building thought about: roughly 820 employees, four-day rotating shifts, contract work the parent railways treated as a cost line.

The job itself is the choreography. Each Tokaido Shinkansen arrives at Tokyo Station, terminates, and within twelve minutes is dispatched back down the line as a new service. The actual cleaning window inside that twelve minutes is **seven minutes**. The remaining five are for boarding.

A single train is 16 cars long and contains **1,323 seats**. One TESSEI cleaner is assigned per car. A team leader works the platform with a stopwatch, hand signals, and a radio. The 22-step checklist each cleaner runs through is fixed and well rehearsed: collect garbage down the aisle, wipe every seat-back tray, flip the seat-backs to face the new direction of travel (the entire car rotates 180 degrees by hand, a row at a time), replace headrest covers, vacuum the carpet, check under the seats for forgotten items, refill toilet paper, deep-clean the lavatory, wipe the door handles, reset the curtains. Each step has an expected duration. The leader's stopwatch is not decorative.

Nothing about the seven-minute number is approximate. The schedule built around it — connecting Yamanote departures, *ekiben* vendors timing carts to platform stops, taxi queues at the Yaesu exit — assumes the train will be ready.

## The Bow, the Essay, the Viral Video

The part the internet knows is the bow. Before boarding, the line of cleaners turns toward the arriving train and bows in unison. After exiting, they turn and bow again. This is not a tradition imported from anywhere; it was a deliberate operational choice by TESSEI management in the mid-2000s, partly to signal that the car was "received" and "released," partly as ceremony intended to reframe the work.

The name "**seven-minute miracle**" (七分間の奇跡, *nanafunkan no kiseki*) is younger than the routine itself. The essayist usually credited with first applying it in print is **Haruki Murakami**, in a 2008 piece for the JAL in-flight magazine in which he described the Tokyo Station turnaround as a study in collective competence. The phrase escaped containment in 2012 when a short YouTube video — a US business-news segment edited to ninety seconds and scored to mild orchestral music — picked up several million views and was re-uploaded in dozens of languages. By 2015, **Harvard Business School** had published a teaching case (*Trouble at Tessei*, by Ethan Bernstein and Ryan Buell) examining how the company had turned a low-status outsourced function into something employees competed to join.

The Harvard case is mostly the story of one person, **Teruo Yabe** (矢部輝夫), who joined TESSEI in 2005 from JR East's safety division and spent roughly seven years rebuilding the role from the inside. Yabe's recurring line in interviews is that he changed almost nothing about the technical work. He changed what the work was called and what it looked like. He gave the staff seasonal uniforms — sakura pink in spring, blues in summer, deep reds in autumn — replaced the word "cleaner" with "**Shinkansen theater crew**" in internal materials, instituted daily team meetings, and introduced an annual "Most Improved" awards ceremony at which veteran staff are publicly thanked by name. Absenteeism dropped sharply. Average tenure climbed. Applications, for a job that had previously required active recruiting, began arriving unsolicited.

The work did not become easier. Twenty-two steps in seven minutes is twenty-two steps in seven minutes. What changed was that the people doing it could explain, to themselves and to a relative at dinner, what it was.

## How the Rest of the World Turns a Train Around

The comparison numbers are the part that lands hardest with engineers.

- **Amtrak Acela**, Washington to Boston: scheduled turnaround at terminal stations of roughly **45 minutes**, often longer in practice.
- **Eurostar**, London St Pancras: around **30 minutes** for cleaning and resupply on a typical rotation.
- **ICE**, Deutsche Bahn long-distance: **20 to 30 minutes** depending on service.
- **TGV**, SNCF: similar, with a 20-minute floor at most terminals.
- **Tokaido Shinkansen** at Tokyo Station: 12 minutes total dwell, **7 minutes** of which is cleaning.

The Shinkansen achieves it partly by sheer headcount per train, partly by the 22-step protocol, and partly because the rolling stock is designed for it — the seats are mechanically rotatable as a row, the carpets short-pile, the lavatories modular. Operators elsewhere have studied the playbook. None have replicated it at scale, mostly because the labor structure does not exist outside Japan.

The comparison also flattens the fact that TESSEI cleans the same train in seven minutes whether or not it is a peak-demand service. The Mt-Fuji-side window seats — the E side of the train on Tokyo-bound services, where on a clear day Fuji is visible for about twelve minutes near Shizuoka — are the most-requested seats on the line. They receive the same 22-step pass as the unloved aisle seats on the mountain side. The protocol does not flex for demand.

## Cleaning as a National Default

It is worth situating TESSEI inside the larger frame, because the seven-minute turnaround does not happen in a country that finds cleaning unusual. Japanese public elementary and middle schools, including elite ones, do not employ janitorial staff for the classrooms. The students clean their own rooms, hallways, and toilets daily in a 15-minute slot called **o-souji** (お掃除), wearing aprons and bandanas, and the practice continues into many high schools and the occasional white-collar office. Most Japanese companies still observe a periodic **all-staff cleaning day**, in which executives and new hires wipe the same windows.

Against that backdrop, twenty-two adults cleaning a train in seven minutes is not a miracle. It is the same instinct, professionalized and put on a stopwatch. The miracle framing is almost entirely external. Inside Japan, the TESSEI crew is regarded the way a competent local bus driver is regarded: respected, useful, not the subject of a TED talk.

Tourists do treat it as a TED talk. The most common practical advice now circulating on Tokyo travel forums is to **arrive at the Tokaido Shinkansen platform at Tokyo Station roughly ten minutes before a scheduled departure**, watch the arriving train empty, the bow, and the cleaning, and then board the same train. The viewing is free, requires no ticket beyond the platform fare, and is one of the few infrastructure spectacles in the world that runs on a published timetable.

## What 2026 Looks Like on the Platform

The system absorbed two real disruptions in the last six years. The COVID-19 protocols introduced in early 2020 added a disinfection pass to the 22-step checklist — every handrail, tray table, and door handle wiped with a quaternary-ammonium solution — and TESSEI initially hired roughly 40 additional staff to keep the seven-minute window intact under the heavier workload. Most of those positions have been retained. The masks are gone from the crew uniforms as of 2024, but the wipe-down step remains.

The other change is generational. Teruo Yabe formally retired in 2015 and has since become a fixture on the Japanese business-lecture circuit, but TESSEI's current management has held the line on the cultural choices he made. The seasonal uniforms are still seasonal — the spring 2026 sakura set was unveiled in March — and the annual Most Improved awards still happen, photographed for the internal newsletter and occasionally for NHK.

The seven-minute number has not moved. The bow has not moved. Foreign tourists are still on the platform with their phones held overhead, and the crew, on the whole, still does not seem to notice.

## TL;DR

TESSEI, a 1952 JR subsidiary, cleans every 16-car Tokaido Shinkansen at Tokyo Station in 7 minutes with a 22-person team running a fixed 22-step checklist — 1,323 seats rotated, vacuumed, wiped, and restocked inside a 12-minute platform dwell. The bow at each end of the routine became globally famous after a 2008 Murakami essay and a 2012 viral video, and Harvard Business School published a 2015 case study on how former safety executive Teruo Yabe re-engineered the work into a role employees competed for. Compare 7 minutes to Eurostar's 30 and Acela's 45. The country that invented it does not think it is unusual.
