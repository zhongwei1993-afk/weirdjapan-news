# Why Tokyo's Trains Are Almost Completely Silent

A Yamanote line car at 8:42 a.m. on a Tuesday carries roughly 200 people pressed shoulder to shoulder. The loudest sound is the air conditioning. Somewhere a phone buzzes once and is silenced inside two seconds. Nobody looks up.

The Yamanote loop moves about four million riders a day. Across the wider Tokyo rail network — JR East, Tokyo Metro, Toei, the private operators — the daily figure pushes past forty million. By volume it is the busiest urban rail system on earth. By decibel, it sounds like a library with seat cushions.

This is not an accident, and it is not a stereotype. It is the result of a fifty-year project run by operators, regulators, advertising agencies, and roughly 125 million people who agree, mostly without discussing it, that the train is a quiet place.

## The cultural baseline: meiwaku

Before any poster or PA announcement, there is **meiwaku** (迷惑) — "bother," "nuisance," "trouble caused to other people." Japanese children learn the phrase *hito ni meiwaku o kakenai* ("don't cause meiwaku to others") at roughly the same age American children learn to share. It is the operating principle of public life.

Meiwaku is not politeness in the Western sense. Politeness implies a positive act — holding a door, saying please. Meiwaku-avoidance is a negative discipline: the minimization of your own footprint in shared space. On a packed train, that means your voice, your music, your bag, your perfume, and your phone all need to disappear into the background.

This is why the trains are quiet even when no one is enforcing anything. The enforcement is internal, and it was installed in childhood.

## How the operators engineered it

Cultural baseline is the soil. The operators planted on top of it.

JR East and its predecessor JNR have run formal **manner posters** since 1976 — a continuous five-decade campaign of illustrated reminders telling riders not to eat smelly food, not to sprawl, not to apply makeup, not to play music through leaky headphones, and above all not to take phone calls. The posters change monthly. They are studied by design schools. The 2008 series, which scolded specific bad-passenger archetypes ("the man who sits with his legs wide open"), is in the permanent collection of several Japanese museums.

The announcements are equally relentless. On most Tokyo lines a recorded voice reminds riders every few minutes to *keitai denwa wa manaa moodo ni setei no ue, tsuuwa wa o-hikae kudasai* — "please set your mobile phone to manner mode and refrain from voice calls." It runs in Japanese and English. It runs in tunnels. It runs at 6 a.m. and at midnight.

**Manner mode** (マナーモード, *manaa moodo*) is itself a Japanese coinage. The feature — silent ringer, vibrate-only, no notification sounds — was standardized on PHS handsets around 1996 specifically so commuters could comply with the new train etiquette. Every Japanese phone sold since has shipped with a manner-mode toggle, usually a dedicated physical button or a long-press shortcut. The English-speaking world calls it "silent mode" and treats it as optional. In Japan it is the default state of a phone in public.

Then there are the **priority-seat zones**. Marked in a different upholstery color near each door, these seats are reserved for elderly, pregnant, disabled, and injured passengers — and around them, signs instruct everyone to **switch phones off entirely**, not merely to manner mode. The rule is widely ignored by locals checking text messages, but the principle is intact: there exists a zone in which your phone should not even passively exist.

## What the surveys actually show

JR East runs an annual **manner ranking** (マナーアンケート), polling tens of thousands of riders on the behaviors that annoy them most. The top complaints are remarkably stable year over year:

1. Loud conversation between passengers
2. Noise leaking from headphones (*oto-more*, "sound leak")
3. Sitting position — bags on seats, sprawled legs
4. Eating and drinking
5. Drunken behavior
6. Phone calls and ringtones

Phone calls used to rank first. They dropped because compliance got near-total — there is almost nothing left to complain about. The new top entry is just other humans talking to each other, at normal volumes, like humans do.

The Cabinet Office's 2018 public opinion survey on mobile phone use produced the cleanest number on the subject: roughly **80 percent of respondents considered any voice call on a train to be rude**, regardless of length, volume, or content. Not "long calls." Not "loud calls." Any call. A two-second "I'm on the train, I'll call back" is, to four out of five Japanese commuters, a small social violation.

## How quiet, in actual numbers

Ambient noise studies of urban rail systems put a moving Tokyo metro car at roughly 60–65 decibels — most of it mechanical, from wheels and HVAC. A New York City subway car typically measures 80–90 dB and frequently exceeds 100 dB on older lines, much of that human and announcement noise layered on top. The London Underground and the Paris Métro sit in between, around 75–85 dB, with significantly more passenger conversation.

The mechanical noise on Tokyo trains is comparable. The human noise is roughly an order of magnitude lower.

This quiet is what makes **inemuri** possible — the Japanese practice of dozing off in public, fully dressed, on a moving train, and reliably waking up at your station. It is not a national talent for sleep; it is a national achievement in not waking each other up. A New Yorker cannot inemuri on the 4 train because the 4 train will not allow it. A Tokyoite can, because the car has been collectively engineered, by everyone in it, to be a place where sleep is the reasonable response to a long commute.

## The exceptions, and the shaming

The silence has predictable cracks. Friday night last trains carry drunk salarymen who have transitioned, around their fourth highball, into a temporary exemption from meiwaku — loud, sentimental, occasionally singing. Locals tolerate it with the same weary patience reserved for weather. School-age children in uniform, in groups, on the way home, get a similar pass; the volume is forgiven because they are not yet fully socialized adults.

Everyone else gets policed by phone camera. Twitter (now X) hosts a steady stream of **manaa ihan** ("manner violation") posts — photos and short videos of passengers eating ramen, taking video calls on speaker, sprawling across three seats, or letting children stand on the upholstery. Faces are sometimes blurred. Often they are not. A viral manner-violation post can pull tens of thousands of quote-tweets within hours, almost all of them some variation of *hazukashii* — "embarrassing."

The shaming is the enforcement mechanism. There is no fine. There is no conductor walking the cars writing tickets. There is the knowledge that any breach might be photographed by any of the two hundred strangers in your car, and that the photograph will be seen, and that the seeing will be brutal.

## What a tourist will accidentally break

The unspoken rules a visitor will violate inside their first 48 hours, in rough order of severity:

- Taking a phone call. Even a short one. Even in English. Even saying "I can't talk, I'm on the train."
- Letting music leak from headphones. The bar is lower than you think — if the person beside you can identify the song, you are in violation.
- Eating on a local or commuter train. (Shinkansen and limited expresses are explicitly fine; commuter trains are not.)
- Loud conversation with travel companions, especially in groups of three or more.
- Rolling a suitcase down the aisle during rush hour.
- Standing in the priority-seat area while looking at your phone, when an elderly passenger is visibly standing nearby.
- Eye contact held for longer than half a second with anyone, ever.

None of these will get you arrested. All of them will get you noticed, and being noticed, in a Tokyo train car, is the punishment.

## TL;DR

- Tokyo trains move ~40 million people a day and run at roughly library-level volume.
- The baseline is **meiwaku** — a cultural rule against bothering others, taught in childhood.
- JR East has run manner posters since 1976 and "no phone call" announcements on a continuous loop ever since.
- **Manner mode** on phones was standardized on Japanese PHS handsets in 1996 specifically for train use.
- Cabinet Office 2018: ~80% of riders consider any phone call on a train rude, regardless of length.
- Exceptions: drunk Friday salarymen and schoolkids. Everyone else gets shamed on X.
- Enforcement is social, not legal, and it is brutally effective.
