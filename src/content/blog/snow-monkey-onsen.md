# The Hot Spring Where Snow Monkeys Outnumber Tourists

At **-10°C**, roughly **160 wild Japanese macaques** walk down from the cliffs above Jigokudani — "Hell's Valley," named for the steam that hisses out of the volcanic ground — and lower themselves, with visible relief, into a hot spring built for humans. They've been doing it since **1963**.

There is no glass. There is no fence. There is a low stone rim, a cloud of sulfur steam, and a monkey staring through you while a snowflake melts on its eyelid. You stand about three meters away. Nobody is in charge of this situation except the monkeys.

This is the only place on Earth where this happens. Here is how to actually go.

## 1. How They Discovered the Bath (1963)

The standard "wild animals doing something cute" story usually involves a researcher slowly habituating a population over decades. The Jigokudani story is funnier than that.

In the early 1960s, a nearby ryokan called **Korakukan** had an outdoor onsen for human guests. The Shiga Kogen forests above had been logged, food was getting scarce, and a population of macaques started raiding the inn's apple orchards. One day in **1963**, a juvenile female macaque watched humans soaking, climbed in, and apparently decided this was the best thing that had ever happened to a monkey.

The rest of the troop copied her. Within a year, the ryokan had a monkey hygiene problem. The compromise — built in **1964** — was a **dedicated monkey-only onsen** about 200 meters away, fed by the same hot spring system. Park rangers feed the troop barley and apples to keep them from wandering back into town.

So: the monkeys did not evolve a love of bathing. **They learned it from tourists.** This is, in a very Japanese way, the entire story of Japan in miniature — a foreign idea, adopted, refined, and then somehow more polished than the original.

## 2. The 30-Minute Hike That Costs Nothing But Kills Your Shoes

The park is not drive-up accessible. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something.

The route:

1. **Nagano Station** → local train or **Nagaden bus** (~45 min) to **Yudanaka Station**.
2. **Yudanaka Station** → local bus to **Kanbayashi Onsen** stop (~15 min, ¥310).
3. **Kanbayashi trailhead** → **1.6 km uphill snow trail** through cedar forest to the park entrance (~30–40 min walking).
4. **Park entrance** → another 5-minute walk to the actual onsen pool. Entry is **¥800**.

The trail is not technical. It is, in winter, **packed snow over ice over more ice**, with a steady incline the entire way. Regular sneakers will be soaked and useless within ten minutes. Hiking boots are ideal; **rubber-soled waterproof boots with traction** are the minimum. The park rents crampon-style **ice cleats** at the trailhead for ¥300 and you should absolutely pay the ¥300.

Bring a small backpack — **no food allowed** in the park (the monkeys will mug you, and yes, this happens regularly). Water is fine. Cameras are fine. Drones are banned.

## 3. When to Actually Go

The monkeys are technically there year-round, but **you came for the snow shot**, so let's be honest about that.

- **December – March**: snow season. The iconic photo only exists in these months.
- **Mid-January to mid-February**: the **peak window**. Deepest snow, steam at maximum drama, snowflakes on monkey eyelashes.
- **April – November**: monkeys still visit the pool, but it looks like… a monkey in a puddle. Skip unless you're already in Nagano.

**Avoid weekends.** Saturday between 11am and 2pm in February is a wall of tripods three deep. The park opens at **9:00am in winter** (8:30am in summer). **Arrive at opening.** You'll get a clean hour before the tour buses from Nagano roll in around 10:30.

One thing nobody tells you: the monkeys are *wild*, and on warm winter days they sometimes don't bother bathing at all. There's no guarantee. Check the **park's live webcam** the morning of your visit — if the pool is empty at 9am, it'll probably be empty all day.

## 4. Where to Stay

Three real options, ranked by how committed you are to the bit.

### Korakukan Ryokan — the in-park stay (~¥18,000/night)

The **same ryokan from the 1963 origin story** is still operating, and it's the only accommodation inside the park boundary. You walk out the door at 7am and you are alone with the monkeys for two hours before the park officially opens. The rooms are simple tatami. Dinner is a proper kaiseki. The outdoor onsen is human-only now, but monkeys do occasionally wander past while you're soaking.

It **books out 6 months in advance** for January and February. There is no walking this in. Reserve in July for a January stay.

### Shibu Onsen — the better-value play (~¥15,000–25,000/night)

A 1,300-year-old onsen town **30 minutes by bus** from the trailhead. Nine public bathhouses, wooden ryokan, cobblestone alleys, the works. The famous one is **Kanaguya**, a nine-story wooden inn from 1758 that allegedly inspired *Spirited Away*'s bathhouse. Stay here, eat dinner here, taxi to the trail in the morning.

### Day trip from Nagano Station (¥0 extra)

If you're hotel-based in Nagano city, the trip is doable in a day. Budget **8 hours door-to-door** and don't try to combine it with anything else.

[**Find Nagano hotels on Booking.com**](https://www.booking.com/searchresults.html?ss=Nagano+Japan) — Shibu Onsen and Nagano city inventory both live here.

## 5. The Nagano vs. Tokyo Question

Most travelers ask the same thing: *Can I do this as a day trip from Tokyo?*

Technically: **yes**.

- **Tokyo (Tokyo Station)** → Nagano via **Hokuriku Shinkansen**: **~1h 20min**, ¥8,340 each way.
- **Nagano** → Snow Monkey Park: **~1 hour** by express bus or train + bus combo.
- **Total**: ~2.5 hours each way, ~5 hours round-trip in transit.

You leave Tokyo at 6:30am, you're at the park by 10am, you leave at 1pm, you're back at your Tokyo hotel by 5pm. It works. People do it.

**Don't do it.** You will spend more on the round-trip shinkansen (~¥16,700) than on a night at Korakukan. You will skip the only **two hours of empty park access** that exist. And you will skip the kaiseki dinner, the snow-lantern walk through Shibu Onsen, and the entire reason Nagano exists as a destination.

The ryokan stay is the recommendation. Always.

## 6. Photography: Rules and Reality

The shots you've seen on Instagram require, at minimum, a **70-200mm telephoto** or longer. The pool is small, the monkeys are intimate, and you cannot get closer than about 3 meters without a ranger asking you to back up.

- **No flash.** Ever. It startles the troop and the rangers will eject you.
- **No touching.** The park rule is technically **50cm** in practice, but treat it as **a no-touch rule, full stop** — these are wild adult primates and a stressed macaque bite means a rabies workup.
- **No selfies with monkeys.** They will steal your phone. This is not a joke; the park has its own posted signage warning against it.
- **Tripods**: tolerated in winter when the deck isn't crowded, banned-by-glare from other visitors on busy days. Bring a monopod instead.
- For tight close-ups of faces and steam droplets, a **private photography tour** with early access gets you better angles than the general crowd. Worth it once.

[**Amazon: photography telephoto lens recommendations**](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=telephoto+lens+wildlife&tag=weirdjapan20-20) — a 70-300mm in the $500–800 range is the sweet spot for this and 90% of other wildlife travel.

## TL;DR

> Wild snow monkeys learned to bathe in a hot spring in 1963 by copying tourists at a ryokan, and Japan responded by building them their own onsen. Go in January or February, stay at Korakukan if you can book it 6 months out or Shibu Onsen if you can't, hike in at 9am sharp, and bring a telephoto. Skip the Tokyo day-trip. The two hours of empty park before the buses arrive are the entire point.
