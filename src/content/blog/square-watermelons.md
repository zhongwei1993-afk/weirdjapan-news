# Japan Grows Square Watermelons. They Cost ¥10,000 Each.

Somewhere in Zentsuji, a small city on the island of Shikoku, a farmer is rotating a watermelon by a quarter turn inside a clear acrylic box. He does this every two days for about a month. The watermelon, having no choice in the matter, eventually gives up trying to be a sphere and becomes a cube. It is then picked early, polished, wrapped in a ribbon, flown to a department-store basement in Tokyo, and sold for the price of a flight to Seoul. Nobody eats it. That is not what it is for.

This is **shikaku-suika** (四角スイカ), the square watermelon. It is one of the purest expressions of the Japanese luxury-fruit economy — a system in which produce is judged less on how it tastes and more on how perfectly it has been disciplined into a shape humans approve of.

## A Graphic Designer Invents a Vegetable

The square watermelon was not the product of a research institute or an agricultural ministry. It was invented in 1978 by **Tomoyuki Ono**, a graphic designer living in Zentsuji, Kagawa Prefecture. Ono was not trying to revolutionize agriculture. She was trying to solve a domestic problem: round watermelons rolled around in the refrigerator, took up too much space, and were a nuisance to slice evenly. So she built a box.

The first molds were rough wooden frames. Today they are precision-cut acrylic cubes, manufactured to a specific tolerance, slipped over the fruit when it reaches roughly the size of a grapefruit. The watermelon then spends the rest of its short, structured life pressed against six flat walls, photosynthesizing into a corner.

The design rationale was practical, and Ono said so plainly at the time:

- Square watermelons **stack** in a fridge instead of rolling
- Square watermelons are **easier to slice** on a cutting board
- Square watermelons **display** more cleanly in a shop window

What she did not predict was that the third point would devour the first two entirely. The fruit became famous as an object, not a food. By the late 1980s, no one was actually putting them in their fridges. They were sitting on plinths in department stores.

## How Zentsuji Cornered the Market

Today, almost every square watermelon in Japan comes from a small group of growers in Zentsuji, organized through the **JA Kagawa** agricultural cooperative. Total annual production is around **400 fruit** — not 400,000, not 4,000, just 400. The supply is artisanally tiny.

The growing process is unforgiving. It requires:

- A specific Zentsuji microclimate — warm, dry, with reliable mid-summer sun
- A narrow harvest window in **mid-July**, lasting roughly three weeks
- **Manual rotation** of each fruit inside its mold, repeated throughout the growing cycle
- Pristine acrylic boxes that must not crack under pressure as the fruit pushes outward
- Early picking — before the sugar has had time to develop properly

That last point is the open secret of the whole industry. To get the cube shape clean and the rind a marketable green, the watermelon has to be harvested while still essentially unripe. The result is a fruit that looks magnificent and tastes like a wet sponge. The Zentsuji growers don't pretend otherwise. The boxes ship with a small printed note advising buyers that the fruit is for **decoration, not consumption** — a kind of agricultural product disclaimer that exists almost nowhere else on Earth.

Retail prices in 2026 run from about **¥15,000 to ¥30,000** per cube, depending on size, symmetry, and which department store you buy it from. Imperfect specimens — a slight bulge, an off-center stem — are quietly diverted to gift-fruit dealers at a steep discount. Perfect ones go straight to Isetan.

## The Wider Universe of Sculpted Fruit

Once you accept that a watermelon can be a cube, the rest of the catalogue starts to make sense. Japanese novelty-shape growers also produce:

- **Heart-shaped watermelons**, grown in clamshell molds, retailing around **¥20,000**
- **Pyramid watermelons**, with a flat triangular base for display
- **Buddha-face pumpkins**, grown in Saga Prefecture using molds shaped like a meditating face, marketed for autumn altar displays
- **Square Yubari melons**, occasionally trialled but never commercially stable

The pumpkin-Buddhas are the strangest item in the lineup, partly because the mold gives them an expression somewhere between serenity and mild dyspepsia. They show up in temple gift shops around Obon and are usually bought by people who do not entirely know what to do with them once they get home.

## The Export Trade: Cube Fruit as Soft Power

Square watermelons travel. A meaningful share of annual production is exported — not to neighboring markets where consumers might want to actually eat fruit, but to luxury buyers in **Russia, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE**, where the cube functions as a conversation piece on an oligarch's countertop. A 2019 auction in Russia reportedly cleared a single specimen for around **$850**, a markup so silly it briefly made international news and then was promptly forgotten.

In the Gulf, the fruit is sometimes presented as a corporate gift, sealed inside a wooden display box with handles. It is the agricultural equivalent of a Patek Philippe — something whose function has long since been replaced by its meaning.

## Densuke, Yubari, and the Two Kinds of Luxury Fruit

To understand why this market exists at all, you have to understand that Japan has two distinct luxury-fruit categories, and they almost never overlap.

**Taste-luxury** fruit is bred for flavor that genuinely exceeds the normal range. The **Densuke watermelon**, grown only on Hokkaido, is the canonical example — jet-black rind, high sugar, crisp texture. A single Densuke set the all-time record at auction in 2008 at **¥820,000**. The annual **Yubari melon** auction in Sapporo regularly produces pairs that clear **¥3,000,000** for the ceremonial first lot of the season. **Miyazaki mangoes** sell at retail for **¥10,000** apiece in good years. People do, in fact, eat these.

**Form-luxury** fruit is bred for appearance. The square watermelon is the canonical example. Nobody pretends they are eating it. The two markets coexist peacefully because they are answering different questions: Densuke answers *what is the best watermelon possible?* The square watermelon answers *what is the most photogenic watermelon possible?* Both are valid in Japan. Only one is valid almost anywhere else.

## Why Gift Fruit Drives the Entire Economy

The whole novelty-fruit industry exists because of two seasonal Japanese gift cycles. **Ochugen** in July and **oseibo** in December are the formal mid-year and end-of-year gift-giving periods, during which white-collar Japan sends thank-you presents to bosses, clients, in-laws, and anyone whose continued goodwill matters professionally. The accepted currency for these gifts is, increasingly, premium fruit.

A wrapped Yubari melon at ochugen says *I value this relationship and I have ¥15,000 to prove it.* A square watermelon at oseibo says *I value this relationship and I would also like you to remember the gift after you have eaten it,* which you will, because you cannot eat it. The square watermelon's inedibility is, in this context, a feature. It outlasts the season.

## How a Foreigner Can Actually See One

Square watermelons rarely sit out in regular supermarkets. To find one in person, head to a **depachika** (food hall) in a flagship department store during the July harvest window:

- **Isetan Shinjuku** — the basement gift-fruit counter regularly displays one or two cubes during the season
- **Daimaru Tokyo** — adjacent to Tokyo Station, easy to reach with luggage if you have just arrived
- **Sembikiya Nihonbashi** — the original luxury-fruit specialist, founded in 1834, where the cube is treated with appropriate gravity

Bring your phone. Do not touch. Staff are polite about photography but firm about fingers on the rind. If you actually intend to buy, expect the transaction to be conducted with the formality of buying a watch — a folded brochure, a printed certificate, and a paulownia-wood box on request.

## The Criticism: Agricultural Performance Art

Not everyone in Japan is charmed by this. Critics — including some agricultural economists at Kagawa University — have called the square watermelon **agricultural performance art rather than food**, arguing that the labor and microclimate it consumes could be better spent on Densuke-grade taste cultivars. There is a fair case to be made. The square watermelon trains farmers to optimize for the wrong variable, normalizes the idea that food is primarily a display object, and quietly contributes to the ongoing weirdness of the Japanese gift economy, where a cube of unripe watermelon and a ¥30,000 Yubari pair can sit on the same shelf with equal dignity.

But it also keeps an aging cooperative in Zentsuji solvent, exports a small piece of Japan to people who would otherwise never have heard of Kagawa Prefecture, and gives every tourist who sees one in an Isetan basement a story they will tell for the rest of their life. Whether that is worth 400 unripe watermelons a year is, ultimately, a matter of taste.

## TL;DR

Square watermelons (shikaku-suika) were invented in 1978 by a Zentsuji graphic designer who wanted fruit that fit in a fridge. Today, JA Kagawa grows about 400 a year, sells them for ¥15,000–¥30,000 to depachika gift counters, and ships them to Russia and the Gulf as ultra-luxury display objects. Nobody eats them — they are picked early and taste terrible. The square watermelon is the form-luxury counterpart to taste-luxury fruit like the ¥820,000 Densuke. It is, depending on whom you ask, either a charming piece of Japanese over-engineering or agricultural performance art with a ribbon on top.
