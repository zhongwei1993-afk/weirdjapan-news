# Japan's Starbucks Sells Sakura Lattes the Rest of the World Can't Buy

Every February, a chain coffee company headquartered in Seattle releases a pink drink in Tokyo that turns adults into queue-standing teenagers. The cups are designed by regional Japanese illustrators. The tumblers resell on Mercari for triple their retail price within forty-eight hours. The drink is gone by mid-March. Then everyone pretends, until next February, that none of this happened.

Starbucks Japan is the same company as Starbucks everywhere else. It is also, functionally, a different company. And the SAKURA series — the annual spring menu it has been running since 2007 — is the single clearest example of how far a global brand will bend when it lands somewhere that takes seasonal beverages as seriously as Japan does.

## A Joint Venture That Quietly Became the Second-Largest Starbucks Market

Starbucks opened its first Japanese store in Ginza in 1996, on the corner of Matsuya-dori. It was the company's first location outside North America. The arrangement was a 50-50 joint venture with Sazaby League, the Japanese lifestyle group behind Afternoon Tea Living and a long list of import retail brands that specialize in making foreign concepts feel domesticated.

That partnership lasted nearly two decades. Sazaby ran the local operation with a degree of autonomy unusual for a global franchisee — choosing the architecture, hiring the baristas, deciding which seasonal flavors to test. Starbucks Corporation eventually bought out Sazaby's share in 2014, but the operational DNA stuck. The Japan team kept its own menu development, its own store design studio, and its own marketing calendar.

By 2026, Starbucks Japan operates roughly 1,900 stores. That makes it the second-largest Starbucks market on earth, behind only the United States and ahead of China — which had briefly overtaken it before the chain's slowdown in the mainland. Japan never had a slowdown. It had, instead, a queue.

## The SAKURA Series: A 19-Year-Old Marketing Machine

The first SAKURA drink launched in February 2007. It was a sakura-flavored creme frappuccino, pale pink, sold in cups printed with cherry blossom branches. It sold out. So the next year there were two drinks. Then three. By 2012 the SAKURA series was the most important commercial event on Starbucks Japan's annual calendar — bigger than the Christmas red cup, bigger than the autumn Pumpkin Spice rollout that the rest of the world treats as the brand's defining moment.

The typical lineup, in any given year, runs something like this:

- **Sakura Strawberry Latte** — pink, milky, with sakura-leaf flavoring and a strawberry sauce ribbon. The reliable bestseller.
- **Sakura Chiffon Cake Frappuccino** — chunks of sakura-flavored sponge cake blended into a base of white chocolate and milk. Aggressively sweet.
- **Hojicha Sakura Tea Latte** — the grown-up option, pairing roasted green tea with sakura syrup. Marketed at people who claim to find the Strawberry Latte too sweet but secretly buy one anyway.
- **Sakura Mochi Frappuccino** — appears in some years, featuring small chewy mochi pieces in a sakura-pink base. When it returns, it sells out fastest.

The cups change too. Starbucks Japan commissions regional Japanese designers and illustrators each year to produce the SAKURA cup art, the tumblers, and the merchandise line. The 2024 series used illustrator Aimi Kawai. The 2025 collection brought in three different artists for three drop dates. The cups themselves become collectible objects.

## Sold Out in Days, Lines Outside Ginza

In Tokyo, the SAKURA launch behaves less like a beverage release and more like a sneaker drop. The flagship Ginza store and the Marronnier Gate location regularly have lines forming before opening on launch day. By the end of the first week, certain drinks are unavailable in central Tokyo, with baristas posting handwritten apology signs at the register: *honjitsu wa urikire desu* — sold out today.

The tumblers are worse. Starbucks Japan releases a limited-edition SAKURA tumbler line every year, typically priced between ¥2,200 and ¥3,500. They are gone within hours of going on sale. Within twenty-four hours, the same tumblers appear on Mercari and Yahoo Auctions at ¥8,000 to ¥15,000. The most coveted designs — usually the ones featuring traditional Japanese motifs or specific regional artists — have cleared ¥20,000 in resale.

There is a small but persistent secondary economy of people who line up at multiple stores on release morning, buy the per-customer maximum at each, and flip the inventory by lunchtime. Starbucks Japan knows this happens. The per-customer purchase limits are an attempt to slow it. It does not work.

## The Concept Stores Most Tourists Miss

Two locations exist that have nothing to do with the SAKURA series but explain why Japan accepted Starbucks so completely in the first place.

The Kyoto Ninenzaka store, opened in 2017, is built inside a traditional 100-year-old machiya townhouse on the cobblestone approach to Kiyomizu-dera. It is the only Starbucks in the world with tatami seating. Customers remove their shoes. The exterior is wood, plaster, and tile, with a discreet siren logo on a hanging cloth banner. It is the most-photographed Starbucks on the planet, and arguably the least Starbucks-looking.

The Reserve Roastery in Nakameguro, opened in 2019, is the other direction entirely. It is a four-story building designed by Kengo Kuma, with a copper cask installation that runs the full height of the central atrium and a cherry-tree-lined canal view that becomes one of Tokyo's premier hanami spots in April. There are exactly five other Roasteries in the world — Seattle, New York, Milan, Chicago, Shanghai. Nakameguro is widely considered the best of them.

## Hojicha, Star Rally, and the Quiet Competitors

Starbucks Japan's R&D operation is also the channel through which Japan-only menu items occasionally leak into the global system. The Hojicha Tea Latte, developed in Japan and tested for years on the domestic menu, was rolled out to international Starbucks markets in 2023. Several SAKURA-adjacent items — yuzu citrus drinks, certain matcha frappuccino formulations, the wagashi-shaped cake pops — have followed similar paths.

The loyalty program runs on the same logic. The Starbucks Reward Star Rally, the Japanese version of the global Stars system, layers on seasonal bonus campaigns timed to product drops. Earn a star with a SAKURA drink and you unlock a digital stamp. Collect five and you get a free drink ticket. It is, in form, identical to any global Starbucks app. In behavior, it taps a Japanese consumer instinct for completionism that the global team did not invent and could not have invented.

The domestic competition keeps the pressure on. Doutor and Tully's both pre-date Starbucks in Japan — Doutor since 1980, Tully's since 1997 — and Komeda Coffee operates the morning-set business model that Starbucks deliberately does not compete with. The cumulative effect is that Starbucks Japan cannot coast. The SAKURA series is, in part, a defensive moat against rivals who would happily eat its breakfast.

## Why "Third Place" Worked Here

Howard Schultz's original Starbucks pitch was the *third place* — somewhere between home and work where people could sit. The concept was borrowed from American sociologist Ray Oldenburg. It was, in the United States, a useful marketing frame but somewhat aspirational; most American Starbucks customers grabbed a cup and left.

In Japan, the third-place idea landed on prepared soil. Japanese cities were already organized around dense networks of *kissaten* — small independent coffee shops where customers sat for hours over a single drink, often alone, often reading. Starbucks slotted into a slot that already existed in the urban grammar. It just happened to be larger, cleaner, English-friendly, and equipped with wireless charging.

The SAKURA series is what happens when that infrastructure meets a domestic consumer culture organized around limited-edition seasonal goods. The same instinct that drives the 400-flavor Kit Kat economy and the depachika basement food halls drives the annual sakura tumbler stampede. Starbucks Japan did not engineer this. It was already there. Starbucks Japan only had to show up in February with pink drinks and good cup art.

In 2026, the SAKURA series is in its nineteenth year. Sales are reportedly the strongest they have ever been. The tumbler resale market is, if anything, more aggressive. The drinks are still pink. The lines are still out the door at Ginza Matsuya-dori, the same corner where the first Japanese Starbucks opened thirty years ago. Nobody finds this strange. That is the strange part.

## TL;DR

- Starbucks Japan opened in Ginza in 1996 via a joint venture with Sazaby League and now runs ~1,900 stores, the second-largest Starbucks market in the world.
- The annual SAKURA series, running every February-March since 2007, includes Sakura Strawberry Latte, Sakura Chiffon Cake Frappuccino, Hojicha Sakura, and occasional mochi versions, with cup art commissioned from regional Japanese designers.
- Drinks sell out within days in central Tokyo; ¥3,000 limited tumblers resell on Mercari for ¥10,000-plus within hours of release.
- Concept stores include the Kyoto Ninenzaka machiya (2017) and the Kengo Kuma-designed Reserve Roastery in Nakameguro (2019).
- Japan-developed menu items occasionally export globally — the Hojicha Latte went worldwide in 2023.
