# Japanese Taxi Doors Open Automatically. Touch Them and You're Fired.

You stand on the curb in Shinjuku. A black Toyota glides up. The rear-left door swings open on its own, about fifty centimeters, hesitates, waits for you. You haven't touched anything. The driver hasn't moved his upper body. White gloves rest at ten and two on the wheel.

If you reach out and pull the handle yourself, you have just done something mildly humiliating. The driver will say nothing. He will not need to.

This is the *jido doa* — the automatic door — and it has been quietly normal in Japan since 1948.

## The 1948 Origin Story Nobody Tells

The first automatic taxi door in Japan was bolted on by a small Nagoya-area coachbuilder called **Mitsuwa Motor Body**, which had spent the war years welding truck cabs and was looking for postwar work. In 1948 they fitted a rear door to a Toyopet sedan with a hand-pumped vacuum cylinder. The driver yanked a lever near his seat, atmospheric pressure did the rest, and a Japanese cultural fixture was born almost by accident.

The pitch was practical, not poetic. Postwar Tokyo streets were narrow, drivers were trying to look professional, and women in kimono could not easily reach a sedan's outside handle while holding parcels. A door that opened itself solved three problems at once: dignity for the passenger, theater for the driver, and a clean differentiator from the unlicensed *shiro taku* (white-plate gypsy cabs) the new regulators were trying to wipe out.

By the early 1960s, every legitimate taxi in Tokyo had one. By 1964, in time for the first Olympics, it was a national standard nobody had bothered to legislate. It just *was*.

## How the Mechanism Actually Works

There is no sensor. There is no app. There is a lever, or on newer cars a small button, mounted to the right of the driver's steering column.

Older vehicles — Crown Comforts, Cedric sedans, the diesel workhorses that defined the trade for decades — used a vacuum piston tapped off the engine's intake manifold. Pull the lever, a valve opens, manifold vacuum sucks a rod that swings the rear-left door outward roughly fifty centimeters. The passenger boards. The driver tugs the lever the other way and the door closes itself with a soft thunk. No power-window whine, no servo hum, just the breath of the engine doing one extra small job.

Modern hybrids, especially the JPN Taxi, use a 12-volt electric actuator instead. Same lever, same fifty centimeters, slightly more polite closing speed. The dominant supplier of the mechanism — both the vacuum and the electric versions — is **Tomei Auto Service**, an Aichi-based specialist that has been refining the linkage for more than half a century and effectively owns the category.

This is why touching the door is a problem. The mechanism is calibrated for a specific arc, a specific resistance, a specific closing force. A passenger yanking it open mid-cycle, or slamming it shut tourist-style, can shear the linkage, bend the rod, or knock the actuator out of alignment. **The repair runs ¥30,000 to ¥50,000**, the cab is off the road for a day, and the driver — who often leases his vehicle from the company by the shift — eats some portion of the bill.

He is not going to shout. He is going to seethe. Quietly. In white gloves.

## The Fleet: Crown Comfort, JPN Taxi, Sienta

Japan has roughly **265,000 registered taxi vehicles** as of the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism's 2024 figures, and almost all of them are one of three cars.

The **Toyota Crown Comfort** was the archetype: a boxy, upright sedan styled in 1995 and produced essentially unchanged for twenty-two years until the last one rolled off the line in 2017. LPG-fueled, rear-wheel drive, with seats covered in lace doilies and a back bench tall enough to keep a salaryman's hairstyle intact. For two generations of Tokyoites, "taxi" *meant* a Crown Comfort.

Its replacement, the **Toyota JPN Taxi**, launched in late 2017 with the 2020 Olympics in mind. It is an LPG-electric hybrid, painted a deep indigo Toyota markets as "Koiai," and built on accessible-design principles: a sliding rear door (still automatic, still driver-operated), a low flat floor, and a fold-out wheelchair ramp stowed under the rear seat. You will see thousands of them in Tokyo now. Drivers are split — some love the efficiency, some miss the Crown's turning radius.

A third tier of operators, particularly in the regions, use **Toyota Sienta MPV variants** retrofitted with the auto-door kit. Same mechanism, smaller footprint, popular with the school-run and grandma-to-clinic rural trade.

## Fares, Apps, and the Cashless Creep

Tokyo's regulated meter starts at **¥500 for the first kilometer**, then ticks **¥100 every 233 meters**. Between 22:00 and 05:00 a **20 percent night surcharge** applies. A typical Tokyo-to-Haneda run lands at ¥7,000-¥9,000; Tokyo-to-Narita, the journey tourists most often regret, is **¥20,000-¥25,000** before tolls. (The Narita Express train is ¥3,070. Take the train.)

For years Japanese taxis were a cash-only fortress. That has finally cracked. Industry surveys now put **credit-card acceptance at roughly 95 percent in urban fleets**, with Suica, PASMO, PayPay and the major QR wallets close behind. Carry cash anyway. The reader will fail at least once on a long trip.

The app layer has consolidated. **Go** — formed in 2020 when JapanTaxi and MOV merged — is the dominant domestic hailer, covers most of the country, and accepts foreign cards. **DiDi** runs a serviceable second, especially in Osaka. **Uber operates in Japan but only as Uber Taxi**: it dispatches existing licensed cabs at the regulated meter rate. Genuine peer-to-peer ride-share remains illegal as of 2026, with limited "Japan-style ride-share" pilots in a handful of rural municipalities and no national rollout anticipated this year.

## Etiquette, Shortages, and What to Actually Say

The unwritten rules, in order of how badly tourists violate them:

- **Do not touch the rear-left door.** Stand back. Let it open. Let it close.
- **Sit on the left.** Enter on the left, exit on the left. The right side opens onto oncoming traffic and the driver will physically lock it.
- **Do not tip.** It confuses the driver and, in some companies, requires him to file a small-cash-receipt form. See [tipping-insulting](/blog/tipping-insulting) for the longer version of why.
- **Do not slam.** The driver closes the door. Always.
- **Address your destination, then add *onegai shimasu*.** "Tokyo Eki made, onegai shimasu." (To Tokyo Station, please.) A printed address or a dropped Google Maps pin works fine if your pronunciation does not.

Drivers — almost uniformly male, almost uniformly over fifty, almost uniformly in a suit jacket and white cotton gloves — will help with your luggage without being asked. The premium operators take this further. **MK Taxi**, originally a Kyoto firm and now in Tokyo, trains drivers to step out, open the door from outside, and bow. **Sakura Kotsu** runs a similar high-touch service. Both cost about 20 percent more than a standard cab and are worth it for a first-time visitor moving with bags.

The industry's quiet crisis is rural. Drivers are retiring faster than they can be replaced — the trade press estimates the countryside has been losing roughly **5,000 taxi vehicles per year** since the late 2010s — and entire prefectures now have towns where the last cab knocks off at 8 p.m. The auto-door, the white gloves, the lace doilies survive in Tokyo. In Akita they are starting to feel like a museum.

## TL;DR

- Rear-left taxi door is driver-operated by lever or button; vacuum piston on older cars, electric actuator on new ones.
- Invented 1948 by Mitsuwa in Nagoya; mechanism now supplied by Tomei Auto Service in Aichi.
- Japan has ~265,000 cabs; the fleet is Toyota Crown Comfort (1995-2017), JPN Taxi (2017-), and Sienta MPVs.
- Touching the door can break it. Repair: ¥30,000-¥50,000.
- Tokyo meter: ¥500 / first km, ¥100 / 233m, +20% at night.
- Apps: Go, DiDi, Uber Taxi. No legal ride-share in 2026.
- Cards work in ~95% of urban cabs; carry cash anyway.
- Don't tip, don't slam, don't sit on the right.
