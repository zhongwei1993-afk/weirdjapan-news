# Kyoto's Smallest Temple Is the Size of a Telephone Booth

Mihoshi Temple in Kyoto fits one priest and three offerings. It's still active. The donation box accepts ¥10 coins.

The priest visits on Wednesdays. The offerings are rice, a small cup of sake, and whatever fruit happens to be in season — usually a single mikan in winter and a half-apricot in early summer. The structure itself is roughly the footprint of an old NTT phone booth, painted dark cedar, with a tile roof the size of a serving tray. Tourists walk past it on the way to somewhere larger. Most never notice. The few who do tend to assume it's a shed.

It is not a shed. It is one of approximately 2,000 active places of worship in Kyoto, and one of perhaps 300 that exist at a scale most foreigners do not have a category for.

## A City Built on Roughly 2,000 Sacred Footprints

Kyoto contains around 1,600 Buddhist temples and roughly 400 Shinto shrines, depending on which prefectural register you trust and how you define "active." Pressed into a basin of about 827 square kilometers, with most of the historical core fitting inside a rectangle you can walk across in two hours, the density becomes absurd. There are stretches of Nishijin where you pass a torii gate every fifty meters and stop noticing.

The tourist itinerary distorts this. Kinkaku-ji draws somewhere north of 5 million visitors a year. Kiyomizu-dera clears 10 million in a good year, with a peak of roughly 60,000 per day during November foliage season. Fushimi Inari, with its corridor of vermillion gates, hovers around the same numbers. Together, perhaps a dozen mega-sites absorb 95% of all temple traffic in the city.

That leaves around 1,980 other places of worship operating quietly in the margins. A meaningful percentage of them are smaller than the average Tokyo apartment.

## The Mikuriya, the Tobora, and the Shrines You Can Step Over

The technical vocabulary is slippery. Mikuriya (御厨) originally referred to small sacred kitchens or food-offering structures attached to larger shrines. Tobora (戸祠) is a regional Kyoto term for "door shrine" — literally a shrine that fits behind, or inside, a doorway. In practice, locals just call them chiisana otera or chiisana jinja — "the small temple," "the small shrine" — and let context handle the rest.

What they share is scale. Most occupy less than 10 square meters of land. Many occupy less than 4. Several occupy a sliver of someone's front wall, with a recessed niche, a wooden gate the size of a cereal box, and a stone basin no larger than a coffee cup.

A few standouts:

- **Misonohata Jinja**, tucked into a residential lane two minutes northeast of Gion's main intersection, measures about 3 meters wide and maybe 4 meters deep. Two stone lanterns, a torii you could reach the top of without standing on tiptoe, and a hand-lettered sign requesting that visitors not park bicycles against the offering box.
- **Mibu-zuka**, near Mibu-dera in the southwest of the old city, is essentially a stone mound with a roof. It commemorates a forgotten member of the Shinsengumi and is maintained by neighbors who rotate the flower arrangement on the first of each month.
- **Mukoi-Jizo**, the wayside shrine partway up Ninen-zaka, is a single carved Jizo statue under a wooden hood not much larger than a mailbox. He is dressed in a knitted red bib that someone replaces every spring. A laminated card explains, in three languages, that he watches over children and travelers, and asks visitors not to touch his face.

You can walk past all three in a single morning and never realize they are, technically, religious institutions with priests on file at the prefectural office.

## Jizo, the Patron Saint of the Sidewalk

To understand the micro-shrine phenomenon, it helps to understand Jizo (地蔵). He is a bodhisattva — in the Buddhist taxonomy, a being who has earned enlightenment but stayed behind to help others — and in Japan, he has been quietly reassigned as the protector of children, travelers, and the souls of the unborn. He stands on street corners. He stands at the edges of rice paddies. He stands in cemeteries and at bus stops and outside konbini.

Kyoto alone is estimated to host over 5,000 roadside Jizo statues, the majority maintained not by temples but by neighborhood associations. The bibs and knitted hats they wear are offerings from local mothers and grandmothers. Every August, the Jizo-bon festival quietly rotates through residential streets — children gather at the local statue, sweets are distributed, an elderly neighbor explains what the small stone figure is for, and then everyone goes home.

This is the substrate beneath the micro-shrine economy. The infrastructure is already there. The reverence is already there. Building a 3-square-meter shrine to house a Jizo is, in the Kyoto context, less an architectural decision than a gardening one.

## Why So Many: Imperial Capital, Clan Politics, Meiji Math

The historical reason for the density is straightforward and slightly sad.

From 794 to 1868, Kyoto was the imperial capital, and during most of that span, any aristocratic clan worth its rice stipend maintained at least one private temple or shrine — for ancestral rites, for political theater, for genuine devotion, often all three. The Fujiwara had dozens. The Minamoto, the Taira, the dozens of court families whose names survive only in genealogical scrolls — each one built. By the late Edo period, the city carried a clerical population that some estimates put as high as 5% of the total.

Then came 1868. The Meiji government, in its rush to consolidate state Shinto and untangle a thousand years of fused Buddhist-Shinto practice, issued the Shinbutsu Bunri edicts. Tens of thousands of small religious institutions across Japan were ordered merged, demoted, or quietly shuttered. Kyoto, with its outsized inventory, was hit particularly hard.

Many of the larger compounds simply absorbed the smaller ones on paper. But the physical shrines often stayed put, because the families who had founded them, or the neighborhoods that had grown up around them, refused to let the structures be removed. So the city was left with hundreds of architectural orphans: shrines with no parent institution, no parishioners, and no formal budget — but with a stone foundation, a Jizo, and a sense among the neighbors that someone had to keep an eye on it.

This is why Kyoto's micro-shrines tend to feel slightly homemade. They are.

## A ¥0 Budget and a Rotating Volunteer Schedule

The modern preservation problem is exactly the one you would predict. Roughly a third of Kyoto's smallest shrines have no formal danka (parishioner family) base. Many operate on annual revenues of zero yen. The priest — if there is one — is often the head of a larger nearby temple, who adds the small shrine to a circuit and visits monthly or quarterly.

The actual maintenance is done by neighbors. Sweeping. Replacing the sakaki branches. Repainting the torii every fifteen years, usually with whatever red paint someone donates. A rotating volunteer schedule taped inside the gate, with names written in pencil and crossed off as years pass and people move or die.

Since 2008, an unexpected lifeline has been **Furusato Nozei** (ふるさと納税), the "hometown tax" system that lets Japanese taxpayers redirect a portion of their residence tax to any municipality of their choice, often in exchange for regional gifts. A handful of Kyoto wards have begun explicitly earmarking Furusato Nozei contributions for small-shrine preservation, with donors receiving a packet of incense, a hand-bound pamphlet about the shrine they helped, and occasionally a small wooden ema. The amounts are modest — a busy ward might raise ¥2-4 million annually for micro-shrine upkeep — but in a budget category that previously ran on zero, it is transformative.

## A Walking List, with Streets You Can Actually Find

For the visitor who wants to look for these places rather than past them, a partial circuit through three neighborhoods.

**Higashiyama**
- *Mukoi-Jizo* — east side of Ninen-zaka, halfway up, behind a wooden hood. Look for the red bib.
- *Yasaka Koshindo's side niche* — a 2-meter-wide annex on the south wall of the main Koshindo compound near Kiyomizu-zaka, dedicated to travel safety. Easy to miss; covered in the colorful kukurizaru cloth monkeys.
- *Misonohata Jinja* — two minutes northeast of Shijo-Hanamikoji intersection in Gion, on a residential side lane. Width of a hallway.

**Nishijin**
- *Kamoinari Jisha* — a small Inari shrine on Imadegawa-dori west of Horikawa, set into the corner of a textile merchant's wall. A single fox, a single torii.
- *Mibu-zuka* — adjacent to Mibu-dera, southwest of Shijo-Omiya. Stone mound, low roof, fresh flowers.

**Pontocho / Kiyamachi**
- *Tatsumi Daimyojin* — a small geisha-district shrine on the south end of Pontocho alley, no larger than a closet, dedicated to performing arts. Maiko leave business cards in the offering tray.

Addresses in Kyoto are functionally a series of intersections rather than street numbers, so the above are described accordingly. Maps apps will find the named ones; the unnamed ones you have to look up from.

## How to Visit Without Being the Problem

The etiquette is light but real. A few rules, observed even loosely, will read as respect.

- **No flash photography**, ever, at any altar. Daylight only.
- **No photography of the inner altar itself**, even without flash, at the smallest shrines. Exteriors are fine.
- **Leave a ¥5 coin** in the offering box if you stop. The Japanese word for ¥5, *go-en*, is a homophone for "honored connection." This is the standard offering and is more appreciated than larger amounts.
- **Two bows, two claps, one bow** at a Shinto shrine. Hands together silently at a Buddhist temple — no clapping.
- **Don't bring food or drinks past the gate.** Especially not coffee. Especially not in summer.
- **Don't touch the Jizo statues.** They are dressed by someone. That someone will notice.

The point is not to perform reverence. It is to behave in a way that, if a neighbor walks past, the shrine continues to feel like theirs.

## TL;DR

Kyoto contains around 2,000 active temples and shrines. About a dozen of them absorb nearly all foreign tourism. The rest range in size from modest neighborhood compounds down to the footprint of a phone booth. Many are post-Meiji architectural orphans kept alive by neighbors, rotating volunteers, and — increasingly — the Furusato Nozei hometown tax system. Walk slowly through Higashiyama, Nishijin, or Pontocho and you will pass at least a dozen of them. Bring ¥5 coins. Don't photograph the altars.
