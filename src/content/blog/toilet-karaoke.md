# Japanese Public Toilets Have Background Music. Now It's Pop Songs.

Walk into a women's restroom at Hankyu Umeda in Osaka on a weekday afternoon and you will hear the second movement of Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony, played at roughly the volume of a hotel lobby, on speakers tucked behind the stall partitions. Walk into the same kind of restroom at Tobu Department Store Ikebukuro and you may hear the Disney songbook on rotation, instrumental versions, slightly compressed. Walk into a restroom at Narita Terminal 2 and you will hear forest birds and a creek. None of this is a mistake. It is the third generation of a piece of acoustic infrastructure that began in 1988 as a small white box with a button, and has spent four decades quietly mutating into a national soundtrack.

## Start With the Box on the Wall

The baseline device, in roughly **80% of women's public toilets in Japan**, is TOTO's **Otohime** (音姫, "Sound Princess"): a wall-mounted speaker that plays about twenty-five seconds of recorded running water on demand, designed to mask the noise of using the toilet so users do not, as they had done for decades, flush the bowl three or four times for cover. TOTO launched it in 1988 and has shipped roughly **5 million units globally**, almost all inside Japan. LIXIL sells a near-identical competitor under the **Etiquette Tone (エチケットトーン)** brand for INAX-fitted buildings. The two products are functionally a duopoly. The engineering and the gender politics are in [the Otohime explainer](/blog/toilet-music-otohime).

What's changed in the 2020s is the content. The running-stream loop is no longer the only option. In a quietly accelerating number of commercial properties, the speaker on the stall wall has been replaced with something less euphemistic: actual music.

## The Department Stores Got There First

The first wave of upgrades came from the depachika-grade department stores. The pattern is consistent: the chain decides the standard waterfall loop sounds cheap relative to the rest of the building, and the restroom soundtrack gets handed to whoever programs the in-store music.

- **Hankyu Umeda** (Osaka) plays Beethoven's Symphony No. 6, the *Pastoral*, on a low-volume loop in its upper-floor women's restrooms — reportedly the head of facilities' personal favorite, in place since around 2019.
- **Tobu Department Store Ikebukuro** plays instrumental Disney soundtracks, refreshed seasonally. The branding tie-in is incidental; the store licensed a catalog that read as universally inoffensive.
- **Mitsukoshi Ginza** runs a rotating classical program — Debussy, Satie, the occasional Joe Hisaishi — curated by the same firm that handles its elevator music.
- **Tokyu Plaza** and **Marronnier Gate** have moved toward licensed J-pop instrumentals, mostly mid-2010s back catalog, on the theory that anything more recent risks recognition and recognition risks awkwardness.

The volume in every case sits around **50 to 60 decibels** — loud enough to mask incidental sound, quiet enough to ignore. That is the band acoustic engineers have settled on as the sweet spot for what is politely called "ambient privacy."

## Airports, Shinkansen, and the Transit Layer

The transit network has taken a different route. Airports and high-speed rail tend to default to nature soundscapes rather than music, on the theory that travelers are already overstimulated and do not need a melody to parse.

**Narita Terminal 2** restrooms cycle through a four-track loop of forest birdsong and distant rainfall — what the airport's 2023 sustainability report calls "low-arousal nature ambience." **Haneda's** newer T3 international restrooms use a similar palette. The **Shinkansen** has standardized on a continuous water-trickling soundscape across most N700S rolling stock, piped through the same in-cabin speakers that handle station announcements. The Tokaido line briefly tested seasonal soundscapes — cicadas in August, snowfall in January — and dropped it after complaints that the snowfall track sounded, to some passengers, like static.

## The Karaoke Phenomenon, Specifically

The karaoke crossover is narrower than headlines suggest, but it does exist. In a handful of entertainment-district restrooms — most reliably **Shibuya 109** and the **Tower Records Shibuya** flagship — the restroom soundtrack is wired into the building's broader music system and includes what staff will, if pressed, describe as a song-request mechanism. In practice it is a small touchscreen near the sink that lets the user pick from about thirty tracks, weighted toward mid-tempo J-pop that does not invite singing along. The track plays in the stall for four minutes. Nobody is expected to perform.

The genuine karaoke installation foreign press writes about is **Roppongi's Karaoke Restaurant Toilet Suite** — a private booth built into a converted accessible restroom on the second floor of a multi-tenant building, soundproofed to commercial-karaoke standards, with a working toilet retained in the corner as a design conceit. Bookable by the hour. It is not a public restroom that happens to do karaoke; it is a karaoke room that happens to contain a toilet. The distinction matters to the city's licensing inspectors.

## The Acoustic Theory Underneath

The reason any of this works is well established. Ambient sound in the **50 to 60 dB range** masks incidental noise through what acousticians call informational masking — the brain stops parsing the masked sound as meaningful once a louder, more recognizable signal sits on top of it. A **Cabinet Office survey in 2018** asked roughly 4,000 respondents about restroom comfort and found that the presence of ambient music or water sound was associated with a **12% reduction in self-reported "toilet aversion"** in public settings. The effect was strongest among women in their twenties and weakest among men over sixty, who, when asked, largely said they had not noticed there was music playing.

Facility managers cite the same survey when justifying the upgrade. A music license runs roughly ¥80,000 per location per year; a small drop in restroom complaints pays for it.

## Toilet Albums Are Now a Genre

The cultural overflow is the strange part. In late 2022, **Shibuya Crossing**, a small omakase restaurant in Tomigaya, released a vinyl LP titled *Music for Bathrooms* — twelve tracks of ambient composed for restroom playback, pressed in 500 copies and sold mostly to other restaurants. It sold out. A 2024 reissue followed. Tower Records now stocks a small but real subcategory of "restroom ambient" releases under the genre tag *toire myūjikku* (トイレミュージック).

This sits inside Japan's broader **noise-management mania** — the culture that produced silent train carriages, soft-close everything, and construction sites wrapped in sound-dampening cloth. The restroom is one acoustic environment among many that the country has decided to actively curate. It is not separate from the silent Yamanote line at 7 a.m. or the muted earpiece volume on the Shinkansen. It is the same project.

## The Foreigner Reaction, and the Critique

Foreign visitors notice the soundtrack mostly by accident — they reach for the Otohime button thinking it is a flush, hear the music, and have to stand still for a moment to work out what is happening. Travel writers file it under "delightful weirdness." Anyone who has lived in Japan for more than a year stops hearing it.

The critique, running in *Asahi Shimbun* and a handful of feminist outlets since the mid-2010s, is that the entire infrastructure is **patriarchal "shame masking" dressed up as accessibility** — installed almost exclusively in women's restrooms, predicated on the assumption that women's bodies require acoustic cover that men's do not. The counter-argument, from disability-access advocates, is that the same masking sound demonstrably helps people with IBS, anxiety disorders, and prostate-related conditions use public restrooms at all. Both readings are defensible. Both are usually airing simultaneously.

## Export Has Mostly Failed

TOTO has periodically tried to sell the Otohime concept abroad. The 2018 US launch targeted hotels in San Francisco and New York; the 2021 European push focused on Germany and the Netherlands. Both stalled. American guests either did not understand the button or pressed it repeatedly out of confusion. European facility managers questioned whether the underlying acoustic shame even existed in their markets. Sales outside Japan remain a rounding error.

The 2026 wrinkle is software. A small cluster of Japanese startups — most prominently **Otoneko** and **Shizukana** — have launched apps that generate personalized restroom soundtracks on the fly, using on-device AI to match the user's heart rate, time of day, and preferred genre, played through the phone's speaker in the stall. Early adoption skews to the same demographic that bought the *Music for Bathrooms* LP. Whether this becomes infrastructure or stays a curiosity is the open question.

## TL;DR

- ~80% of women's public toilets in Japan have a TOTO Otohime or LIXIL Etiquette Tone unit; ~5M units shipped globally since 1988.
- The waterfall loop is being replaced in upscale venues with actual music: Beethoven at Hankyu Umeda, Disney at Tobu Ikebukuro, classical at Mitsukoshi Ginza, J-pop at Tokyu Plaza and Marronnier Gate.
- Airports default to nature sounds (Narita T2 forest birdsong); Shinkansen runs a continuous water-trickling track.
- True karaoke-restroom crossovers are rare: Shibuya 109 and Tower Records Shibuya have song-request systems; Roppongi's Karaoke Restaurant Toilet Suite is a private booth.
- Acoustic theory is informational masking at 50–60 dB; a 2018 Cabinet Office study found a 12% drop in self-reported toilet aversion with music.
- *Toire myūjikku* releases are now a small genre; US/EU export has mostly failed; 2026 AI apps are the next experiment.
