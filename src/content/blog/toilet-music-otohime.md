# Japanese Toilets Play Music So You Don't Embarrass Yourself

Press a small plastic button on the wall of almost any women's restroom in Japan and you'll hear what sounds like a mountain stream — a steady, slightly tinny rush of water that lasts roughly twenty-five seconds and then politely cuts itself off. It isn't plumbing. It's a recording. And it has a name: **Otohime (音姫)**, the "Sound Princess." It exists for one reason — to mask the noises a human body makes while using a toilet — and it is one of the most successful pieces of acoustic infrastructure ever installed in a developed country. Most foreigners walk straight past it. Most Japanese women press it without thinking, the way Westerners flick a light switch.

## A Princess Named After a Dragon

The device was launched by **TOTO in 1988**, initially as an add-on module for the company's commercial restroom fittings. The name was chosen with the kind of careful poeticism that Japanese product naming tends toward: **Otohime** is the dragon-king's daughter from the Urashima Tarō folktale, a princess who lives in a palace at the bottom of the sea. She is associated with water, with hospitality, and — relevantly — with a degree of mythological discretion. (In the story, she sends Urashima home with a box he is told never to open. He opens it. Things go badly. The metaphor here is not subtle.)

The kanji helps too. *Oto* (音) means "sound." *Hime* (姫) means "princess." Together they read as "Sound Princess," which is gentler and more euphemistic than anything an English-speaking firm would have approved. American Standard would have called it the FlushMask 2000. TOTO chose mythology.

## The Problem It Was Built to Solve

Before 1988, Japanese women dealt with the same acoustic problem using the only tool available: the flush handle. They would flush the toilet repeatedly throughout a single visit, sometimes three or four times, to cover any sound that might escape the stall. The cultural norm — that bodily noise in public was deeply embarrassing — collided directly with the engineering reality that toilets use a lot of water.

TOTO ran the numbers in the mid-1980s and the numbers were ugly. The company estimated that the average courtesy-flushing woman was using roughly **14 litres of extra water per restroom visit**. Scaled across the country, TOTO calculated that this habit accounted for **more than 2.7 million tonnes of wasted water nationally per year**. Municipal water authorities in dense urban prefectures had been quietly raising the issue for years. The Otohime was, in this sense, an environmental product disguised as an etiquette product. By replacing the second, third, and fourth flush with a 25-second audio loop, TOTO estimated each device saved around **6 litres per use**.

That framing — water conservation first, dignity second — was what got it past procurement officers at municipalities, train stations, and office buildings. The dignity, of course, was the part that made people actually press the button.

## What's Inside the Box

The standard Otohime is unromantic hardware: a small wall-mounted unit, usually white or beige, roughly the size of a deck of cards, retailing to commercial buyers for somewhere between **¥10,000 and ¥30,000** depending on sensor type and audio quality. Inside is a speaker, a short loop of audio, and either a push button or — increasingly common since the mid-2000s — a passive infrared **motion sensor** that triggers playback automatically when a hand passes in front of it. The default sound is what TOTO's product documentation calls *seseragi*, the soft babble of a shallow river. Some units offer alternatives: a forest ambience, birdsong, or a "flush simulator" that mimics the sound of the toilet itself without actually using any water.

A 2014 acoustics study from the University of Tokyo's engineering faculty measured the effectiveness of the standard Otohime loop and found it capable of masking incidental sounds **up to around 45 decibels** — roughly the range of normal bodily noise in a tiled, hard-surface restroom. The masking effect works through what acousticians call "informational masking": the brain stops parsing the masked sound as meaningful information once a louder, more recognisable signal (running water) is present. The Sound Princess is, in a sense, a tiny applied-psychology device.

By the time the **Cabinet Office surveyed restroom infrastructure in 2020**, somewhere in the region of **80% of public and office women's toilets in Japan** had an Otohime or a competing unit installed. The competitor worth knowing is **LIXIL's "Etiquette Tone"** (sold under the INAX brand), which occupies the same niche with a slightly different audio palette and is the device you're most likely to encounter in non-TOTO buildings. The market is, effectively, a duopoly.

## The Newer, Stranger Versions

The 2020s have not been kind to the original austere river-babble model. Newer Otohime units, particularly those installed in high-end department stores and luxury hotels, now ship with expanded audio libraries: classical piano, ambient nature soundscapes, the sound of rain on temple roofs, and — in a small number of limited-edition installations — **recorded voices of celebrities** offering brief, polite phrases. TOTO has experimented with seasonal soundscapes (cicadas in summer, falling snow in winter), and a small but real subculture of Japanese restroom enthusiasts catalogues the rarer variants.

Some commercial properties have gone further. A handful of karaoke chains have replaced the standard Otohime audio in their restrooms with **short loops of 1980s J-pop instrumentals** — a thematic crossover with their main business, and a phenomenon we've covered in our piece on [karaoke-equipped toilets](/blog/toilet-karaoke). The same logic shows up in the multi-button bidet seats themselves, which we've broken down in our [guide to the 25-button TOTO toilet](/blog/toto-toilet-25-buttons).

Travellers passing through **Narita and Haneda airports, and Tokyo Station**, will find Otohime units with English-language signage explaining what the button does — a recent addition, driven by the realisation that tourists were either ignoring the device entirely or pressing it repeatedly out of confusion. The signage is uncharacteristically blunt: "Press to mask sound."

## The Cultural Reading

It is tempting, from the outside, to read the Otohime purely as a quirk — another entry in the long list of things Japan over-engineers. The more honest reading is that it sits on top of a much broader cultural attitude toward privacy, embarrassment, and the management of shared space. Japan does not have a "courtesy flush" tradition in the Western sense; the equivalent gesture has been industrialised, automated, and removed from the user's hands. Where an American or a Brit might flush twice and trust the plumbing, a Japanese restroom solves the problem at the level of the building.

There is also a real critique worth airing. Some Japanese commentators — feminist writers in particular — have pointed out that the Otohime is installed almost exclusively in **women's** restrooms, and that this asymmetry quietly reinforces a gendered idea about which bodies are allowed to be audible in public. Men's restrooms, for the most part, do not get the Sound Princess. The same shame is presumed not to apply, or at least not to require infrastructure. This critique has not slowed adoption, but it has produced a small wave of articles in *Asahi Shimbun* and elsewhere asking whether the device is a courtesy or a cage.

Meanwhile, a growing number of younger Japanese travellers have started using **white-noise smartphone apps** as portable Otohime substitutes when abroad — a workaround that says something about how deeply the original device has shaped the expectation of acoustic cover. ## TL;DR

The Otohime is a wall-mounted speaker, launched by TOTO in 1988, that plays roughly 25 seconds of running-water sound to mask the noise of using the toilet. It is named after a dragon princess, replaced a wasteful habit of repeat-flushing that cost the country 2.7 million tonnes of water a year, saves around 6 litres per use, masks sound up to 45 dB, and is now installed in roughly 80% of Japanese women's public restrooms. It is also, depending on who you ask, either a model of considerate design or a small monument to gendered shame. Possibly both.
