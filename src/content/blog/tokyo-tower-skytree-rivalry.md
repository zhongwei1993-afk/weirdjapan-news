# Why Tokyo Has Two Iconic Towers — And the Quiet Rivalry Between Them

Tokyo is one of the only major cities on Earth that built a second, taller broadcast tower without retiring the first. Tokyo Tower (1958, 332.6m, painted orange-and-white in compliance with the Aviation Act) still stands in Minato-ku Shibakoen. Tokyo Skytree (2012, 634m, world's tallest freestanding tower) rises across the river in Sumida-ku Oshiage. They do roughly the same job. They serve roughly the same city. And depending on which neighborhood you grew up in, you are probably loyal to exactly one of them — and quietly suspicious of the other.

The two camps don't talk about it. They don't have to. The skyline does the talking.

## Tokyo Tower: The 1958 Eiffel That Refused to Retire

Tokyo Tower opened on December 23, 1958, designed by structural engineer Tachu Naito — the same man who shaped much of postwar Japan's earthquake-resistant high-rise vocabulary. The brief was direct: build a single broadcast antenna that could serve every TV and radio station in the Kanto region, and make it look like the Eiffel Tower, only taller. Naito delivered both. At 332.6m, it edged out Paris by about 9m and held the title of world's tallest self-supporting steel structure for a brief, satisfying moment.

The lattice steel was painted "international orange" and white in alternating bands, a scheme dictated by Japan's Aviation Act for any structure tall enough to interfere with low-flying aircraft. That paint job is not decorative. It is regulatory. Repainting the tower takes roughly a year, uses around 28,000 liters of paint, and costs an estimated ¥10 million annually in maintenance — a slow, perpetual chore performed by a small crew of climbers who treat the structure like a very tall, very public bridge.

Tickets remain modest by Tokyo standards: ¥1,200 for the Main Deck at 150m, and an additional ¥3,000 if you want to continue up to the Top Deck at 250m. The Main Deck has a small Shinto shrine, a glass floor panel, and a gift shop that has sold the same keychain models since the 1980s. It is, by any honest measurement, dated. That is the point.

## Tokyo Skytree: A Broadcast Tower Disguised as a Statement

Tokyo Skytree opened on May 22, 2012, designed by Nikken Sekkei with a structural concept that borrows from the geometry of traditional five-storied pagodas — a central concrete column (the "shimbashira") that moves independently of the outer steel frame, damping seismic motion. Construction took about four years and cost roughly ¥40 billion. At 634m, the height is not arbitrary: 6-3-4 reads as "mu-sa-shi" in old Japanese counting, a reference to Musashi, the historical name for the region the tower sits in.

The official reason Skytree exists is unromantic. By the late 2000s, Tokyo's skyline had filled in with high-rises tall enough to interfere with Tokyo Tower's terrestrial broadcast signal, particularly after the transition from analog to digital TV. NHK and the major commercial networks needed a transmitter that cleared every roofline in central Tokyo by a comfortable margin. Skytree was built specifically to replace Tower as the primary broadcast antenna for digital terrestrial television.

Tower was not decommissioned. It was kept on as a backup transmitter, a role it still quietly fulfills today. If Skytree ever goes offline, the 1958 lattice in Shibakoen can be brought back into full broadcast service within minutes. Tokyo simply decided it was easier to maintain two towers than to gamble on one.

Skytree tickets run higher: ¥2,100 for the Tembo Deck at 350F, and ¥3,400 if you continue to the Tembo Galleria at 450F — a sloping spiral walkway that climbs the final 100m along the outside of the structure. The view is, predictably, enormous.

## The Earthquake That Proved the Design

In March 2011, the Tohoku earthquake — magnitude 9.0 — struck while Skytree was 60% complete. The unfinished tower swayed visibly from across the city. When the shaking stopped, structural surveys found no measurable damage. The shimbashira system, designed to handle exactly this scenario, had done its job during the worst real-world test it could have been given. Construction resumed on schedule. The story is rarely told in Skytree marketing, which is itself very Japanese: the engineering worked, so there is nothing to discuss.

Tokyo Tower also survived the same quake, with one visible exception — the antenna at the very top bent slightly to one side. It was straightened within months. The bend is no longer there, but older Tokyoites will still point to where it was.

## Visitors, Districts, and Two Very Different Days Out

Skytree pulls in roughly 5.8 million visitors a year. Tokyo Tower pulls in roughly 3 million. The numbers are not the whole story.

Skytree sits at the base of Tokyo Solamachi, a 300-shop commercial complex that connects directly to Oshiage Station, with Asakusa and the Sumida River a fifteen-minute walk west. A day at Skytree tends to include Senso-ji temple, a river cruise, and dinner in Asakusa. The crowd skews international and family-oriented. The scale of everything — the tower, the mall, the plaza — is built for foot traffic measured in thousands per hour.

Tokyo Tower sits beside Zojoji, a 14th-century temple whose main hall frames the tower from below in a composition that has appeared on roughly every Tokyo postcard since 1959. Roppongi, with its bars and Mori Art Museum, is a fifteen-minute walk north. A day at Tokyo Tower is quieter, slightly nostalgic, and ends in an izakaya rather than a food court. The crowd skews local, older, and on dates.

Both towers have restaurants at altitude. Skytree's Sky Restaurant 634 at 345m runs French-Japanese tasting menus that require booking weeks ahead. Tokyo Tower's Top Deck Tour includes a glass of sparkling wine and a guided elevator ride. The difference in tone tells you everything.

## Pop Culture, Paint, and the Tokyo Skyline Race

Tokyo Tower is the more cinematic of the two, partly because it has had a 54-year head start. It appears in *Sailor Moon*, *Death Note*, *Magic Knight Rayearth*, *Please Save My Earth*, and roughly every other anime set in modern Tokyo. When a kaiju attacks Tokyo on screen, it usually attacks Tokyo Tower. Skytree, by contrast, dominated the visual identity of the Tokyo 2020 Olympics broadcast packages and shows up in newer series — *Tokyo Revengers*, *Chainsaw Man* — as a kind of architectural shorthand for the present-tense city.

Both towers run nightly LED light shows. Skytree alternates between "Iki" (sky blue) and "Miyabi" (purple), with seasonal specials. Tokyo Tower runs "Landmark Light" (warm orange) on weekdays and "Diamond Veil" (white) on weekends. Neither tower coordinates with the other. They do not need to.

Meanwhile, the broader Tokyo skyline race continues without either of them. Mori Tower in Roppongi Hills hit 238m in 2003. Toranomon Hills hit 256m in 2014. Azabudai Hills Mori JP Tower opened in 2023 at 330m, briefly becoming Japan's tallest building — and sitting almost exactly between Tower and Skytree on a map. Tokyo Cross Park, planned for 2032, is currently slated to top out at 350m. None of these are broadcast towers. They are office buildings with observation decks bolted on, which is a different kind of structure entirely, and arguably a different kind of ambition.

Foreign visitors increasingly sort themselves between three rooftops: Tower for nostalgia, Skytree for sheer scale, and Azabudai for design. The locals, as always, sort themselves by train line.

## The "Which Is Better" Debate, and Where Things Stand in 2026

Ask a Tokyoite which tower is better and you will get a polite, evasive answer that depends entirely on which side of the Sumida River they live on. The honest answer is that the two towers solve different problems. Tower is the city's memory. Skytree is the city's signal. Tower is what Tokyo looked like when it was rebuilding. Skytree is what Tokyo looked like when it had finished.

Neither is going anywhere. Tower's backup-transmitter status has been quietly renewed through 2030. Skytree's lease on its primary broadcast role is effectively indefinite. The two towers will keep blinking at each other across the skyline for the foreseeable future, and the two camps of loyal locals will keep not talking about it.

That, more than the height or the price or the engineering, is the most Tokyo thing about the whole arrangement.

## TL;DR

Tokyo built Skytree (634m, 2012) to replace Tokyo Tower (332.6m, 1958) as its primary broadcast antenna, then kept Tower on as a backup. Skytree wins on scale and visitor numbers (5.8M vs 3M). Tower wins on anime cameos and Zojoji photos. Both are still operational, both are still loved, and almost nobody admits to liking both equally.
