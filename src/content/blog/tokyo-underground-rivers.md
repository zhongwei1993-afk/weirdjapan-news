# Tokyo Has 50+ Underground Rivers. Most Are Sealed.

Walk down Cat Street in Harajuku on a Saturday afternoon. The boutiques sell ¥40,000 sneakers. The street curves gently, for no obvious reason, between Omotesando and Shibuya Station. Nobody is thinking about the river underneath their feet. But it's there — the Onden-gawa, sealed under concrete in 1964, still moving water toward Tokyo Bay at roughly the pace of a slow walk. You're standing on top of it. So is everyone else.

Edo, the city Tokyo used to be, was built on water. Roughly 40 rivers and major canals threaded through it, feeding a network of moats, fish markets, and bathhouses. Most of them are still there. They're just under a lid.

## A City That Quietly Buried Its Own Waterways

The official Tokyo Metropolitan Government inventory lists more than fifty waterways that have been either fully buried, capped with concrete, or rerouted into culverts since the early 20th century. The pattern accelerated after 1945 and again before 1964, when the Summer Olympics pushed the city into a construction frenzy that swallowed neighborhoods, hills, and most of the small streams that used to run through them.

The Onden-gawa is the most famous casualty. Until 1964, it ran above ground from Yoyogi down through what is now Jingumae, joining the Shibuya River near the present-day station. The Olympic preparations sealed it. Today it flows under the Meiji-jingumae intersection and the entire length of Cat Street — which gets its lazy, river-shaped curve from the channel below. Stand at the intersection of Cat Street and Omotesando and look at how the asphalt dips slightly. That dip is the riverbed.

A handful of waterways escaped. The Sumida-gawa still moves through eastern Tokyo in the open, wide enough to carry the summer fireworks crowd. The Nigori-gawa and Sasame-gawa survive in patches, mostly because they fed working districts where reburial was inconvenient. The Karasuyama-gawa runs through Setagaya in something like its original state, a narrow stream lined with cherry trees, walkable for several kilometers if you know where to find the entrance.

Everything else is underground.

## The Four Reasons Tokyo Put Its Rivers Underground

There is no single villain in this story. Tokyo buried its rivers for four overlapping reasons, in roughly this order:

**Post-war sewage.** After 1945, the city's population doubled in under a decade. The rivers, already overloaded, became open sewers. Capping them was cheaper than cleaning them.

**1964 Olympics infrastructure.** The new expressways, subway lines, and arterial roads needed routes through dense neighborhoods. Riverbeds were the only land nobody owned. Engineers laid concrete decks over the channels and ran roads on top — most visibly the Shuto Expressway, which threads above the Nihonbashi River on a low gray viaduct that has been an aesthetic embarrassment to Tokyo for sixty years.

**2000s flood control.** As surface paving sealed off natural drainage, rainwater that used to soak into soil now sheets across asphalt. The response was to redirect rivers into deeper, faster engineered tunnels. The lid stayed on; the volume grew.

**Real-estate value.** A capped river is buildable land. Tokyo land prices have made every horizontal meter precious enough that "river" eventually lost to "convenience store parking lot" in most negotiations.

None of these decisions were dramatic. They were each made quietly, by separate agencies, over decades. The result is a city that sits on top of its own circulatory system without thinking about it.

## The Underground Pantheon: G-Cans in Kasukabe

If Tokyo's buried rivers are the small story, the Metropolitan Outer Area Underground Discharge Channel is the loud one. Locals call it G-Cans, or G-Can'zu. It opened in 2006 in Kasukabe, Saitama — about an hour north of central Tokyo on the train — and it is the largest underground flood-diversion system in the world.

The numbers are the kind of thing the Japanese government understates in pamphlets. Six and a half kilometers of tunnel. Seventy meters below ground. Five concrete silos, each 32 meters wide and 70 meters tall, connected by a cathedral-sized pressure-adjustment chamber held up by 59 pillars weighing 500 tons each. Western visitors who tour it tend to use the same phrase: "Underground Pantheon." The Japanese tour guides have given up trying to discourage the comparison.

It floods, on average, about twice a year. The system catches overflow from five rivers that would otherwise drown the eastern suburbs, then pumps it into the larger Edo River when the storm passes. Between floods, it's dry and tourable.

Tours run roughly daily through the Edogawa River Office, with English options available by reservation. Cost is about ¥1,000 for the standard tour, more for the deeper routes that take you down into the silos themselves. Saturday and Sunday slots fill fastest and require advance booking. Foreign visitors usually go via JR to Saitama-Shintoshin and transfer; the tourist office can arrange shuttle access.

If you only see one piece of Tokyo's water infrastructure, see this one. It is the most cinematic public works project in Japan, and almost nobody outside the country knows it exists.

## Ghost Bridges, Ghost Streets, Ghost Wells

Once you know the rivers are there, you start seeing them everywhere — in the wrong things.

Itabashi means "plank bridge." Korakuen sits on top of a former garden moat. Ochanomizu was named for a well that fed the shogun's tea. Hundreds of Tokyo place names refer to water that is no longer visible. The bridges are still in the names; the bridges themselves were demolished, in some cases, before the war.

You can also spot covered rivers by reading the street geometry. Look for streets that curve gently in a city that otherwise grids itself. Look for sudden dips in elevation — a sidewalk that drops twenty centimeters for no apparent reason, then climbs back up. Look for narrow service alleys that run in suspicious straight lines through otherwise chaotic blocks. Those are usually old riverbeds. Cat Street is the textbook example; the back alleys of Nakameguro hide several others.

The Ringo-ichi well, tucked behind a small shrine in the old Yanaka district, is one of the few open access points to Tokyo's groundwater that remains in active neighborhood use. Locals draw water from it for the New Year. The well sits on top of a stream that was buried in 1952. Nobody has officially confirmed the connection, but the water level rises predictably after heavy rain.

Tunnel ghost stories are an entire genre. The most persistent is the Kanda water-tunnel taxi murder cycle — a set of urban legends about drivers picking up female passengers near tunnel entrances and arriving at the destination alone. Maintenance crews who work the deeper culverts have their own folklore, mostly about footsteps that don't belong to anyone on the shift roster. The stories are too consistent across unrelated work crews to be entirely invented and too vague to be entirely true. They sit, like the rivers, in a layer just under the visible city.

## Uncovering Nihonbashi, and the Aqua-Line Going the Other Way

The Tokyo Metropolitan Government has now committed roughly ¥320 billion, over a project window ending in 2040, to remove the Shuto Expressway concrete cap above the Nihonbashi River. The expressway is being rerouted underground; the river will return to open sky for the first time since 1963. It is the largest river-uncovering project in Japanese history and a quiet admission that the 1964 decisions were not, in retrospect, the right ones.

While Nihonbashi comes up, other things keep going down. The Tokyo Bay Aqua-Line, opened in 1997, is a 9.5-kilometer combined bridge and undersea tunnel connecting Kawasaki to Kisarazu across Tokyo Bay. The toll is about ¥3,000 each way. From the rest stop in the middle — Umihotaru, built on a man-made island over the tunnel portal — you can look down and not see the tunnel, because the tunnel is the point. Japan is comfortable building infrastructure you cannot see. That comfort is the through-line.

## TL;DR

Tokyo has more than fifty rivers underneath it. Most were buried for sewage, then for the 1964 Olympics, then for flood control, then for land value. The Onden-gawa runs under Cat Street. The Sumida, Nigori, and Sasame survived above ground. The G-Cans flood tunnel in Kasukabe is the largest of its kind in the world and tourable for about ¥1,000. The Nihonbashi River is being uncovered by 2040. Street curves, dips, and place names ending in -bashi are the last visible traces of a city built on top of its water.
