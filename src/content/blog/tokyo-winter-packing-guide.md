# What to Pack for Tokyo in Winter: The 14 Things Tourists Forget

Tokyo winter is colder than tourists expect. Not New York cold — but the indoor heating is weaker, the streets are windier, and you'll be outside more than you think. Pack for that, not for the temperature.

This is the part most packing lists get wrong. They look at the forecast — "5–10°C, sunny" — and conclude you can get away with a light jacket. Then you land, walk out of Narita, take a train into Shinjuku, and within twelve hours your lips are cracked, your phone is dead, and you've discovered that the café you're warming up in keeps its thermostat at a brisk 18°C.

Here's what to actually pack — and just as importantly, what to leave at home.

## What Tokyo Winter Is Actually Like

Some honest numbers before the list:

- **Daytime:** 5–10°C (41–50°F). Sunny most days.
- **Nighttime:** Can dip below 0°C (32°F), especially January and early February.
- **Humidity:** 30–40%. This is the part nobody warns you about — your skin will dry out fast.
- **Snow in central Tokyo:** Rare. A few flurries per winter, maybe one real snow day. Hakone and the mountains are a different story.
- **Daylight:** About 11 hours. Sunset is around 4:45pm in late December. Plan accordingly.
- **Indoor heating:** Notably weaker than in North America. Trains are warm. Cafés are lukewarm. Old izakaya can be downright chilly.

The combined effect: you don't need a polar coat, but you need *layers you can adjust constantly* — and the small accessories that make the difference between miserable and comfortable.

## The 14 Things Every Guide Skips

### 1. Heattech-style thermal base layers

Uniqlo Heattech is the local original — about ¥1,500 per piece, sold everywhere from airport terminals to neighborhood Uniqlos. A long-sleeve top and leggings under your normal clothes will do more than any single coat upgrade. The fabric is thin enough to wear under jeans without bulk.

If you want to arrive prepared (and not hunt for your size on day one), grab a set before you fly.

[Heattech-style thermal base layers on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=thermal+base+layer+winter&tag=weirdjapan20-20)

### 2. Pocket kairo (hand warmers)

Japanese disposable hand warmers — *kairo* (カイロ) — are a national winter institution. They cost about ¥30–¥50 each at any konbini, last 10–12 hours, and come in both pocket-shake and stick-on body versions. Stash one in each coat pocket. Stick one to your lower back if you're walking a lot.

You'll find them everywhere once you arrive, but having a small pack on day one — before you know which konbini is closest — is worth the suitcase space.

[Travel-size hand warmers on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=hand+warmers+disposable&tag=weirdjapan20-20)

### 3. A compact umbrella

Tokyo winter rain is rare but biting — cold drizzles that show up without much warning and turn an outdoor wait into a problem. A small folding umbrella that lives in your day bag is enough. (If you forget, every konbini sells a ¥500 clear plastic one.)

### 4. Lip balm and hand cream

This is the item travelers most consistently underestimate. At 30–40% humidity, your lips will crack within 48 hours and your knuckles will follow. A small tube of lip balm and a travel-size hand cream weigh almost nothing and save the trip.

### 5. Slip-on shoes

You'll take your shoes off more than you think — at temples, at ryokan, at some izakaya, at certain tatami-floored restaurants, and inside fitting rooms. Lace-up boots become a chore by day three. Slip-ons, side-zip boots, or anything you can pop off in one motion will earn their place.

### 6. A small wallet with a coin slot

Japan still loves coins. After a week, your fat foreign wallet will be straining at the seams with ¥1, ¥5, ¥10, ¥50, ¥100, and ¥500 pieces. A compact travel wallet with a dedicated coin pouch — or a small zipped purse — keeps things organized and makes you faster at the checkout. (Pair with your IC card for trains.)

### 7. A travel SIM or pocket Wi-Fi

Public Wi-Fi in Tokyo exists but is patchy, slow, and often requires registration in Japanese. A travel eSIM or a rented pocket Wi-Fi unit — picked up at the airport on arrival — is dramatically better. Pocket Wi-Fi is especially worth it if you're traveling as a couple or group sharing one connection.

### 8. A plug adapter (Type A, 100V)

Japan uses **Type A** plugs (two flat prongs) at 100 volts. If you're coming from the US, your plugs fit but voltage is slightly lower — fine for phones and laptops, sometimes finicky for hair tools. If you're coming from Europe, Australia, or the UK, you need an adapter. A small Type A adapter is a few dollars and weighs nothing.

### 9. A small backpack you can wear in front on the train

Tokyo trains have an unspoken rule: at rush hour, you swing your backpack around to your chest. It frees up space behind you and keeps the bag out of other people's faces. A streamlined daypack that's comfortable worn front-side is genuinely useful. Avoid anything bulky or with side pockets that snag.

### 10. A foldable tote bag

Konbini hauls. Don Quijote runs. The Kit Kat aisle. Last-minute groceries before a long train ride. Japan started charging for plastic bags in 2020, and a small foldable tote that lives in your daypack will pay for itself by day two. It also handles the inevitable luggage overflow on the way home.

### 11. Touchscreen-compatible gloves

Light gloves, not ski gloves. You'll be checking Google Maps every five minutes, scanning QR menus, and tapping your IC card. Gloves you have to take off every time are a slow misery. Any pair with conductive fingertips works.

### 12. A reusable water bottle

Tokyo's tap water is genuinely drinkable — it's tested aggressively and tastes fine. Refilling at your hotel saves money and plastic. Bonus: there are surprisingly few public water fountains, but cafés will refill a bottle if you ask politely.

### 13. A coat with zipper pockets

Tokyo is one of the safest big cities in the world — pickpocketing is rare. But a packed Yamanote line at 8am can shake a phone or wallet out of a button-flap pocket without anybody intending malice. Zippered pockets on your main coat solve the problem completely. This is a small detail that quietly removes a whole category of trip-ending mishaps.

### 14. An extra phone battery

Google Maps + Google Translate + the camera + a translation app + the train app will absolutely destroy your battery. Cold weather makes it worse. A small 10,000mAh power bank is the difference between confidently navigating Shinjuku Station at 6pm and standing outside a 7-Eleven trying to find an outlet.

## What You DON'T Need to Pack

Most over-packers bring at least three of these. None are necessary.

- **Heavy snow boots.** Central Tokyo gets little to no snow. Regular waterproof shoes are more than enough. If you're going to Hakone or Nikko, you can rent.
- **A thick parka rated for –20°C.** Overkill, and you'll roast on the train. A medium-weight coat plus Heattech base layers is the right call.
- **Foreign currency in large amounts.** ATMs at 7-Eleven and Japan Post work with foreign cards and have English menus. Bring a small reserve, not a stack.
- **Paper maps.** Google Maps in Japan is excellent — train transfers, exit numbers, walking directions, the lot.
- **An international SIM you bought at home.** Almost always more expensive and slower than a Japan-specific eSIM or pocket Wi-Fi rental.
- **Multiple pairs of jeans.** Two pairs, max. You won't use the third.
- **A hairdryer.** Every hotel has one. Even the budget ones.

## Buy-on-Arrival Beats Pack-It-All

A lot of "essentials" are easier — and often cheaper — to buy after you land. Tokyo is a city built for last-mile shopping:

- **Don Quijote (ドン・キホーテ)** — open late, sells everything from snacks to suitcases to thermal underwear. Tourist-tax-free with your passport.
- **Loft** — for thoughtful, design-y travel goods, stationery, and small electronics.
- **Tokyu Hands** — the slightly more grown-up version of Loft. Excellent for travel gear.
- **Uniqlo** — Heattech, fleece, light puffer jackets. The flagship in Ginza has 12 floors. Sizes run smaller than North American Uniqlos, so size up.
- **Any konbini (7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart)** — kairo, umbrellas, lip balm, snacks, phone chargers, socks.

A useful rule: pack one of everything for your first 24 hours, then resupply locally.

## TL;DR

> Tokyo winter isn't extreme — it's deceptive. You don't need an arctic coat; you need thermal base layers, hand warmers, lip balm, slip-on shoes, touchscreen gloves, a real power bank, and a coat with zipped pockets. Skip the snow boots, paper maps, and foreign cash stack. Buy the rest at Don Quijote on day one.
