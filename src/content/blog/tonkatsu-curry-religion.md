# Tonkatsu Curry: Japan's Brown Comfort Food Religion

A breaded pork cutlet, sliced into neat fingers. A thick brown sauce that is technically curry but tastes nothing like anything served in India or Thailand. A mound of short-grain white rice. That is the dish. There is a chain restaurant in Japan that serves a version of it in over 1,200 locations. There are also tonkatsu specialty shops in Tokyo where the cutlet alone costs more than a steak dinner in most countries.

It is one of the most-eaten meals in Japan, and almost nobody outside Japan has heard of it.

## A Dish Built From Two Borrowed Things

Katsu-curry is a fusion of two foods that Japan did not invent and then absolutely refused to give back. The curry came from the British. The cutlet came from the Austrians, via the French, via a chef in Ginza. The combination came from a baseball player who could not decide what to order.

None of this is the kind of origin story Japan tends to advertise. There is no temple to katsu-curry, no UNESCO designation, no JR poster campaign. There is just the dish, sitting on tens of millions of trays every week, behaving as if it has always been there.

The Japanese word for it — **katsu-karē** (カツカレー) — is itself a fusion: a Japanese clipping of the English "cutlet" stuck to a Japanese rendering of the English word "curry." Two loanwords holding hands.

## The British Navy Brought the Curry (1870s)

Japanese curry does not come from India. It comes from the Royal Navy.

In the late Meiji era, around the 1870s, the Imperial Japanese Navy was busy copying the British Navy in every detail it could find, from uniforms to gun calibres to lunch. British sailors ate a thickened, flour-roux curry stew that had been adapted from Indian recipes during the colonial period — a beige, mild, gravy-like thing designed to keep on a moving ship without spoiling.

Japanese naval cooks adopted it wholesale. It was nutritious, it stretched cheap ingredients, and crucially it solved the navy's beriberi problem because the wheat flour roux delivered vitamin B1. By the early 20th century, **curry rice (karē raisu)** was being served on Friday in every Japanese naval mess. The Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force still does this. They publish the recipes online.

From the navy, the dish escaped into school lunches, then home kitchens, then the instant-roux aisle of every supermarket in the country. By the 1950s, Japanese curry was no longer thought of as foreign. It was just dinner.

## The Cutlet Started at Rengatei in Ginza (1899)

Tonkatsu — literally "pork cutlet" — was first served in 1899 at **Rengatei**, a yōshoku (Western-style) restaurant in Ginza, Tokyo's Chuo ward. The chef adapted the Austrian wiener schnitzel and the French côtelette into something deep-fried in panko, served thick, and eaten with a knife and fork.

Rengatei is still open. It is a small, slightly creaky building two minutes from Ginza station, and it also takes credit for inventing omurice. The original 1899 cutlet was called katsuretsu (a phonetic borrowing of "cutlet") and was eaten the European way. Sometime around the 1920s, a competing restaurant began slicing the cutlet into bite-sized strips, serving it with shredded cabbage and rice, and giving customers chopsticks. That is the form that won.

## A Baseball Player Combined Them in 1948

The katsu-curry as a single dish was invented in 1948 at **Grill Swiss** (グリルスイス), a yōshoku restaurant in Ginza that is also still open.

The story, which the restaurant itself tells on its menu, is that the professional baseball player **Chiba Shigeru** was a regular customer. One day he ordered a tonkatsu and a plate of curry rice separately, then asked the kitchen to please just put the cutlet on top of the curry next time to save him the trouble. The kitchen did. Other customers saw it, asked for the same thing, and within a few years it was a standard menu item across Tokyo.

Grill Swiss still serves the original on a plate labelled "Chiba-san's Katsu-Curry." It costs around ¥1,800. The baseball player died in 2002. The dish did not.

## CoCo Ichibanya: The World's Largest Curry Chain

The most-eaten version of Japanese curry in the world is served by a chain called **CoCo Ichibanya** — usually shortened to **CoCo Ichi** (ココイチ). It was founded in 1978 in Aichi prefecture by **Tokuji Soga** and his wife Naomi. There are now over 1,200 locations across Japan and another 200-plus internationally, including Shanghai, Bangkok, London, and Honolulu.

CoCo Ichi is, by store count, the largest curry restaurant chain on the planet. Guinness has certified this.

Ordering at CoCo Ichi is a customization ritual that visitors find either delightful or paralyzing. You choose:

- **Rice size**: 200g, 300g, 400g, up to 1,300g (the 1.3kg challenge is free if you finish it in 20 minutes)
- **Spice level**: 1 to 10. Levels 6 and above require having ordered level 5 previously, on the honor system
- **Toppings**: Tonkatsu is the most popular. Then chicken katsu, then fried shrimp, then natto, then okra, then 30 other options.

A standard pork katsu-curry at CoCo Ichi costs roughly ¥1,100. It is reliable, mid-tier, and exactly the same in Sapporo as it is in Naha. Japanese people eat it without ceremony, the way Americans eat at Chipotle.

## Regional Variations Worth Tracking

Outside the Tokyo standard, two regional curry traditions have built their own followings.

**Hokkaido soup curry** (Sapporo, since the 1970s) is a completely different format. The roux is dropped entirely. Instead the curry is served as a thin, aromatic broth in a bowl, with whole roasted vegetables, a chicken leg or pork cutlet, and rice on the side that you spoon in. It tastes more like a Sri Lankan or Thai curry than anything resembling Japanese karē raisu.

**Kanazawa "Gold Curry"** (Ishikawa prefecture) goes the opposite direction. The sauce is darker, thicker, almost black, served on a metal plate with a fork instead of a spoon, and topped with shredded cabbage that sits directly under the cutlet. The reigning chain is Go Go Curry, which opened its first Tokyo branch in 2004 and now operates in New York as well.

## The Cultural Status of a Double-Imported Dish

Katsu-curry is on every Japanese restaurant survey's "comfort food" shortlist, alongside ramen, gyōza, and omurice. It is served in elementary school cafeterias. It is the most-ordered single item at karaoke chains with food menus. It is what salarymen eat alone at 9pm at counter seats.

This is, on its face, strange. The two parent dishes are both Western imports, adopted within the last 150 years, neither of which existed in any form in Edo-period Japan. And yet katsu-curry is described, sincerely and without irony, as **soul food**. Surveys regularly place it in the top five "foods that remind Japanese people of home."

The dish has been Japanese for so long, and refined so thoroughly, that the question of where it came from has stopped being interesting. It is now just what is for lunch.

## Six Tokyo Katsu-Curry Spots Worth the Detour

Prices below are for the katsu-curry set, including rice and miso soup, as of 2026.

- **Imakatsu** (今勝) — Roppongi, Minato. Famous for its thick-cut Iberico pork katsu. The curry version is ¥2,400. Counter seats only at the original branch.
- **Tonkatsu Maisen** (とんかつまい泉) — Aoyama, Minato. A 60-year-old Showa institution housed in a former public bathhouse. Katsu-curry from ¥2,100. Multiple branches including in Isetan and Tokyo Station.
- **Katsukura** (かつくら) — Shinjuku Takashimaya, 14F. Kyoto-based chain known for its sesame-grinding ritual. Katsu-curry around ¥2,300.
- **Butagumi** (豚組) — Nishi-Azabu, Minato. A single-pork-cutlet temple inside a converted house. Curry is a special menu item only; ¥3,200. Reservations essential.
- **Tonki** (とんき) — Meguro. The 1939 original. No menu — they ask if you want rosu (loin) or hire (fillet). Curry is not standard but available off-menu; standard set ¥2,000.
- **Narikura** (成蔵) — Takadanobaba, Shinjuku. Constant Tabelog top-50 finisher. Katsu-curry ¥2,500. Expect a 90-minute wait at lunch.

## How to Order Without Embarrassing Yourself

At a tonkatsu specialty shop, you will usually be asked to choose between **rōsu** (ロース, loin, fattier) and **hire** (ヒレ, fillet, leaner). Pick rōsu the first time. The rice and miso soup are almost always free refills (okawari) — the staff will not offer; you have to ask.

At CoCo Ichi, point at the picture menu, mumble a spice number, and you are done. The whole interaction takes about 40 seconds and is, by design, foreigner-proof.

## TL;DR

- Katsu-curry combines two 19th-century imports — British naval curry and Austrian-derived schnitzel — into a single 1948 dish.
- Invented at Grill Swiss in Ginza when baseball player Chiba Shigeru asked the kitchen to combine his two orders.
- CoCo Ichibanya, founded 1978 in Aichi, is the world's largest curry chain with 1,200-plus locations.
- Regional variants include Hokkaido soup curry and Kanazawa's black "Gold Curry."
- Despite being entirely imported in origin, Japanese people consistently rank it as soul food.
