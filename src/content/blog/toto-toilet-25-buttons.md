# Japan's Toilet Has 25 Buttons. Here's What Each One Does.

The first time a foreign visitor sits on a Japanese toilet, three things usually happen in quick succession. The seat is warm, which is alarming. A small panel of unlabeled icons glows beside the right thigh, which is unhelpful. And somewhere in the porcelain, a tiny speaker begins playing what sounds like a recording of a waterfall, which is, depending on your background, either soothing or deeply confusing.

This is the Washlet. It has been the default toilet in Japan for roughly a generation. It has 25 buttons, give or take, and each one exists for a reason that, on close inspection, is more practical than weird.

## TL;DR

- TOTO was founded in 1917 in Kitakyushu and launched the Washlet in 1980.
- The 1982 ad campaign — "Oshiri datte arattehoshii" ("Even your butt wants to be washed") — normalized the product on prime-time TV.
- Cabinet Office household surveys now put washlet penetration at roughly 80% of Japanese homes.
- The control panel typically covers 20 to 25 functions, from oscillating rear cleanse to a privacy soundtrack called Otohime.
- Exports have been slow, partly because Western consumers find the interface uncanny and partly because the buttons used to be labeled only in Japanese.

## A Company Built on Porcelain

TOTO — short for Toyo Toki, "Oriental Ceramics" — was founded in 1917 in Kitakyushu, a heavy-industry port city on the northern tip of Kyushu. For its first six decades it made what ceramic companies make: sinks, tubs, urinals, and the kind of unremarkable Western-style toilet that had begun replacing the traditional squat fixture across urban Japan.

The interesting turn came in 1964, when TOTO began importing an American medical-grade bidet seat called the Wash Air Seat, originally designed for patients with limited mobility. The device was niche, expensive, and unreliable. TOTO bought the rights, brought the engineering in-house, and spent the next sixteen years redesigning every component. The result, released in June 1980, was the Washlet G Series — a self-contained heated seat with an integrated warm-water spray, controlled by a side panel.

It sold poorly at first. Japanese consumers, like everyone else, did not particularly want to discuss what the device did out loud.

## The Ad That Changed Everything

In 1982, TOTO ran a television commercial that is now considered one of the boldest in postwar Japanese advertising. Actress Jun Togawa appeared on screen and delivered the tagline directly: "Oshiri datte, arattehoshii." Roughly: "Even your butt wants to be washed."

Broadcasters initially refused to air it during dinner hours. TOTO pushed back, the spot ran, and within three years Washlet sales had multiplied. By the late 1990s the product had moved from luxury appliance to standard-issue bathroom hardware, installed in new condominiums by default and retrofitted into older homes one bathroom at a time.

According to the Cabinet Office's annual Consumer Confidence Survey, which tracks household ownership of major durable goods, washlet-style toilet seats are now present in roughly 80% of Japanese households — a higher penetration rate than dishwashers or clothes dryers. In hotels, train stations, convenience stores, department stores, and most government offices, the Washlet is not an upgrade. It is the baseline.

## The 25-Button Anatomy

A mid-range Washlet control panel — the kind found in a business hotel or a middle-class apartment — typically offers the following:

- **Rear cleanse** (oshiri): warm-water spray aimed at the back.
- **Soft rear cleanse** (yawaraka): same spray, lower pressure, for sensitive use.
- **Front cleanse** (bidet): a separate nozzle position for women.
- **Pulsating** (massage): the spray turns on and off in rhythm.
- **Oscillating** (move): the nozzle slides back and forth during use.
- **Water pressure**: usually five levels, marked plus and minus.
- **Water temperature**: typically three to five levels.
- **Nozzle position**: forward or back, in fine increments.
- **Seat temperature**: off, low, medium, high, with a winter setting on premium models.
- **Warm-air dryer**: with its own temperature scale.
- **Deodorizer**: a small fan that pulls air through a carbon filter.
- **Lid auto-open / auto-close**: triggered by a motion sensor.
- **Music or Otohime**: a looping flushing sound that masks bodily noise without wasting water. See [the Otohime explainer](/blog/toilet-music-otohime).
- **Flush, small / flush, big**: two water volumes, often on a separate wall panel.
- **Stop**: the largest and most important button on the entire interface.
- **Nozzle clean**: a self-rinse cycle that runs before and after each use.

That comes to roughly 20 distinct functions, expanded to 25 once you count power, energy-saving mode, child mode, and the small status indicators that double as buttons on touch-sensitive panels.

The "stop" button is almost always red, almost always oversized, and almost always the first thing a first-time user fails to find.

## The Competition and the Price Ladder

TOTO is dominant but not alone. Its main rival is LIXIL, the conglomerate formed in 2011 from the merger of five companies including INAX, the second-largest porcelain maker in Japan. LIXIL's Satis line competes directly with TOTO's Neorest at the top of the market, while Panasonic, Toshiba, and Janis fill the budget end.

A bare-bones replacement seat from a home center starts at around ¥30,000. A mid-range unit with auto-open lid, deodorizer, and remote control runs ¥80,000 to ¥150,000. The flagship integrated toilets — Neorest NX, Satis G-Type, the tankless models with self-cleaning ceramic glaze and ambient lighting — top out near ¥300,000 before installation. For comparison, a basic Western-style flush toilet in the same store sells for under ¥20,000.

The price gap is one reason penetration plateaued around 80% rather than continuing toward 100%. The other reason is rural housing stock, which sometimes lacks the electrical outlet a Washlet requires.

## A Cultural Export That Refuses to Travel

When Tokyo won the 2020 Olympics, the organizing committee briefly considered the Washlet a soft-power asset on par with sushi and Shinkansen. TOTO ran international campaigns. Hotels in the host city upgraded units. The expectation was that returning visitors would demand the product back home.

This has not happened at scale. American and European buyers consistently describe the Washlet as either intimidating or excessive, and online reviews skew toward variants of "I do not need my toilet to do this." Plumbing codes in much of North America make retrofit installation expensive. And the interface itself — those rows of small pictograms — has historically been printed only in Japanese, which limits adoption in markets where the user cannot read the panel.

The English-misprint stories have not helped. Visitors regularly photograph buttons translated as "BOTTOM WASH" and "FANNY WASH" and, in one widely circulated case from a Kyoto ryokan, "POWERFUL DELUGE." The signs end up on Reddit. TOTO's overseas marketing team has spent years trying to walk that back.

In 2017, the Japan Sanitary Equipment Industry Association — the trade body that represents TOTO, LIXIL, Panasonic, and the rest — announced a voluntary pictogram standard. Eight icons were agreed on: rear cleanse, front cleanse, dryer, stop, flush large, flush small, raise lid, raise seat. The standard was timed for the Olympics and the surge of foreign visitors expected with it. It has since spread across new installations in airports, train stations, and major hotel chains, though older panels in private homes and small businesses remain idiosyncratic.

So if you sit down in a Tokyo apartment in 2026 and see icons you do not recognize, the answer is usually the same one it has been since 1980. The big red button is stop. Everything else can wait until you have time to experiment.
