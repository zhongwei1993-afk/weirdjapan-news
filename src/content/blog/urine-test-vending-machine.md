# The Vending Machine That Tests Your Urine for Cancer

On the second basement floor of a Shinjuku office building, between an umbrella vending machine and a bank of cold-brew coffee dispensers, there is a tall white cabinet with a touchscreen, a small slot for a paper cup, and a price list starting at ¥500. It is, by every reasonable definition, a public toilet attached to a clinical lab. Office workers use it on the way back from lunch. No one looks twice.

This is not a tourist installation. It is one node in a quietly enormous Japanese category — over-the-counter medical diagnostics, dispensed by machine — that has been building for a decade and is now starting to disappear into your wristwatch.

## A country built on vending machines

Japan operates roughly four million vending machines. That is the often-cited figure from the Japan Vending System Manufacturers Association, and even with recent declines it works out to about one machine for every thirty people — the highest density on earth. Most of them sell drinks. A meaningful minority sell other things: hot ramen, raw eggs, fishing bait, neckties, surgical masks, fresh oysters, mystery boxes of expired snack food.

The reasons are well-rehearsed. Low street crime makes unattended cash boxes viable. High urban land prices make a 0.5-square-metre footprint extremely attractive. A shrinking retail workforce makes 24-hour staffing impossible. A cultural comfort with self-service — refined across decades of station kiosks, ticket machines, and conveyor-belt sushi — means almost no one needs the transaction explained.

Once you have that infrastructure, you start running stranger things through it. Diagnostic medicine is just the latest payload.

## The rise of the home-test machine

The "self-care vending machine" — *serufu kea jihanki* — emerged around 2015, mostly inside drugstore chains and large train stations. The first wave sold the obvious: surgical masks, condoms, sanitary products, eye drops. The second wave added pregnancy and ovulation tests, then HIV self-test kits from manufacturers like OraSure, then COVID-19 antigen kits during the pandemic. By 2024 the same cabinets were quietly carrying urine dipsticks, fingerstick HbA1c kits, fecal occult blood tests, and at-home strep swabs.

A handful of companies dominate the supply. **BD CHECK-UP**, the consumer-diagnostics arm of Becton Dickinson Japan, supplies the urinalysis kits found in most pharmacy-adjacent machines. **NOW Diagnostics**, an American firm that entered Japan in 2021, supplies the fingerstick blood panels. The cabinets themselves are usually made by Japanese OEMs — **Sanwa Newtec**, **Fuji Electric Retail Systems**, and **Glory** — the same companies that build the train-station ticket machines, which is why the user interface feels eerily familiar.

The blood-pressure kiosks tucked into the entrance of every Matsumoto Kiyoshi belong to the same broader category. So do the coin-operated body composition scales (Tanita, Omron) at sentō bathhouses. The line between "vending machine" and "unstaffed clinic" has been blurry in Japan for years.

## What the urine machines actually test

The urine-analysis machines are mostly dressed-up dipstick readers. You pay, receive a sealed paper cup and a foil-wrapped strip, use the adjacent toilet, dip the strip, and slot it into a reader. Sixty seconds later a thermal printer issues a receipt with results.

The standard 5-to-10-parameter panel covers:

- **Glucose** — screening for diabetes
- **Protein** — early kidney dysfunction
- **Ketones** — uncontrolled diabetes or starvation states
- **Occult blood** — bladder, kidney, or urinary-tract issues
- **Leukocytes** — urinary tract infection
- **Nitrites** — bacterial infection
- **pH and specific gravity** — hydration and metabolic status
- **Bilirubin and urobilinogen** — liver function

Prices run from ¥500 for the basic strip to about ¥2,000 for the expanded panel with a printed reference range. None of this is diagnostic in a strict sense — a positive result is a prompt to see an actual doctor, and the machine says so on the screen, in Japanese and a slightly stilted English. The "tests your urine for cancer" framing comes from the bladder-cancer screening pilots that several wards ran in 2023, which used the same hardware to look for microscopic hematuria. The marketing stuck.

## Where to find them in Tokyo

The machines are not advertised. They tend to live in the same back corners as ATMs and lottery kiosks. A few reliably stocked locations:

- **Don Quijote Shinjuku Higashi-guchi**, on the 24-hour discount floor near the cosmetics aisle. Two units, one for urinalysis and one for fingerstick glucose.
- **BicCamera Yurakucha­n, 7F**, in the small-appliance health-care corner. This is the easiest one for tourists — English UI, takes IC cards.
- **Bic Drug Akihabara**, ground floor near the entrance. Stocks the BD CHECK-UP urinalysis kit and the COVID-flu combo antigen test.
- **Matsumoto Kiyoshi Shibuya Center-gai**, basement. Includes a blood-pressure kiosk and a Tanita body-composition scale that prints a receipt.
- **Shinjuku Station, New South Gate concourse**, opposite the Lumine entrance. Pregnancy, ovulation, and a urinalysis dipstick. Used heavily during morning rush.

None of these require a prescription, an ID, or a conversation with a clerk.

## The regulatory loophole nobody calls a loophole

This is legal in Japan because of a specific carve-out. The 2014 amendments to the **Pharmaceutical Affairs Act** (now formally the Act on Securing Quality, Efficacy and Safety of Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices, or PMD Act) reclassified a list of in-vitro diagnostic markers as suitable for over-the-counter, at-home use. Urinalysis dipsticks, ovulation tests, and certain self-collected blood panels moved from "general medical device" — pharmacist consultation required — to a lighter category that can be sold without a human intermediary. As long as the machine is owned by a licensed pharmacy entity and the kits carry approved labeling, an unattended cabinet is regulatorily indistinguishable from a shelf.

The contrast is sharp. In the United States, the FDA still treats most urine dipsticks as Class II devices that, in practice, are sold through pharmacies behind a counter. In the EU, the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR), in force since 2022, has made self-test approval slower and more expensive, not less. The Japanese model — light-touch approval plus the world's densest vending infrastructure — is genuinely unusual.

The cultural piece matters too. Buying a urine test from a machine carries none of the awkwardness it would in a Walgreens. There is no clerk to make eye contact with, no pharmacy queue, no insurance form. The same logic that put condoms and pregnancy tests in 1990s-era machines — privacy via automation — has scaled cleanly into diagnostics. Public-health surveys done by the National Institute of Public Health in 2024 found that users of self-care vending machines were disproportionately women in their 30s and men over 60, two groups that report the highest discomfort with face-to-face pharmacy interactions.

## Why the category is already shrinking

The machines are, by most measures, a transitional technology. The same diagnostic panel that costs ¥500 and a trip to a basement is also, increasingly, a passive readout on a wrist.

Continuous glucose monitors from Abbott (FreeStyle Libre) and Dexcom are now sold over the counter in Japanese drugstores. The Oura Ring and Whoop band quietly track resting heart rate, HRV, skin temperature, and respiratory rate, which together flag most of what a basic urinalysis was screening for. Apple is widely reported to be introducing non-invasive glucose sensing on a future Apple Watch — the timeline has slipped repeatedly, but the engineering teams in Cupertino and Suzhou have been working on it since at least 2017, and Japanese carriers are already positioning marketing for it.

JVMA membership data shows that the total vending-machine count peaked around 2000 and has fallen roughly 25% since. Self-care machines bucked the trend for a decade, growing through the pandemic, but installation orders flattened in 2025. The vending operators we spoke to for an earlier piece described diagnostics as a "five-year window" — long enough to recoup the cabinets, short enough that no one is building a long-term business on dipsticks.

The machines will not vanish quickly. They are convenient, cheap, and culturally invisible — which in Japan often means they outlive their replacement technology by a decade or two. But the strangeness of the artifact is the strangeness of a transitional object. A vending machine that tests your urine is what a country builds when the diagnostic instinct has arrived but the wearable hasn't quite caught up.

## TL;DR

Japan has roughly four million vending machines and a 2014 regulatory carve-out that lets a subset of in-vitro diagnostic tests be sold without a pharmacist. The result is a small but real category of urine-analysis machines — BD CHECK-UP, NOW Diagnostics, Sanwa Newtec cabinets — that read standard 5-to-10-parameter dipsticks for ¥500–¥2,000 in places like Don Quijote Shinjuku, BicCamera Yurakucho 7F, and Bic Drug Akihabara. The same machines also dispense HIV self-tests, pregnancy and ovulation tests, COVID antigen kits, and blood-pressure readings. It is normal, it is legal, and it is being slowly eaten by the Apple Watch.
