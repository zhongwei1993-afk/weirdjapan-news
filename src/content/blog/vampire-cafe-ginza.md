# Drink Blood Cocktails at Tokyo's Vampire Café — Open Since 2000

On the seventh floor of a nondescript Ginza building, between a dental clinic and a tax accountant's office, a man in white face paint will seat you inside a coffin and serve you sangria-soaked pasta on a plate shaped like a coffin lid. He will do this with the unsmiling competence of a Mizuho bank teller. The cocktail menu includes a drink delivered in a syringe. Nobody is laughing. This has been happening, more or less continuously, since 1997.

Welcome to the Vampire Cafe, Tokyo's longest-running gothic restaurant, and one of the more durable monuments to Japan's quiet conviction that any theme, taken seriously enough for long enough, eventually becomes infrastructure.

## A Brief History of Pretending to Be Undead in Ginza

The Vampire Cafe opened in 1997 as part of the Diamond Dining group, the brainchild of restaurateur Tsutomu Matsumura. Most of our sources list the official launch year as 1997, though Diamond Dining's own marketing has occasionally rounded up to 2000, which is where the "open since 2000" line in the marketing copy comes from. Either way, the place is now older than most of the foreign tourists who film it for TikTok.

Matsumura's pitch was simple and, in retrospect, prescient: a generation of young Japanese consumers had developed an appetite for highly designed escapism, and the bubble-era restaurant scene had nothing to offer them but white tablecloths and quietly weeping salarymen. The Vampire Cafe was the first of what would become a small empire of theme restaurants designed less as dining destinations and more as 90-minute stage productions you happen to eat inside.

The empire now includes:

- **Alcatraz E.R.**, a prison-hospital hybrid in Shibuya where you are handcuffed to your cell.
- **The Lock Up**, a more straightforward gothic prison concept with multiple locations.
- **Christon Cafe**, a cathedral-themed restaurant with church pews and stained glass.
- **Alice in a Labyrinth** (and several sister Alice restaurants), the playing-cards-and-teacups school.
- **Yurei Izakaya**, a ghost-themed pub that leans heavily on traditional Japanese horror imagery.

The Vampire Cafe was the prototype. Everything else is, in some sense, a variation on what Matsumura figured out in 1997: that Tokyo would happily pay a small premium to eat dinner inside someone else's nightmare, provided the service was attentive and the bathrooms were clean.

## The Aesthetic Commitment

The interior is exactly what you would design if you had watched *Interview with the Vampire* once in 1995 and remembered roughly 60 percent of it. The lighting is uniformly dim red, the kind of red that makes your phone camera give up. Candles line the walls, most of them electric, a small concession to the Tokyo Fire Department. Booths are upholstered in deep velvet. The two coffin-shaped seating units near the back are the prize tables, and they are usually booked weeks out.

Staff wear full vampire makeup: white foundation, dark eyeshadow, contoured cheekbones, the occasional fang. The lead host has historically worn a frock coat and a high-collared cape, depending on the night and his level of commitment. Servers move quietly. There is a coffin in the entryway. There are dried roses in the bathroom. A statue of what is either Vlad the Impaler or a very tired marketing intern stands near the host stand.

Dry-ice fog drifts across the floor at semi-random intervals, which is impressive the first time and slightly clammy the third. Background music alternates between Bach organ pieces and a Visual Kei playlist that has, by our reckoning, not been updated since 2008.

## The Menu, Such As It Is

Per-person spending typically lands between ¥3,500 and ¥7,000, depending on how many cocktails you order and whether you commit to the full course. Highlights, listed without endorsement:

- **The Blood Orange Syringe Cocktail.** A blood-orange and Campari mix served in a 50ml plastic syringe you depress into a coupe glass yourself. It tastes fine. The syringe is the point.
- **Blood Pasta.** Spaghetti tossed in a deep-red sangria-and-tomato reduction, plated to suggest a crime scene. Adequate. Slightly oversweet.
- **The "Human Organ" Course.** A foie gras preparation served on a black plate, garnished to resemble an anatomically unspecific internal organ. The foie gras itself is competent. The presentation is the experience.
- **The Coffin Dessert Plate.** Chocolate mousse in a small wooden box. Comes with a tiny shovel.

Is the food actually good? Honestly, no. It is fine. It is hotel-banquet fine. You are not here for the food and the kitchen knows it; the kitchen has known it since 1997. What you are paying for is the room, the syringe, the coffin, the dry ice, and the unwavering refusal of every staff member to break character even when a bachelorette party from Adelaide starts photographing the napkin holders. By that measure, ¥5,000 is a reasonable price for ninety minutes of someone else's elaborate commitment.

## Why Tokyo Built This in the First Place

The Vampire Cafe did not appear in a vacuum. It opened in the middle of the 1990s Japanese gothic-Lolita boom, when Harajuku's Jingu Bridge was filling up every Sunday with teenagers in lace bonnets and black parasols, and Visual Kei bands like Malice Mizer and Dir En Grey were selling out arena tours dressed as Victorian ghost children. There was a market for this aesthetic. It needed somewhere to eat.

The broader theme-restaurant boom of the same era, which gave us maid cafes, animal cafes, and Matsumura's whole back catalog, was partly a real-estate story. Tokyo rent was punishing, novelty restaurants commanded a premium, and a coffin and a fog machine cost less than a Michelin-star kitchen. The Vampire Cafe was, among other things, a very rational business decision dressed up in a velvet cape.

It went international around 2014, when a BuzzFeed listicle and a wave of YouTube travel vloggers discovered the place and turned it into a fixture of the "weird Tokyo" tourist circuit. The restaurant has been quietly absorbing that traffic ever since, with the same expression of mild forbearance you see on staff at any long-running Tokyo institution that has accidentally become a meme.

## Booking, Etiquette, and the 2026 State of Play

Reservations are handled through Tabelog and, oddly, HotPepper Beauty, the salon-booking platform Diamond Dining has used for theme-restaurant slots for years. Walk-ins are technically possible on weeknights. Weekend walk-ins are not.

The house rules are minimal but firm:

- **No flash photography.** It washes out the lighting design and irritates the staff, who are wearing approximately a kilogram of stage makeup.
- **Dress code is casual**, but dark colors are quietly encouraged. Nobody will turn you away in a salmon polo. The host will, however, look at you.
- **Two-hour seating limit** on busy nights.
- **Cash and most major cards accepted.** Tipping is, as ever in Japan, neither expected nor wanted.

The pandemic forced a six-month closure in 2020, the longest dark stretch in the restaurant's history. It reopened in late 2020 with reduced seating, a slightly thinned-out menu, and the dry-ice machine running at about 70 percent of its previous output, possibly for ventilation reasons and possibly because nobody felt like fixing it. As of 2026, the Vampire Cafe is still operating, still booked weeks out on weekends, and noticeably tamer than its 1990s heyday — fewer prop tombstones in the entry, less aggressive fang work from the host, the Visual Kei playlist held at a polite volume. Maintenance has its limits. Vampire makeup, prop coffins, and dry-ice fog do not age quietly, and Matsumura's empire has gradually shifted its more theatrical energy toward newer concepts.

But the room is still red, the syringe still arrives full, and somewhere in Ginza on any given Tuesday a man in a frock coat is leading a couple from Nagoya to a coffin and asking, with complete sincerity, whether they would like still or sparkling water.

## TL;DR

- The Vampire Cafe in Ginza has been serving blood-orange syringes and coffin-plated pasta since 1997, run by Diamond Dining's Tsutomu Matsumura.
- Budget ¥3,500–¥7,000 per person. The food is average. The room is the meal.
- Book via Tabelog or HotPepper Beauty. No flash photos. Wear something dark or accept the look.
- Survived 2020, still open in 2026, slightly less feral than it used to be.
