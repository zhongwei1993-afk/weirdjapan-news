# Why One Slice of A5 Wagyu Costs ¥10,000 — And How to Eat It Without Wrecking Your Stomach

One slice of A5 Kobe beef. Five seconds on the grill per side. Melts before you chew. Costs ¥4,000.

That is not a typo and it is not a tourist trap. That is the actual market rate for a single piece of correctly-graded, correctly-sourced Japanese wagyu at a respectable Tokyo restaurant in 2026. A full course — six or seven cuts, rice, soup, dessert — will run you ¥25,000 to ¥45,000 per person. The cattle that produced it lived longer than most pet rabbits and were fed on a more carefully calibrated diet than most Olympic athletes.

This is one of those things Japan does quietly — without fanfare, without irony, and without any apparent awareness that the rest of the world finds it remarkable. Here's how the system actually works, and how to spend your money on it without getting fleeced.

## The grading system, decoded

Every cow processed in Japan gets a two-part grade from the **Japan Meat Grading Association (JMGA)**. It looks like a letter plus a number — *A5*, *B4*, *C3* — and almost nobody outside Japan understands what those characters mean.

**The letter is yield grade.** A, B, or C. It refers to how much usable meat the carcass produced relative to its size. A is the best (more meat, less waste). C is the worst. This is a butcher's metric. It tells you almost nothing about how the meat tastes.

**The number is quality grade.** 1 to 5, scored on four sub-criteria:

- **Marbling** (the intramuscular fat, called *sashi*)
- **Meat color and brightness**
- **Firmness and texture**
- **Fat color and quality**

The lowest of the four sub-scores becomes the overall number. So A5 means: top yield, and every single quality criterion scored 5. A4 means at least one criterion dropped — often marbling.

**Then there's BMS.** The Beef Marbling Standard runs from 1 to 12, and it's the metric obsessives actually care about. A5 only requires BMS 8 or higher. But there's a big difference between a BMS 8 and a BMS 12. The truly absurd, fat-laced, snowflake-pattern wagyu you've seen on Instagram is usually BMS 11 or 12. That extra marbling is what bumps a slice from ¥2,000 to ¥4,000.

For context: a USDA Prime ribeye — the top US grade, the stuff that costs $80 at a Manhattan steakhouse — would score somewhere around **BMS 4 or 5** if it were graded on the Japanese scale. American "marbled" beef is, by Japanese standards, lean.

## The five brand wagyu hierarchy

"Wagyu" (和牛) literally just means "Japanese cow." Within Japan there are roughly 200 regional brands, each with its own breeding registry, feeding rules, and prefectural pride. Five sit at the top, often called the **gozanke** — the great houses.

- **Kobe (神戸)**. Hyogo prefecture. The most famous internationally, the most counterfeited abroad. Only Tajima-strain Japanese Black cattle, born, raised, and slaughtered in Hyogo, scoring A4 or A5 with BMS 6+, qualify. Around 5,000 cows per year meet the standard. That's the total global supply.
- **Matsusaka (松阪)**. Mie prefecture. Female-only virgin cows, fed for an unusually long 900-day cycle. Often considered the connoisseur's pick. Smaller annual production than Kobe.
- **Omi (近江)**. Shiga prefecture. The oldest brand wagyu — written records date back roughly 400 years to when samurai allegedly smuggled it as "medicine" during Japan's Buddhist beef ban.
- **Yonezawa (米沢)**. Yamagata prefecture. Cold mountain climate, slow-grown cattle, deeper red color than the other four.
- **Sendai (仙台)**. Miyagi prefecture. The most accessible of the five in terms of price. Often appears in high-end Tokyo yakiniku because the supply is comparatively stable.

Below the gozanke sit dozens of excellent regional brands — **Hida** (Gifu), **Saga**, **Miyazaki**, **Iwate Maesawa** — which are often just as good and considerably cheaper. The brand name is partly about quality and largely about marketing.

## Why it costs ¥10,000 a slice

Real wagyu is expensive for boring, structural reasons.

**The feeding cycle is 28 to 32 months.** Most commercial beef cattle in the US are slaughtered at 14 to 18 months. Japanese wagyu spends roughly twice as long eating, which means twice the feed bill, twice the barn space, twice the labor.

**The feed itself is expensive.** A precisely calibrated mix of rice straw, corn, barley, wheat bran, and soybean meal — much of it imported. Feed alone can run ¥500,000 per animal across its lifetime.

**Beer and sake feeding is mostly myth, with a kernel of truth.** Most wagyu farmers do not pour Asahi into the trough. The story comes from a small number of Hyogo and Tottori farms that historically gave cattle a little beer in summer to stimulate appetite during heat-induced loss of hunger. Sake-lees (the rice mash byproduct of brewing) is sometimes added to feed because it's a useful local protein source. Massage and music are also marketing exaggerations of real practices used by a small number of farms to reduce stress in confined animals. None of it is the reason the meat tastes the way it does.

**Genetics do the heavy lifting.** Japanese Black cattle (*Kuroge Washu*), which produce roughly 90 percent of all wagyu, carry a genetic predisposition to intramuscular fat deposition that no other cattle breed on earth comes close to. The bloodlines are tracked obsessively. Frozen semen from elite bulls sells at auction for millions of yen.

**Slaughter is volume-limited.** Kobe certifies around 5,000 head per year. Matsusaka, fewer. Multiply that against global demand — including a Chinese market that lifted its 20-year wagyu ban in 2023 — and the math gets ugly fast.

## Where to actually eat it in Tokyo without getting scammed

Tokyo has thousands of restaurants advertising "wagyu." Maybe two hundred serve real, top-grade, brand-certified beef. Here are five spots that consistently deliver.

- **Wagyumafia (Nishi-Azabu and Hamamatsucho)**. Hideki Hamada's members-leaning operation, famous for the ¥20,000 Kobe katsu sandwich. Touristy, theatrical, but the meat is genuinely elite. Book weeks ahead.
- **Han no Daidokoro (several locations, flagship in Roppongi)**. Yakiniku chain that buys whole cows at auction and butchers in-house. Excellent value relative to the quality. Easier to book than the others.
- **Yakiniku Jumbo Shirokane (Shirokane)**. The course-only counter omakase. A small team of chefs grills each cut for you with the cut, grade, and prefecture announced like wine. Reservation-only, books out two months in advance.
- **Sumibi Yakiniku Nakahara (Shinbashi)**. Run by a former wholesale buyer who sources directly. Reasonably priced for the quality. The kind of place Tokyo food critics actually eat at on their own time.
- **Yoroniku (Ebisu and Omotesando)**. Yakiniku with a more contemporary, design-forward feel. Strong on rarer cuts. Popular with the fashion crowd; easier walk-in than the others on a weeknight.

If a tourist-area restaurant advertises "Kobe beef" on a chalkboard outside without specifying a grade, prefecture, or certification number, assume it's regional wagyu at best and Australian crossbreed at worst.

## Cuts to know

Wagyu menus in Japan list cuts in Japanese with no translation. Five to recognize.

- **Ribeye / リブロース (*riburosu*)**. The classic marbled slice. Most photogenic. Most expensive.
- **Sirloin / サーロイン (*saroin*)**. Slightly leaner than ribeye, deeper flavor. The "steak" cut.
- **Chateaubriand / シャトーブリアン (*shatoburian*)**. The center of the tenderloin. Buttery, less fatty than ribeye. Premium-priced for its rarity per animal.
- **Hanging tender / ハラミ (*harami*)**. Skirt steak from the diaphragm. Cheaper, beefier, often the cut chefs eat themselves. Underrated.
- **Zabuton / ザブトン**. Literally "cushion." The chuck flap — a small marbled cut from the shoulder. Buttery, rich, only one small piece per cow. Increasingly trendy.

## How to eat A5 without getting sick

Here is the part nobody tells first-timers.

A5 wagyu is roughly **50 percent fat by weight**. A 300-gram steak — which is a perfectly normal serving size for American ribeye — is the equivalent of eating 150 grams of pure beef fat in one sitting. Your gallbladder will not be amused. Several Tokyo doctors have a quiet term for the foreign tourist who arrives at a clinic the next morning with stomach pain: *wagyu shock*.

The rule, used by virtually everyone who eats this regularly:

- **Cap your A5 portion at 100 grams.** That's roughly four to six slices in a yakiniku course.
- **Eat it across the meal, not all at once.** Two slices to start, two in the middle, two at the end.
- **Contrast every bite with rice, pickled vegetables, or grilled scallions.** This is what *shari* (vinegared rice) and *tsukemono* are doing on the table — not decoration, ballast.
- **Drink something with acid or bubbles.** Highballs, dry sake, sparkling water. Avoid heavy red wine; it doubles the fat load.
- **If you want a full steak, get A4 instead of A5.** Less marbling, more meat, vastly more digestible at scale.

A 300-gram A5 ribeye is not a flex. It is a medical event with garnish. The Japanese eat wagyu the way the French eat foie gras: small, paired, infrequent.

## TL;DR

> Real Japanese A5 wagyu is graded on a precise system most foreigners don't understand, comes from five protected regional brands, and costs what it costs because the cattle live twice as long as US beef cattle and the supply is genetically and geographically capped. Eat it in slices, not steaks, or your gallbladder will file a complaint.
