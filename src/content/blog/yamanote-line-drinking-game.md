# Tokyo's Drinking Game Played on a Real Train Line

There is a tradition in Tokyo where a small group of people get on the Yamanote Line, open a beer at the first station, and try to finish the loop alive. It is called **Yamanote-sen ichi-shu** (山手線一周), literally "Yamanote loop one round," and it has been quietly destroying livers since the 1980s. Nobody invented it. Nobody promotes it. JR East would very much like you to stop. It happens anyway, mostly on Friday nights, mostly involving men in untucked dress shirts holding warming cans of Asahi Super Dry.

## The Loop, In Numbers

The Yamanote Line is the green circle on every Tokyo subway map — the one that connects Shinjuku, Shibuya, Tokyo Station, Ueno, and twenty-six other stops into a single oval around central Tokyo. It runs **30 stations**, covers **34.5 kilometres**, and takes about **60 minutes** to complete a full loop. During peak hours, **8 trains an hour** pass through each station in each direction, which means you are almost never waiting and almost never alone.

For most commuters, this is just transit. For a specific subset of salarymen, university students, and visiting foreigners with poor impulse control, it is a track. A circuit. A timed challenge with a built-in finish line that returns you to where you started, which is convenient, because by then you cannot read the signs.

The rules, as they are passed down in izakaya backrooms and on Reddit threads, vary slightly. But the core idea is unchanged: one drink per station, all the way around.

## The Math Nobody Wants to Do

Here is where the tradition runs into chemistry.

The classic version of Yamanote-sen ichi-shu requires you to finish a drink between each pair of stations — so 29 drinks for a full 30-station loop. If those drinks are standard 350ml cans of Japanese beer at 5% ABV, you are consuming roughly **10 litres of beer and about 400 grams of pure ethanol** in 60 minutes.

The lethal dose of alcohol (LD50) for a healthy adult sits somewhere around 5 to 8 grams per kilogram of body weight. For a 70kg man, that's about 350–560g. The classic ruleset, executed literally, is a suicide attempt scheduled by timetable.

This is why nobody actually plays the classic version. What actually happens, in practice, is one of two things:

- **The chuhai version**: a single 100ml shot of canned chuhai per station. 29 stops × 100ml = 2.9 litres of 7% ABV liquid, or roughly 160g of ethanol split across a group of four to six people. Survivable. Stupid, but survivable.
- **The category version**: at each station, the next player must name something from an agreed-upon category — train lines, AKB48 members, types of ramen, prime ministers. Miss your turn or repeat an answer, you drink. The drink is small. The shame is large.

Both versions end the same way: at Shinjuku, badly.

## Where It Came From

The tradition is informal, undocumented, and almost certainly older than anyone admits. The earliest references most Tokyo drinkers will give you put it in the **mid-1980s**, during the bubble years, when salarymen had expense accounts that needed emptying and university circles had reputations that needed cementing.

It fits cleanly into the broader **nomikai** culture — the after-work drinking session that is technically optional and functionally mandatory in many Japanese companies. December's **bounenkai** ("forget-the-year parties") have historically been the peak season, with corporate teams allegedly using the loop as a bonding exercise. There is no statistical evidence for this. There are, however, a great many photographs.

The Yamanote was chosen for boring, practical reasons: it loops, so you cannot get lost; it is frequent, so you cannot strand yourself; and it passes through enough major nightlife districts (Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro, Ueno) that you can bail at any point and find a karaoke booth, a ramen counter, or a pharmacy.

## The Legal Bit

Drinking on Japanese trains is, technically, legal. You can buy a beer at any kiosk inside any JR station, walk onto any train, and crack it open. Nobody will stop you. Nobody will comment. The shinkansen even sells beer from a cart.

What is **not** legal — or at least, not tolerated — is being disruptive. JR East's published conduct rules prohibit loud conversation, blocking doors, harassing other passengers, and creating "**meiwaku**" (迷惑), the catch-all Japanese term for "behaviour that is a nuisance to others." Get drunk quietly in a corner with your friends: fine. Sing the Pokémon theme song at Ebisu station: you are getting walked off the platform by station staff with the polite firmness of a Michelin maître d' removing a shoeless diner.

In **2023**, JR East updated its priority-car policy to explicitly discourage open containers in the designated women-and-elderly carriages, following a string of complaints. The new signage is small, polite, and entirely ignored by people who were already going to ignore it.

## The Sister Traditions and the Copycats

Yamanote-sen ichi-shu has spawned variants. The most famous is the **Yamanote Line Karaoke Game**, in which players must sing the chorus of a different song at every other station, the rest of the carriage either pretending not to notice or quietly filming for TikTok. There is also the **Tozai Line attempt** (longer, more boring, fewer transfers) and the **Den-en-toshi Line attempt** (suburban, depressing, ends in Kanagawa).

International coverage has been sporadic but enthusiastic. The **BBC ran a piece in 2008** following a group of British expats on a full loop, which mostly served as cautionary footage. **Chris Broad's *Abroad in Japan* YouTube channel** documented an attempt in 2019 that ended, predictably, with most of the participants asleep on a bench at Yoyogi. Several smaller YouTubers have since attempted "responsible" versions involving non-alcoholic beer, which defeats the purpose so thoroughly that the videos rarely break 50,000 views.

## How to Do It Without Dying

If you are determined to attempt this — and we are not encouraging you, merely acknowledging that you will — there is a known harm-reduction protocol passed around among long-term Tokyo expats:

- **Eat first.** A full meal. Then carry **onigiri and takoyaki** in your bag as a stomach buffer between stations.
- **Use small drinks.** 100ml canned chuhai or 135ml mini-cans of beer. Not full 500ml tallboys. The tradition is about the loop, not the volume.
- **Stay in a group of four or more.** One sober-ish person at all times. Rotate the designation.
- **Do the foreigner-friendly version**: five stops, then bail at a major station for a sit-down dinner. You get the bit, you keep your dignity, you do not vomit in front of a salaryman who is trying to read his Kindle.
- **No shouting. No spilling. No missed transfers.** These are the three landmines. Violate any of them and someone in your carriage will photograph you, and that photograph will end up on a Japanese-language Twitter account dedicated to badly-behaved foreigners.
- **End at a karaoke bar.** This is traditional. The loop closes at Shinjuku or Shibuya, you stumble into a Big Echo, you sing one song badly, and you go home. This is the ritual finish line.

The Tokyo Metropolitan Police publish annual statistics on public-intoxication incidents, and while they do not break out "Yamanote game" as a category, the numbers spike reliably in **December and April** — bounenkai season and the new-fiscal-year welcome parties. Make of that what you will.

## TL;DR

A loose tradition called Yamanote-sen ichi-shu involves drinking your way around all 30 stops of Tokyo's Yamanote loop in roughly 60 minutes. The literal classic-rules version is a medical emergency; the actual common version is small chuhai cans, large groups, and a karaoke finish. It is legal. It is frowned upon. It is, like most things in Japan, technically fine as long as nobody around you notices.
