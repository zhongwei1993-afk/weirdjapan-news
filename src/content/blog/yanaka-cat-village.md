# Yanaka: Tokyo's Cat Village Where the Felines Run the Alleys

Twenty minutes north of Tokyo Station, in a sliver of Taito-ku that the twentieth century mostly forgot to demolish, a tortoiseshell cat is asleep on a stack of tofu crates. Nobody is doing anything about this. Nobody has done anything about this for roughly a hundred years.

Welcome to Yanaka (谷中), the old-town district where the buildings are wooden, the croquettes are cat-shaped, and the unofficial mayors all have four legs.

## The Old-Town Tokyo That Refused to Burn

Most of central Tokyo is, architecturally speaking, a 1950s reconstruction project. The Great Kanto Earthquake of 1923 flattened roughly half the city. The 1945 firebombing campaign finished the job, with the night of March 9th alone killing an estimated 100,000 people and leveling sixteen square miles of timber housing.

Yanaka, somehow, survived both.

The neighborhood sits on a small plateau in Taito-ku, hemmed in by Yanaka Cemetery on one side and a network of temple grounds on the other. The fires didn't catch in the same way. The cemetery acted as a firebreak in 1945. In 1923, the area was far enough from the epicenter and dense enough with stone temple walls that most of the wooden machiya houses stayed standing.

The result is a kind of accidental museum: low wooden buildings, narrow lanes designed for handcarts rather than cars, tiny corner shrines, and the kind of Showa-era shopfronts that everywhere else in Tokyo had to be reconstructed from photographs. The cats are part of this. They have been part of this for generations, and nobody is quite sure when the tradition started, because the tradition predates anyone who could be asked.

## The Cat Census

Local volunteers, who keep informal counts on small whiteboards stuck inside shop windows, estimate that more than 100 free-roaming cats live in the Yanaka area at any given time. The number fluctuates. Some are stray, some are semi-domesticated, and a surprising number have a kind of distributed ownership: fed by one tofu shop in the morning, by a sake brewery at noon, by an elderly woman with a flat near the cemetery in the evening.

The cats are not, strictly speaking, anyone's responsibility. They are also not anyone's problem. Tokyo's TNR (trap-neuter-return) volunteers operate quietly in the area, and the local merchants' association has, for at least two decades, treated the cat population as a feature of the district rather than a public-health debate.

This is unusual in a country where most large cities lean toward removing strays. Yanaka simply doesn't. The cats stay. The cats are listed in tourist pamphlets. The cats have, in several cases, their own Instagram accounts maintained by neighbors.

## Yanaka Ginza and the Cat-Shaped Everything

The spine of the neighborhood is Yanaka Ginza, a 170-metre shopping street that runs east from Nippori Station and ends in a small staircase called Yuyake Dandan ("Sunset Stairs"), so named because the western view at dusk is, on a good day, embarrassingly nice. The cats sit on the stairs. Tourists photograph the cats. The cats are unimpressed.

The shops along Yanaka Ginza have, over the past two decades, leaned hard into the cat motif without ever quite tipping into theme-park territory. A short and incomplete inventory:

- **Niku-no-Suzuki**, a butcher's shop that has, for years, sold a deep-fried minced-beef croquette shaped like a cat's head. ¥250 a piece, hot from the fryer, eaten while standing. The ears are sometimes lopsided. The locals call them "neko menchi."
- **Yanaka Bidoricho**, a small workshop selling hand-carved cat masks and figurines by the artisan **Niwa Honyo**, whose work ranges from palm-sized clay charms to elaborate full-face theatrical pieces. Prices climb steeply once you get past the souvenir tier.
- **Shiba Inu**, a stationery and gift shop named, confusingly, after a dog, but stocked almost entirely with cat illustrations on postcards, tote bags, and tea towels.
- **Cat Sweets Cafe Necochan**, open since 2013, which sells matcha rolls iced to look like sleeping cats and a black-sesame cheesecake with a cat-paw imprint pressed into the top.

A short walk away, on the gentle slope of **Sansaki-zaka**, two small woodblock-print shops sell ukiyo-e reproductions and original works on a cat theme — including reprints of Utagawa Kuniyoshi's nineteenth-century cat caricatures, which remain, by general consensus, the funniest things any pre-Meiji artist ever drew.

And on a side lane near the cemetery, **Sanyado**, a family-run tofu shop in business since 1923, keeps a resident cat that has, for at least three generations, slept on the same wooden counter while the morning's tofu sets in cold water beside it. The current cat is, the owner clarifies, not the original cat.

## The Yanesen Triangle and a Cemetery Full of Shoguns

Yanaka is the "ya" in **Yanesen**, the locally-used shorthand for the triangle formed by Yanaka, **Nezu**, and **Sendagi** — three adjoining neighborhoods that, taken together, form the best-preserved stretch of pre-war Tokyo still inhabited as a normal residential district. Nezu Shrine, with its tunnel of vermilion torii gates, sits at the southern point. Sendagi, to the west, is where Natsume Soseki and several other Meiji-era novelists lived and wrote. Yanaka, to the north, is where the cats are.

Anchoring the whole thing is **Yanaka Reien** (谷中霊園), Yanaka Cemetery: roughly 25 acres, around 100,000 graves, and a central avenue of cherry trees that locals refer to as Sakura-dori. Among the residents:

- **Tokugawa Yoshinobu**, the fifteenth and final shogun, who handed power back to the emperor in 1867 and then lived another fifty years, mostly photographing things and cycling.
- **Yokoyama Taikan**, the painter who more or less invented modern Japanese-style painting (nihonga) in the early twentieth century.
- A long list of Meiji statesmen, kabuki actors, and at least one notorious bank robber.

The cemetery is also, predictably, full of cats. They sleep on the older gravestones, which retain heat longer than the polished modern ones. The caretakers do not chase them off. The visitors do not seem to mind.

## How to Actually Visit

Yanaka is one of the few Tokyo districts that genuinely rewards aimlessness, but if you want a plan, here is the cheap one:

- **Get there**: Take the JR Yamanote Line to **Nippori Station** (west exit) or **Nishi-Nippori Station**. From Nippori, Yanaka Ginza is a five-minute walk. From Nishi-Nippori, you enter through the temple lanes.
- **Loop**: A 90-minute walking loop covers Yanaka Ginza, Sansaki-zaka, the cemetery's central avenue, and the back lanes toward Nezu Shrine. Add another hour if you stop for croquettes, coffee, or to negotiate with a cat.
- **Time of day**: Late afternoon. The cats are more active, the light on Yuyake Dandan is the entire point of the stairs' name, and the shops close around 18:00, so finish browsing first.
- **Cash**: Bring it. Many of the older shops still don't take cards, and the smaller ones don't take IC either.
- **Cost**: A full afternoon, including a croquette, coffee, and a small ceramic cat, runs under ¥3,000.

## Cats, Yokai, and the Gentrification Problem

The cultural backdrop is older and stranger than the souvenir shops suggest. About two kilometers southeast, in Asakusa, sits **Imado Shrine**, generally credited as the birthplace of the **maneki-neko** — the beckoning-cat figurine that now sits in the window of every Chinese restaurant on earth. The shrine's nineteenth-century origin story involves a poor old woman, a dream, and a clay cat that brought back her fortunes. Yanaka's relationship with cats is part of the same broader cultural soil: cats as lucky, cats as quietly sacred, cats as worth feeding even when they don't, technically, belong to you.

Folklore goes further. The **bakeneko** is a yokai — a supernatural being — in which a household cat that lives long enough (the traditional cutoff is around thirteen years) transforms into a shape-shifting spirit capable of walking on its hind legs, speaking, and, in some versions, eating its owner. Edo-period prints are full of them. Several of the woodblock shops on Sansaki-zaka sell reproductions. The locals, asked about this, generally shrug.

For scale, it is worth comparing Yanaka to Japan's actual **Cat Islands** — **Tashirojima** in Miyagi and **Aoshima** in Ehime — where the cat-to-human ratio reaches roughly six to one and the entire local economy is, at this point, the cats. Yanaka is not that. Yanaka is a working residential district that happens to have a lot of cats. The distinction matters, because the cats here are not a tourist attraction so much as a feature of the housing stock.

Which brings us to the part nobody on Yanaka Ginza wants to talk about. Rents in the Yanesen triangle have risen roughly 30% since 2015. Several long-standing tofu makers, sake shops, and printers have closed in the past five years. The replacements tend to be cat-themed cafes aimed at weekend tourists, which is, on its face, a continuation of the local character, but is, in practice, a slow displacement of the people and the cats that made the local character a thing in the first place. As of 2026, the neighborhood remains cat-friendly, the merchants' association is still counting, and Sanyado is still making tofu. None of these things are guaranteed for the next decade.

For now, the tortoiseshell on the tofu crates is still asleep. Nobody is doing anything about it. That, more than the croquettes, is the point.

## TL;DR

- **Yanaka** is an old-town district in Taito-ku, Tokyo, that survived both the 1923 earthquake and the 1945 firebombing largely intact.
- Roughly **100+ free-roaming cats** live in the neighborhood, fed and informally cared for by local shops.
- **Yanaka Ginza** is the spine: ¥250 cat-shaped croquettes at Niku-no-Suzuki, hand-carved cat masks at Yanaka Bidoricho, cat illustrations at Shiba Inu, sweets at Necochan (since 2013).
- The **Yanesen triangle** (Yanaka–Nezu–Sendagi) is the best-preserved stretch of pre-war Tokyo.
- **Yanaka Cemetery** holds ~100,000 graves, including the last shogun Tokugawa Yoshinobu and painter Yokoyama Taikan — and many cats.
- Take JR to **Nippori** or **Nishi-Nippori**; a 90-minute loop covers everything.
- Cultural roots: **Imado Shrine's maneki-neko** and **bakeneko** folklore. Not to be confused with the **Tashirojima/Aoshima** cat islands.
- Watch the gentrification: rents up ~30% since 2015. Visit while it still feels like a neighborhood.
